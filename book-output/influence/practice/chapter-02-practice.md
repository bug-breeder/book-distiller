# Practice Exercises: Chapter 2 — Reciprocation: The Old Give and Take . . . and Take

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why does the reciprocity rule produce compliance even when the person doing the asking is someone you actively dislike? What does this tell us about the rule's relationship to other social forces like liking?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Cialdini's account of the Regan experiment shows that subjects who had received a Coke from Joe bought just as many raffle tickets from him whether they liked him or not. The reciprocity rule was powerful enough to completely override the normally strong effect of liking on compliance. This tells us that the rule operates at a more fundamental level than conscious preferences — it is a deeply conditioned sense of obligation that functions almost automatically, independent of whether the person triggering it is attractive, friendly, or welcome. This is why the Hare Krishna Society could extract donations even from people who found the devotees strange or off-putting: the gift of a flower engaged a rule that swamped the targets' personal feelings.

</details>

---

**Q2:** What is the distinction between the obligation to repay and the obligation to receive, and why is the latter the more exploitable of the two?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The obligation to repay is the core of the reciprocity rule — once you have received something, social norms demand you give something back. The obligation to receive is the lesser-discussed flip side: social pressure also makes it difficult to refuse an offered gift in the first place. The obligation to receive is more exploitable because it removes the target's ability to choose who they become indebted to. Once a gift has been pressed into your hands — like the Hare Krishna flower — declining it feels rude or socially awkward, so most people accept. At that point they have been involuntarily recruited into an indebtedness they never sought, and the requester can then invoke the obligation to repay. The victim never got a clean choice; the exploiter selected both the form of the initial favor and the form of the expected return.

</details>

---

**Q3:** Cialdini describes how the reciprocity rule can trigger unfair exchanges — where a small favor generates a much larger return favor. What are the two psychological forces that drive people to over-repay?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The first force is internal discomfort: being in a state of indebtedness feels psychologically aversive. Humans are conditioned from childhood to feel a kind of emotional chafing under obligation, and this discomfort motivates them to discharge the debt quickly — sometimes with more than is strictly required — just to restore psychological equilibrium. The second force is external shame: people who fail to repay are labeled with loaded, negative terms like "moocher," "ingrate," or "welsher." To avoid being seen as one of these socially despised types, people will accept an unequal exchange rather than bear the social stigma. Together, these two forces — internal guilt and external social judgment — create a pressure that can cause someone to hand over a car (as in the student's story) in return for a jump-start received a month earlier.

</details>

---

**Q4:** How does the rejection-then-retreat technique work, and why does it produce not just compliance but also follow-through and willingness to agree to future requests?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The technique works by first making a large request that will almost certainly be refused, then retreating to a smaller request — the one actually desired. The retreat is perceived by the target as a concession, triggering the reciprocity rule: since the requester gave up something (the bigger ask), the target feels obligated to give up something too, which means complying with the reduced request. What makes the technique especially potent is that it also engages perceptual contrast — the second request looks smaller than it would have if asked in isolation. The deeper surprise is that targets who comply via this method show higher follow-through rates (85% vs. 50% in the Canadian study) and are more willing to agree to future requests. This happens because the requester's concession creates feelings of personal responsibility in the target (they believe they influenced the outcome) and satisfaction with the arrangement — both of which make people more likely to honor and repeat the agreement.

</details>

---

**Q5:** What is Cialdini's recommended strategy for defending against reciprocation-based influence, and why is blanket refusal of all gifts a poor defense?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Cialdini recommends a mental redefinition strategy rather than blanket rejection. When you receive an initial favor or concession, accept it — but categorize it accurately. If it turns out to be a genuine act of goodwill, reciprocating is appropriate and socially healthy. If it reveals itself to be a calculated compliance device — a sales tactic dressed as a gift — mentally reclassify it as such. The reciprocity rule applies to favors, not tricks; once you define the "gift" as a sales device, the rule no longer compels a return. Blanket refusal is a poor defense because it is impossible to know at the time of receipt whether an offer is genuine, and reflexively rejecting all gifts insults sincere benefactors, creates social friction, and could lead to isolation. The child handing out flowers at the school open house illustrates the collateral damage of this approach. The smarter defense preserves the healthy social function of reciprocity while neutralizing its weaponized misuse.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Conference Lunch**
You attend a two-day industry conference. A vendor you have never met pays for your lunch both days, asks thoughtful questions about your work, and at the end of day two asks for a 30-minute meeting with your VP to pitch their product — bypassing your company's normal procurement process. You do not actually need their product.

*What should you consider?*
- Distinguish between genuine generosity and a calculated debt-creation strategy: were these lunches a real gesture of goodwill or a deliberate compliance investment?
- Recognize that accepting the lunches has generated a felt obligation, but that the obligation to repay a favor does not require you to grant an inappropriate request that bypasses your organization's rules.
- Notice the escalating nature of the request — small favors (lunches) followed by a disproportionate return ask (executive access) — as a hallmark of unfair-exchange exploitation.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The pattern here maps directly onto what Cialdini describes as exploiting uninvited favors to trigger obligations. The vendor chose the form of the "favor" (paying for lunches) and is now choosing the form of the return (executive access), which is a structural sign of manipulation rather than genuine gift-giving. The correct response is to apply Cialdini's redefinition strategy: mentally reclassify the lunches as sales investments, not personal generosity. Doing so frees you from the pull of the reciprocity rule. You can thank the vendor warmly and explain that the right path to your VP is through your procurement process — which you can facilitate through proper channels if there is genuine interest. You are not obligated to grant an inappropriate favor simply because someone bought you lunch; you would only be obligated if the lunches had been genuine gifts. The reciprocity rule governs favors, not sales tactics.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Negotiation Anchor**
You are negotiating salary for a new job. The hiring manager opens with an offer of $78,000. After you express hesitation, she says: "I went to bat for you internally — the original budget was $72,000. I'm already giving you $6,000 more than I was supposed to." You had researched the market and know $88,000 is fair.

*What should you consider?*
- Recognize the rejection-then-retreat structure: the "original budget" of $72,000 may have been a deliberately extreme anchor designed to make $78,000 feel like a generous concession.
- Be alert to how the contrast principle makes $78,000 appear more reasonable when framed against $72,000, even though your research says $88,000 is appropriate.
- Separate the emotional pull of "she went to bat for me" (liking and reciprocity combined) from the objective question of what the job is worth.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The manager has executed a textbook rejection-then-retreat sequence. Whether the $72,000 figure was ever real or was created specifically as a losing anchor, its effect is the same: by retreating to $78,000, the manager has framed this as a concession you should reciprocate. Cialdini's research shows that people feel more satisfied with and responsible for outcomes reached through apparent concessions — which is precisely the feeling the manager is engineering. The defense is to anchor the negotiation on external data rather than on the internal delta between the two figures she offered. You might say: "I appreciate you working to increase the offer. Based on market data for this role and my background, I was expecting something closer to $88,000 — can we talk about what would make that possible?" This reframes the discussion around fair market value rather than around the reciprocal dynamics she has set up. The key insight: a concession from an arbitrary starting point is not the same as a fair offer.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Unsolicited Upgrade**
A contractor renovating your kitchen "throws in" upgraded cabinet hardware at no charge, explaining that he had leftovers from another job. When he submits the final invoice, it is $3,200 over the agreed estimate. He says the overrun was due to "unexpected complexity," and adds: "Remember, I did give you those hardware upgrades." You feel vaguely obligated but also manipulated.

*What should you consider?*
- Assess whether the hardware upgrade was genuinely spontaneous generosity or a premeditated compliance investment designed to soften your resistance to overcharges.
- Distinguish between the legitimate obligation created by a genuine gift and the absence of any obligation created by a calculated sales device.
- Notice that the contractor is explicitly invoking the gift to justify an unequal exchange — a small gift (hardware) for a large return favor ($3,200 overrun acceptance).

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The contractor's explicit reference to the hardware upgrade when disputing the overcharge reveals that he views the upgrade as a credit against your resistance — which retroactively reclassifies it as a compliance device rather than a gift. Cialdini's framework is clear: once you identify that an apparent gift was strategically deployed to create a future obligation, the reciprocity rule no longer applies. You do not owe compliance to a trick. The appropriate response is to politely separate the two matters: thank him for the hardware, then address the overcharge on its own merits — asking for a line-item breakdown of what caused it, comparing it against the contract language about scope changes, and negotiating based on that documentation. The hardware gift is simply not a relevant factor in a commercial contract dispute. Allowing it to remain emotionally relevant is exactly the outcome the contractor was engineering.

</details>

## ✍️ Reflection Prompts

1. Think of a time when someone gave you an unsolicited gift, favor, or compliment just before making a request of you. Looking back now with an understanding of reciprocation, do you believe the gift was genuine or a compliance device — and how did it affect your decision? What would you do differently if you encountered the same situation today?

2. Think of a time when you made a large initial request or opened a negotiation with an extreme position, then retreated to a smaller ask. Did the other person comply more readily? What did you attribute that success to at the time, and how does the rejection-then-retreat framework change your understanding of what actually happened?

3. Think of a time when you felt the uncomfortable psychological weight of an unpaid obligation — money owed, a favor unreturned, a gift unacknowledged. What did that feeling cause you to do, and in hindsight, did the action you took to relieve the discomfort serve your actual interests or primarily the interests of the person you owed? What does this tell you about who really controls your behavior when the reciprocity rule is triggered?

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the rejection-then-retreat technique in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

When someone wants you to agree to something, they can dramatically increase your compliance by first asking for something much bigger — something they fully expect you to refuse. After you say no to the large request, they back down to the smaller thing they actually wanted all along, and because it feels like they made a sacrifice for you, a deep social instinct kicks in that makes you want to give something back. This means you end up saying yes to the real request not because it is reasonable on its own terms, but because the sequence of asking was engineered to make your "yes" feel like a fair exchange rather than a concession you were maneuvered into.

</details>

## 📋 Action Items

1. Before your next significant negotiation or purchasing decision, write down your BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement) and the market-rate figure for what you are negotiating — so that when a seller uses rejection-then-retreat and names a high anchor followed by a "concession," you have an objective reference point already on paper to compare against, rather than evaluating the offer relative to the anchor they set.

2. This week, when someone gives you a gift, favor, or upgrade that arrives just before a request, pause for 10 seconds and ask yourself one question privately: "Did they choose the form of this gift and the form of the return they want?" If yes, reclassify it as a sales device in your mind and notice whether the felt obligation dissolves — then make your decision about their request based purely on its merits.

3. Audit one recurring situation in your work or personal life where you routinely feel obligated to say yes (a colleague who always seems to do you small favors before asking for your time, a vendor who sends holiday gifts before contract renewal, a relative whose generosity always precedes a large ask) and decide in advance, before the next ask arrives, exactly what you are and are not willing to give in return — so the decision is made rationally rather than in the emotional heat of a felt obligation.
