# Practice Exercises: Chapter 2 — Reciprocation: The Old Give and Take . . . and Take

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why does the reciprocity rule still produce a sense of obligation even when the initial gift was uninvited or unwanted — and why is this feature specifically valuable to those who want to exploit it?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The reciprocity rule carries an obligation to receive as well as to repay. Because social norms make it awkward and impolite to reject a gift outright, most people accept the initial offering even when they don't want it — and acceptance triggers the obligation to reciprocate. This removes the target's ability to choose whether to enter the exchange at all, handing that power entirely to the giver. Exploiters value this feature because it allows them to manufacture indebtedness unilaterally: the target never consented to the relationship yet still feels compelled to comply with a subsequent request.

</details>

---

**Q2:** How does the reciprocity rule overpower factors like personal liking, and what does this reveal about its depth as an influence mechanism?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Regan's experiment demonstrated that subjects who received a Coke from "Joe" bought twice as many raffle tickets from him regardless of whether they liked him. In the no-gift condition, liking strongly predicted compliance; in the gift condition, that relationship was entirely wiped out. This reveals that the reciprocity rule operates below the level of conscious preference — it is a deeply conditioned social norm that functions more like an automated response than a deliberate decision. The implication is sobering: even people we actively dislike can compel our cooperation simply by giving us something first.

</details>

---

**Q3:** Cialdini distinguishes between two mechanisms within the reciprocity framework: direct favor reciprocation and reciprocal concessions. How do these two mechanisms differ in their structure, and why are reciprocal concessions sometimes more effective?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Direct favor reciprocation works by giving a gift or benefit first, then requesting a favor in return — the recipient feels obligated to repay the generosity. Reciprocal concessions (the rejection-then-retreat technique) work differently: the requester makes a large initial request that gets refused, then backs down to a smaller request, which the target perceives as a concession deserving a concession in return. The smaller request is what the requester actually wanted all along. Reciprocal concessions can be more effective because they simultaneously engage three forces: the reciprocity rule (concession demands concession), the perceptual contrast principle (the second request looks smaller after the large one), and a structural "heads I win, tails you lose" asymmetry — if the large request is accepted, the requester gets even more than they needed.

</details>

---

**Q4:** The rejection-then-retreat technique produces not only higher initial compliance rates but also higher follow-through rates and more willingness to agree to future requests. What psychological mechanisms explain these surprising side effects?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Two by-products of the concession process drive these effects. First, targets who experience a requester retreating feel personally responsible for having "won" the concession — they believe their own resistance shaped the final outcome, even when the retreat was scripted. This sense of ownership over the agreement increases commitment to honoring it. Second, targets report greater satisfaction with arrangements reached through concession than those simply imposed, because the process feels like a negotiation rather than a demand. People who feel satisfied with a deal and responsible for its terms are naturally more willing to engage in further agreements with the same party, which is why the technique generates unusually high rates of both follow-through and future compliance.

</details>

---

**Q5:** Cialdini's recommended defense against reciprocity exploitation is not blanket refusal of all gifts, but rather mental redefinition. What exactly does this mean, and why is blanket refusal a flawed strategy?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Mental redefinition means accepting a gift or concession while internally categorizing it correctly: if it turns out to be a sales device or manipulation tactic rather than a genuine act of goodwill, you relabel it as such. The reciprocity rule only obligates you to return favors with favors — it does not obligate you to return tricks with favors. Once you identify the initial offering as a compliance tool, the psychological obligation evaporates because the rule's premise (a real gift was given) no longer holds. Blanket refusal fails because it is socially costly — it insults genuinely generous people, creates friction, and isolates you — and it is practically difficult to distinguish manipulation from authentic generosity at the moment of offering. The redefinition approach lets you accept genuine kindness while stripping manipulative gifts of their leverage after their true purpose becomes clear.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Vendor Lunch**
Your company is evaluating three software vendors for a major contract renewal. One vendor invites your evaluation team to an elaborate lunch at an upscale restaurant, picks up the tab ($400), and spends the meal emphasizing how much they value the partnership. Two days later, they submit a bid that is 15% higher than their competitors. Your manager asks for your recommendation.

*What should you consider?*
- Does the lunch represent genuine relationship-building, or is it a strategic gift designed to generate a sense of obligation before a high-stakes ask?
- How might the reciprocity rule be distorting your team's perception of the vendor's bid relative to competitors?
- What process can you put in place to ensure the evaluation is based on objective criteria rather than felt indebtedness?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The lunch is a textbook example of an uninvited gift used to trigger the reciprocity rule before a large request. The fact that the lavish meal preceded the inflated bid — rather than following a fair proposal — is the tell. The appropriate response is mental redefinition: accept that the lunch was enjoyable, but categorize it explicitly as a sales tactic rather than a genuine gift. This dissolves the reciprocity obligation. You should recommend your manager establish a clear evaluation rubric based on price, features, and support quality, and apply it equally to all three vendors. Documenting the evaluation criteria before reviewing bids helps insulate the decision from post-hoc rationalization driven by felt debt. If the vendor's product is genuinely superior at that price, choose it — but not because of the lunch.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Negotiation Opening**
You are negotiating salary for a new job offer. The hiring manager opens at $85,000. You had hoped for $100,000. Before you can counter, the manager says, "I know that's below what you may have hoped for, but let me see what I can do" — and comes back with $90,000, saying, "I went to bat for you and got an extra $5,000." You feel grateful and find yourself about to accept.

*What should you consider?*
- Is the manager's "concession" a genuine sacrifice, or is it a structured retreat designed to make $90,000 feel like a win?
- How are the reciprocity rule and the contrast principle working together in this moment?
- What would a well-prepared negotiator do instead of accepting out of gratitude?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

This is the rejection-then-retreat technique combined with the contrast principle. The opening at $85,000 may have been deliberately low to make $90,000 feel generous by comparison, and the manager's "going to bat for you" narrative frames their move as a concession deserving reciprocation. The feeling of gratitude is exactly the intended response. A well-prepared negotiator would recognize that the baseline was set by the employer, not anchored to market value, and would counter from their own research rather than reacting to the manufactured concession. You might say, "I appreciate the effort — based on my research and the scope of this role, I was targeting $100,000. Is there room to get closer to that?" By introducing an external anchor (market data), you shift the negotiation away from the employer's reciprocity frame and toward objective value, reducing the pressure to treat their retreat as a favor that demands gratitude.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Nonprofit Mailer**
You receive an unsolicited envelope from a charity you've never donated to. Inside are a personalized notepad, a sheet of return-address labels with your name printed on them, and a donation card. You have no particular connection to this cause, but you find yourself hesitating to throw the items away without donating.

*What should you consider?*
- What psychological mechanism is producing the hesitation to discard the items without donating?
- Is there an ethical obligation created by these items, or something else?
- How should you decide whether and how much to donate?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The hesitation is the reciprocity rule in action: an uninvited gift has been received, and the deeply conditioned obligation to repay is activating discomfort. The items cost the charity very little (perhaps $0.50–$1.00) but are calibrated to trigger a donation that far exceeds their value — the same asymmetry that produced a 500% ROI in Regan's Coke experiment. There is no genuine ethical obligation created by items you didn't request and didn't ask for. The honest question to ask is: "Would I donate to this cause if I had simply received a letter with no gifts?" If yes, donate an amount proportionate to how much you value the cause — not an amount calibrated to the perceived value of the labels. If no, you can keep the labels guiltlessly, because the obligation arose from a manufactured social pressure, not from a genuine relationship or cause you endorse. Using Cialdini's framework: the gifts were sales devices, not true favors, and tricks need not be repaid with favors.

</details>

## ✍️ Reflection Prompts

1. Think of a time when you agreed to something — a purchase, a commitment, a concession in an argument — and only later realized the other person had first given you a gift or done you a small favor. What would you do differently now that you understand how the reciprocity rule can override your actual preferences and even your judgment about the quality of what was being offered?

2. Think of a time when you made a large opening request in a negotiation, a favor, or a persuasion attempt and then backed down to something smaller — whether consciously or instinctively. What was the outcome? Now that you understand the rejection-then-retreat technique and its structural "heads I win, tails you lose" advantage, how might you use this framework more deliberately (and ethically) in future negotiations?

3. Think of a time when you felt genuinely uncomfortable being in someone's debt — not because you couldn't repay them, but because being obligated to them felt psychologically costly. How did that discomfort shape your behavior? Now that you understand why humans are conditioned to find indebtedness aversive (it served adaptive purposes in social evolution), how might recognizing that pressure help you slow down and evaluate whether the return favor being prompted is actually proportionate or in your interest?

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the rejection-then-retreat technique in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

When someone wants you to agree to something, they can first ask for something much bigger — something you'll almost certainly refuse — and then back down to the smaller thing they actually wanted. Because you perceive their backing down as a concession, you feel social pressure to make a concession of your own, which means agreeing to the smaller request. The result is that you end up doing what they wanted all along, feeling as though you won the negotiation, even though the entire sequence was designed to get you there.

</details>

## 📋 Action Items

1. Before your next negotiation, salary discussion, or vendor meeting this week, write down your actual target outcome on paper before the conversation begins. This anchors your decision-making to your own interests rather than to whatever opening gambit the other party uses — making you resistant to both the contrast principle and the rejection-then-retreat technique in the moment.

2. The next time someone gives you a gift, free sample, or unsolicited favor before making a request of you — whether a vendor, a colleague, or a solicitor — pause before responding and explicitly ask yourself: "Is this a genuine act of goodwill, or is it a compliance device?" Label it out loud if you need to. The mental redefinition takes seconds and is the single most effective defense Cialdini recommends.

3. Review one recurring request you make of others this week (asking a teammate for help, getting a client to respond, persuading a family member) and deliberately restructure it using the rejection-then-retreat sequence: open with a larger ask than you actually need, then retreat to what you truly want. Track whether compliance and follow-through rates differ from your usual approach, and reflect on whether you stayed within ethical bounds.
