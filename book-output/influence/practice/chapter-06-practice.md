# Practice Exercises: Chapter 6 — Commitment and Consistency: Hobgoblins of the Mind

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why does Cialdini argue that automatic consistency functions as a "shield against thought," and why do people sometimes deliberately seek that shield?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Automatic consistency removes the burden of continuous re-evaluation: once a commitment is made, the consistency tape plays and we simply act in line with the prior decision without expending mental energy re-examining it. Beyond the cognitive relief, people also use it as an emotional refuge. The TM lecture example illustrates this vividly — audience members who understood the logical demolition of the presentation rushed to pay their deposit precisely because they feared that thinking further would leave them without hope for solving their real problems. The commitment served as a fortress wall against the unwelcome conclusions that continued thought might produce.

</details>

---

**Q2:** Cialdini identifies four properties that make a commitment most effective at changing a person's self-image and future behavior. What are they, and which does he consider most important — and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The four properties are: active (you perform or write it yourself, not merely hear it), public (others witness it, triggering social consistency pressure), effortful (the harder it is to obtain, the more the person values what they committed to), and internally owned — meaning the commitment was made without strong external rewards or threats so the person attributes the action to their own values. Cialdini considers internal ownership the most important by far, because only a commitment made without obvious outside pressure causes a genuine change in self-image. A large reward or severe threat produces temporary compliance but leaves the person free to say "I only did it for the reward," which prevents the belief-altering mechanism from activating.

</details>

---

**Q3:** Explain how the foot-in-the-door technique produces compliance with requests that are far removed in content from the original small request, using the Freedman and Fraser experiment as evidence.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Freedman and Fraser found that homeowners who had signed a petition for "keeping California beautiful" were significantly more likely to allow a large, ugly DRIVE CAREFULLY billboard on their lawns — a completely different topic — than people who had not signed anything. The mechanism is self-image change: the small act of signing a civic petition caused people to see themselves as public-spirited citizens who support good causes. That new self-image then made them willing to comply with any other request that fit the identity of "someone who does this sort of thing," regardless of whether it was topically related to the original commitment. The commitment works not by creating issue-specific loyalty but by altering how people categorize themselves.

</details>

---

**Q4:** What is the lowball tactic, how does it exploit the commitment-consistency mechanism, and why does it often succeed even after the original inducement is removed?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

In lowballing, a compliance professional offers an attractive deal that induces a purchase decision, then removes or degrades that deal before the transaction is finalized. The tactic exploits the fact that once a decision is made, people begin generating new, independent reasons to justify it — they picture themselves owning the car, feel excited about it, arrange financing mentally, and so on. By the time the original price advantage disappears, these self-generated justifications have grown strong enough to hold the decision upright on their own. The buyer has, in effect, built their own cage. The Iowa energy-conservation study illustrates an even more striking version: when the promise of newspaper publicity was removed, residents not only maintained their conservation behavior but increased it, because the removal of the external reason allowed them to fully own the commitment as a reflection of their own values.

</details>

---

**Q5:** Cialdini offers two internal signals to detect when consistency is being exploited. What are they, what are their limits, and how does the "heart of hearts" technique work?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The first signal is a gut tightening — a stomach-level warning that fires when we are obviously being maneuvered, as Cialdini experienced when trapped by the entertainment-club surveyor. Its limitation is that it only activates when the manipulation is transparent; in cases where we have genuinely rationalized a bad commitment to ourselves (like Sara with Tim), the stomach never protests because we believe we are acting correctly. The second signal is what Cialdini calls the "heart of hearts" — the first, unfiltered emotional flash that precedes conscious rationalization. To use it, one asks: "Knowing what I now know, if I could go back in time, would I make the same choice?" The answer must be read from the very first impression felt before the mind can summon justifications. This pre-cognitive signal reveals whether supporting reasons are genuine motivators or post-hoc rationalizations constructed to protect a commitment already made.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Nonprofit Survey Call**
Your organization is designing a telephone outreach campaign to solicit donations for a local food bank. A colleague proposes opening every call with "How are you doing today?" and waiting for the answer before presenting the ask. Another colleague objects that this seems manipulative and suggests jumping straight to the pitch.

*What should you consider?*
- Does the "How are you doing?" opener actually influence compliance, and through what mechanism?
- Is using this technique ethically different from other forms of persuasion, and does the cause matter?
- What does the chapter suggest about the durability of compliance obtained this way versus a straightforward ask?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The technique works precisely as Cialdini describes through Daniel Howard's research: eliciting a positive self-report ("Fine, thanks") creates a small public commitment to one's own favorable circumstances, making a subsequent refusal to help those worse off feel inconsistent and stingy. Howard found nearly double the compliance rate (32% vs. 18%) compared to the standard ask, and 89% of those who agreed did follow through — suggesting the compliance is genuine rather than superficial. The ethical question is real but nuanced: the opener exploits a social reflex rather than providing information relevant to the decision. Using it for a legitimate cause does not neutralize the manipulation, though it reduces the harm to the target. The more defensible approach is to use it knowingly, briefly, and in service of a cause the donor would likely endorse on reflection — but leaders should acknowledge internally that it is an influence technique, not mere courtesy, and be prepared to justify that choice.

</details>

**Scenario 2: Managing a Direct Report's Performance Goal**
You are a manager at a sales firm. You want your team members to commit to ambitious quarterly targets. One approach is to assign targets top-down and announce them in a team meeting. Another is to ask each person to write down their own goal privately and hand it to you. A third option is to have each person state their goal aloud in the team meeting and post goals on the shared dashboard.

*What should you consider?*
- How does the chapter's framework predict different levels of commitment across these three approaches?
- What are the risks of the most powerful approach?
- How does the concept of internal ownership interact with management-imposed goals?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The chapter predicts that commitment strength follows this order: top-down assignment (no personal commitment) < private written goal < publicly stated and posted goal. The Deutsch and Gerard line-length experiment confirms that public commitment produces the most stubborn adherence — subjects who publicly recorded estimates were the least willing to revise them even when presented with contradictory evidence. The public posting approach will produce the strongest commitment. However, the critical caveat from the chapter's discussion of inner ownership is that the goal must feel self-chosen, not externally coerced. If team members feel pressured to write an inflated number to please the manager, the written or public commitment will produce compliance without genuine internal buy-in, and may backfire when circumstances change. The ideal is to use a structure where employees genuinely choose their own ambitious targets and make them public — combining the active, public, and internally owned properties. Managers should also be aware of the flip side: a publicly committed employee will resist downward revision of a goal even when business conditions clearly warrant it, potentially driving poor decisions out of consistency pressure.

</details>

**Scenario 3: Recognizing a Lowball in a Lease Negotiation**
You have spent three weekends apartment hunting. You find an apartment you love, negotiate the rent down to your budget ceiling, and sign a letter of intent. Two days before lease signing, the landlord calls to say the building's owner has rejected the negotiated rent and the price will be $150/month higher than agreed. The landlord expresses frustration and says the owner is being difficult but that he personally recommends you take it because "you've already done all the work to get here."

*What should you consider?*
- What compliance tactic is likely being used, and what mechanism makes it effective at this moment?
- What new justifications have you likely already generated since signing the letter of intent?
- How would you apply the "heart of hearts" question to evaluate your real position?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

This is a textbook lowball. The letter of intent, the mental and physical investment of three weekends of searching, and the excitement of picturing yourself in the space have all caused you to generate independent reasons to want this particular apartment — the natural light, the commute, the layout, the neighborhood. The landlord's framing ("you've already done all the work") is designed to make those sunk costs feel decisive. The commitment has grown its own legs, and the $150 increase feels small relative to the total decision already made. To evaluate clearly, apply the "heart of hearts" test: if you had known from the beginning the actual price was $150 higher, would you have pursued this apartment, or would you have kept looking? Focus on the first, uncensored answer before the justifications flood in. If the honest answer is "no, I would have passed," then the new reasons you've generated since signing the letter of intent are rationalization, not genuine value. At that point, walking away is the correct choice, even though the sunk search cost feels painful — that cost is gone regardless of what you decide now.

</details>

## ✍️ Reflection Prompts

1. Think of a time when you made a small, seemingly inconsequential commitment — signing a petition, agreeing to attend an event, saying "yes" to a minor favor — and later found yourself doing something much larger or more burdensome than you ever intended. Now that you understand how foot-in-the-door works and how small commitments alter your self-image, what would you do differently at the moment of that first small ask?

2. Think of a belief you currently hold strongly — a political view, a lifestyle choice, a professional stance — and ask yourself honestly: "Did I choose this position because I thought it through, or did I take a public stand early on and then spend years finding reasons to support it?" What would it mean to apply Cialdini's "heart of hearts" test to that belief today, and what would you need to be willing to do with the answer?

3. Think of a time when you tried to change someone else's behavior — a child, a partner, an employee, a friend — by using a strong threat or a significant reward, and the change didn't stick once the pressure was gone. Now that you understand the difference between external compliance and inner ownership, how would you redesign that approach to produce genuine, lasting change rather than temporary performance?

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the foot-in-the-door technique and why it works in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

When someone agrees to a small request — signing a petition, accepting a free sample, taking a survey — they unconsciously update their self-image to include "I am the kind of person who does things like this." That updated identity then creates internal pressure to act consistently with it, making them far more likely to say yes to a larger, related request later, even one they would have refused if asked cold. The trick is not the size of the first request but the self-image change it quietly produces: you're no longer being asked to do something new, you're being asked to stay true to who you've already decided you are.

</details>

## 📋 Action Items

1. Before your next one-on-one meeting or team check-in, ask each person to write down — by hand, not typed — one specific goal they want to accomplish before your next meeting, sign it, and read it aloud to the group. Notice the difference in follow-through compared to goals you simply announce or assign, and reflect on what the chapter predicts about why that difference exists.

2. Identify one belief, preference, or habit you hold that you have publicly defended or built an identity around, and run the "heart of hearts" test on it this week: ask yourself "If I had never taken that public stand or made that first commitment, would I still hold this position?" Sit with the first, unfiltered answer for at least 60 seconds before letting your rationalizations respond.

3. The next time you are in a sales or negotiation situation — buying a car, renewing a subscription, agreeing to a contract — pause before signing anything and ask: "What was the original reason I decided to move forward with this, and is that reason still present and unchanged?" If the original reason has shifted, been removed, or looks different than it did when you said yes, treat that as a lowball signal and re-evaluate from scratch before committing further.
