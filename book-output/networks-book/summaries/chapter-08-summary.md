# Chapter 6: Games

## 🧠 Core Thesis
Individual rationality — each person choosing the best strategy for themselves — does not guarantee good collective outcomes; game theory provides a precise language for understanding how interdependent decisions produce equilibria that can be stable, multiple, or tragically suboptimal compared to what cooperation could achieve.

## 📖 Detailed Breakdown

### What Is a Game? The Basic Framework
- **What it is:** A game is any situation with three ingredients: a set of players, a set of strategies available to each player, and a payoff function that maps every combination of chosen strategies to a numerical reward for each player. The payoff matrix is the canonical tool for representing two-player games: rows are one player's strategies, columns are the other's, and each cell contains both players' payoffs in the form (Player 1 payoff, Player 2 payoff).
- **Why it matters:** The framework is powerful because it captures a huge range of real situations — exam preparation, pricing wars, arms races, auctions, network routing — under a single unified model. Once you can write down the payoff matrix, all the analytical tools of game theory apply.
- **How it works:** The opening example is two college students who must independently decide whether to study for an exam or prepare a joint presentation. If both prepare the presentation they each average 90; if both study they each average 88; if one studies while the other prepares, the studier gets 92 (free-riding on the presenter's joint grade) and the preparer gets 86. The key feature is that each student's outcome depends not just on their own decision but on their partner's as well — this strategic interdependence is what makes it a game.
- **Key quote or example:** The payoff matrix for the Exam-or-Presentation Game:

  |  | Partner: Presentation | Partner: Exam |
  |---|---|---|
  | You: Presentation | 90, 90 | 86, 92 |
  | You: Exam | 92, 86 | 88, 88 |

- **Connection:** This game serves as the running example throughout the entire chapter and is used to illustrate dominant strategies, Nash equilibrium, Pareto-optimality, Stag Hunt variants, and Hawk-Dove variants as the payoffs are tweaked.

### Underlying Assumptions and Rationality
- **What it is:** Game theory rests on two key assumptions: (1) payoffs capture everything a player cares about (so if a player is altruistic, their altruism must be encoded in the payoff numbers); (2) each player is rational, meaning they want to maximize their own payoff and they successfully choose the strategy that does so.
- **Why it matters:** These assumptions are necessary to make predictions. The payoff-completeness assumption means you cannot complain "but they care about fairness" without adjusting the payoffs to reflect that. The rationality assumption breaks down in complex games played by inexperienced players — there is an entire literature on learning and bounded rationality — but it is a reasonable starting point.
- **How it works:** The chapter also assumes common knowledge: each player knows the full payoff matrix, knows that the other player knows it, knows that the other player knows they know it, and so on. Common knowledge is a stronger assumption than just mutual knowledge and enables players to reason about each other's reasoning.
- **Connection:** Common knowledge connects to the discussion of the Marketing Strategy Game, where Firm 2 needs to know Firm 1's payoffs to predict Firm 1's strategy.

### Best Responses and Dominant Strategies
- **What it is:** A best response by Player 1 to a strategy T by Player 2 is any strategy S that achieves at least as high a payoff as any other strategy paired with T: P₁(S,T) ≥ P₁(S',T) for all alternative strategies S'. A strict best response achieves strictly more: P₁(S,T) > P₁(S',T). A dominant strategy is a strategy that is a best response to every possible strategy of the opponent. A strictly dominant strategy is a strict best response to every opposing strategy — it is uniquely best no matter what the opponent does.
- **Why it matters:** When a player has a strictly dominant strategy, prediction is trivial: rational players will always play it. No reasoning about the opponent's intentions is needed.
- **How it works:** In the Exam-or-Presentation Game, studying for the exam is strictly dominant for you: if your partner prepares, you get 92 by studying vs. 90 by preparing (studying wins); if your partner studies, you get 88 by studying vs. 86 by preparing (studying wins again). Because the game is symmetric, studying is also dominant for your partner. Prediction: both study, each gets 88 — even though both would prefer the (90, 90) outcome if both had prepared.
- **Key quote or example:** "When a player has a strategy that is strictly better than all other options regardless of what the other player does, we will refer to it as a strictly dominant strategy."
- **Connection:** Dominant strategies feed directly into the Prisoner's Dilemma analysis, and the absence of dominant strategies motivates Nash equilibrium.

### The Prisoner's Dilemma
- **What it is:** Perhaps the most famous game in all of game theory, the Prisoner's Dilemma captures the tragedy of individual rationality. Two suspects are interrogated separately. Each can Confess (C) or Not Confess (NC). The payoff matrix (measured in negative prison years):

  |  | Suspect 2: NC | Suspect 2: C |
  |---|---|---|
  | Suspect 1: NC | -1, -1 | -10, 0 |
  | Suspect 1: C | 0, -10 | -4, -4 |

- **Why it matters:** Confessing is strictly dominant for both suspects, so both confess and each gets -4. But if both had stayed silent, each would get only -1. Rational individual play produces a collectively worse outcome. This structure is not just a curiosity — it models arms races between nations, performance-enhancing drug use in professional sports (Figure 6.3 shows the drug-use payoff matrix directly), price wars, and many situations where individual incentives diverge from collective welfare.
- **How it works:** The Prisoner's Dilemma arises only when payoffs are aligned in a specific way: the dominant strategy leads to an outcome that is worse for everyone than the cooperative outcome. Even small changes to payoffs — like making the exam easier in the Exam-or-Presentation Game — can eliminate the dilemma entirely and produce a situation where individual and collective rationality align.
- **Key quote or example:** "There is an outcome that the suspects know to be better for both of them — in which they both choose not to confess — but under rational play of the game there is no way for them to achieve this outcome."
- **Connection:** The Prisoner's Dilemma motivates the concepts of Pareto-optimality and social optimality discussed in Section 6.9, and its structure will reappear in arms races, adoption cascades, and market pricing in later chapters.

### Nash Equilibrium
- **What it is:** Proposed by John Nash in 1950 (for which he shared the 1994 Nobel Prize in Economics), a Nash equilibrium is a pair of strategies (S, T) such that S is a best response to T and T is a best response to S. Neither player has a unilateral incentive to deviate. It is an equilibrium concept — a fixed point in the space of mutual best responses — rather than a consequence of rationality alone.
- **Why it matters:** Nash equilibrium is the central solution concept of game theory. It applies even when no player has a dominant strategy, dramatically widening the scope of games that can be analyzed. Nash's great theorem proved that every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium, once mixed strategies (randomization) are allowed.
- **How it works:** To find Nash equilibria, compute each player's best response to each of the opponent's strategies, then look for strategy pairs where each strategy is a best response to the other. The Three-Client Game (Figure 6.6) has three strategies per firm (approach client A, B, or C) and no dominant strategies. The only Nash equilibrium is (A, A): both firms approach the largest client and split its business (payoff 4 each), even though both would prefer to approach the same smaller client and win it outright. Nash equilibrium can be thought of as "an equilibrium in beliefs": if each player believes the other will play their Nash equilibrium strategy, that belief is self-consistent and neither player wants to deviate.
- **Connection:** Nash equilibrium generalizes dominant strategy reasoning; when a strictly dominant strategy exists, playing it is automatically a Nash equilibrium. The concept connects forward to coordination games and mixed strategies.

### Multiple Equilibria: Coordination Games
- **What it is:** Many natural games have more than one Nash equilibrium. The Coordination Game (Figure 6.7) involves two people each choosing independently between PowerPoint and Keynote for a joint project. If they match, payoff is 1 each; if they don't match, payoff is 0 each. Both (PowerPoint, PowerPoint) and (Keynote, Keynote) are Nash equilibria.
- **Why it matters:** When there are multiple equilibria, Nash equilibrium alone cannot predict behavior — it only narrows down the set of plausible outcomes. Real-world examples include driving on the left or right side of the road, choosing measurement systems, and military coordination.
- **How it works:** Thomas Schelling's concept of a focal point (salience) helps. When equilibria are symmetric, external social conventions can serve as focal points (e.g., the U.S. drives on the right). The Unbalanced Coordination Game (Figure 6.8) has two equilibria but one is strictly better for both players — Schelling's theory predicts players will gravitate to the superior equilibrium. The Battle of the Sexes (Figure 6.9) complicates things further: there are two equilibria, but each player prefers a different one. Here, neither a payoff-intrinsic focal point nor an arbitrary social convention is sufficient; resolution requires knowing something about how these specific players resolve disagreements. The Stag Hunt (Figure 6.10) adds another wrinkle: hunting stag together yields the highest payoff (4,4), but if you try to cooperate and your partner defects to hunt hare, you get 0 while they get 3. Hunt Hare alone gives a safe payoff of 3. Both (Stag, Stag) and (Hare, Hare) are equilibria, but choosing Stag is risky — the challenge is the trade-off between high reward and the cost of miscoordination.
- **Connection:** The Stag Hunt structure resembles the Prisoner's Dilemma in that both games raise cooperation challenges, but the mechanism differs: in the Prisoner's Dilemma, defection is dominant; in the Stag Hunt, cooperation is possible but depends on trust.

### Multiple Equilibria: The Hawk-Dove Game
- **What it is:** A fundamentally different kind of multiple-equilibrium game is the Hawk-Dove Game (Figure 6.12), where the two Nash equilibria are anti-coordination outcomes. Two animals contest food. Each can behave aggressively (Hawk) or passively (Dove). If both play Dove: each gets 3. If one Hawk and one Dove: Hawk gets 5, Dove gets 1. If both Hawk: each gets 0 (they destroy the food fighting). The two Nash equilibria are (Hawk, Dove) and (Dove, Hawk) — one player is aggressive and one is passive.
- **Why it matters:** Unlike coordination games (where players want to match), Hawk-Dove games require differentiation. Real-world versions include two countries deciding foreign policy posture, two drivers in a game of Chicken, and two firms deciding whether to compete aggressively or cede a market segment. The equilibria predict one side will back down, but the theory cannot say which.
- **How it works:** The Hawk-Dove structure arises in the Exam-or-Presentation Game if the penalty for neither preparing is severe enough (Figure 6.13). With payoffs showing that if both study for the exam the joint grade collapses to 76, the equilibria become (Presentation, Exam) and (Exam, Presentation) — exactly one person must play the passive role. The game is also known as "Chicken": two cars race toward each other, the one that swerves loses face but avoids catastrophe; neither swerving is catastrophic for both.
- **Connection:** Hawk-Dove connects to evolutionary biology applications in Chapter 7, where the mixed-strategy equilibrium of this game corresponds to a stable population mix of Hawk and Dove phenotypes.

### Mixed Strategies
- **What it is:** A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over a player's pure strategies. Instead of choosing H or T (as in Matching Pennies), a player chooses a probability p of playing H. Pure strategies are the special cases p=0 and p=1. The payoff from a mixed strategy is the expected value of the payoff across all random outcomes.
- **Why it matters:** Some games have no Nash equilibrium in pure strategies. Matching Pennies (Figure 6.14) is the canonical example — a zero-sum game where Player 1 wins a penny if the pennies don't match, and loses if they do. For any pure strategy profile, one player can improve by switching. Nash's great theorem guarantees that every finite game has at least one mixed-strategy equilibrium.
- **How it works:** In Matching Pennies, if Player 2 plays H with probability q, Player 1's expected payoff from pure H is 1-2q and from pure T is 2q-1. For a mixed equilibrium to exist, Player 1 must be indifferent between H and T: 1-2q = 2q-1, giving q = 1/2. By symmetry, p = 1/2. The key insight: in a mixed equilibrium, each player randomizes precisely to make the opponent indifferent between their pure strategies, making their own behavior "non-exploitable." The Run-Pass Game (Figure 6.15) illustrates asymmetric mixed equilibria: passing (the stronger option) yields 10 yards when undefended, running yields 5 yards when undefended. The equilibrium has the offense passing only 1/3 of the time and the defense defending against the pass 2/3 of the time. Counterintuitively, the offense uses its stronger weapon less than half the time — but the threat of passing forces the defense to overweight pass defense, which is itself the benefit.
- **Key quote or example:** "The strategy q = 1/2 is effectively 'non-exploitable' by Player 1 ... each player wants their behavior to be unpredictable to the other, so that their behavior can't be taken advantage of."
- **Connection:** Mixed strategies connect to evolutionary biology (Chapter 7), where a mixed-strategy equilibrium can represent a stable population where fixed proportions of individuals play each pure strategy.

### The Penalty-Kick Game: Empirical Validation of Mixed Strategy Theory
- **What it is:** Ignacio Palacios-Huerta's 2002 study of approximately 1,400 professional soccer penalty kicks provides a remarkable empirical test of mixed-strategy equilibrium. The kicker aims left or right; the goalie dives left or right. From empirical scoring rates, the payoff matrix (Figure 6.16) is:

  |  | Goalie: L | Goalie: R |
  |---|---|---|
  | Kicker: L | 0.58, -0.58 | 0.95, -0.95 |
  | Kicker: R | 0.93, -0.93 | 0.70, -0.70 |

- **Why it matters:** This is a rare case where game theory makes precise, quantitative predictions about behavior that can be tested against real data from high-stakes experts. The theory predicts the goalie dives left 42% of the time and the kicker aims left 39% of the time. In the actual data, goalies dive left 42% of the time and kickers aim left 40% of the time — matching the theory to within one percentage point.
- **How it works:** The indifference equations are set up using the empirical scoring probabilities. Setting the goalie's randomization probability q to make the kicker indifferent: 0.58q + 0.95(1-q) = 0.93q + 0.70(1-q), solving to q = 0.42. The result holds precisely in expert, high-stakes play — exactly the conditions under which game-theoretic rationality is most plausible.
- **Connection:** This example validates the broader mixed-strategy framework before moving to the social welfare analysis in Section 6.9.

### Pareto-Optimality and Social Optimality
- **What it is:** Two concepts for evaluating whether an outcome is "good for society." An outcome is Pareto-optimal if there is no alternative outcome where all players do at least as well and at least one does strictly better. An outcome is socially optimal (a social welfare maximizer) if it maximizes the sum of all players' payoffs.
- **Why it matters:** Nash equilibria can be Pareto-dominated — rational individual play can lead to outcomes that are worse for everyone than some achievable alternative. This is the formal statement of what goes wrong in the Prisoner's Dilemma and the Exam-or-Presentation Game.
- **How it works:** In the Exam-or-Presentation Game, the Nash equilibrium (Exam, Exam) with payoffs (88, 88) is not Pareto-optimal: the outcome (Presentation, Presentation) gives (90, 90), which is better for both. The cooperative outcome is the social optimum (total payoff 180). The equilibrium outcome yields total payoff 176. But the cooperative outcome cannot be achieved by rational individual play — each player has an incentive to defect from it. Note that social optimality implies Pareto-optimality, but not vice versa: the Exam-or-Presentation Game has three Pareto-optimal outcomes, only one of which is socially optimal. Crucially, Nash equilibria are not always at odds with social optimality — in the easier-exam version of the game, the unique Nash equilibrium is also the unique social optimum.
- **Connection:** The tension between Nash equilibria and Pareto-optimality is a central theme that carries forward to network routing inefficiencies (Braess's paradox in Chapter 8) and auction design (Chapter 9).

### Iterated Deletion of Strictly Dominated Strategies
- **What it is:** A procedure for solving games that lack dominant strategies by repeatedly removing strategies that are strictly dominated — strategies for which some other strategy always does strictly better, regardless of opponents' choices. The procedure iterates: after removing dominated strategies, new strategies may become dominated in the reduced game, and these are removed in turn.
- **Why it matters:** Iterated deletion can uniquely predict the outcome of a game using only the assumption of rationality plus common knowledge, without requiring the equilibrium concept. When it leads to a unique outcome, that outcome is also the unique Nash equilibrium, and the reasoning justifying it is grounded purely in rationality rather than in the assumption that play will reach equilibrium.
- **How it works:** The Facility Location Game (Figure 6.20) has two firms choosing locations (A, C, or E for Firm 1; B, D, or F for Firm 2) along a six-town highway. No firm has a dominant strategy. But A is strictly dominated for Firm 1 (C always does better), and F is strictly dominated for Firm 2 (D always does better). After removing A and F, strategies B and E become dominated. After removing those, only (C, D) remains — both firms locate in the center. This is the unique Nash equilibrium. The iterated deletion process also illuminates the "median voter theorem" intuition: competing firms (or political candidates) gravitate to the center of their domain to maximize coverage at the expense of competitors. Crucially, unlike iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies, iterated deletion of weakly dominated strategies is order-dependent and can destroy Nash equilibria — so it must be used with caution.
- **Connection:** Iterated deletion provides a rational-choice foundation for Nash equilibrium in games where the equilibrium can be derived step by step through pure logic, bridging the gap between dominance reasoning and equilibrium reasoning.

### Dynamic Games: Extensive Form and Backward Induction
- **What it is:** Dynamic games are games where players move sequentially rather than simultaneously. They are represented in extensive form (a game tree): a rooted tree where internal nodes represent decision points labeled by the player who moves there, edges represent choices, and terminal nodes carry payoff vectors. The normal form of a dynamic game encodes complete contingent plans (what each player does in every possible situation) as strategies, then derives payoffs — creating the familiar payoff matrix.
- **Why it matters:** Many economically important situations are sequential — negotiations, market entry, technology adoption — and the dynamic structure creates new phenomena that the static normal form can obscure.
- **How it works:** Backward induction: start from terminal nodes, determine what the last-mover will do in each situation, then work backward up the tree. In the Market Entry Game (Figure 6.26), Firm 1 decides first whether to Stay Out or Enter. If Firm 1 enters, Firm 2 decides to Cooperate (payoffs 1,1) or Retaliate (payoffs -1,-1). Working backward: if entry occurs, Firm 2 prefers Cooperation (1 > -1). Knowing this, Firm 1 prefers Entry (payoff 1 > staying out with payoff 0). The extensive-form prediction is (Enter, Cooperate). But the normal form of the same game (Figure 6.27) reveals a second Nash equilibrium: (Stay Out, Retaliate). This equilibrium corresponds to Firm 2 pre-committing to always retaliate — a credible threat that deters entry. The extensive form makes clear this threat is not credible: when entry actually happens, Firm 2 would prefer to cooperate. The normal form conflates the threat strategy with the actual decision node, obscuring this distinction. The key question is whether pre-commitment is possible: if Firm 2 can credibly pre-commit (e.g., by publicly advertising a price-war policy), the (Stay Out, Retaliate) equilibrium can be sustained in the real world.
- **Connection:** The distinction between credible and non-credible threats is fundamental to understanding market entry deterrence, international relations, and negotiation — topics that appear throughout the later chapters on networks and markets.

## 🔑 Key Takeaways

1. A game has three ingredients: players, strategies, and payoffs. Everything else in game theory follows from these.
2. When a player has a strictly dominant strategy, they should always play it regardless of what the opponent does — no strategic reasoning about the opponent is required.
3. The Prisoner's Dilemma shows that individual rationality can produce collectively terrible outcomes. This is not a failure of the model — it is precisely what the model predicts and why institutions, contracts, and repeated interactions matter.
4. Nash equilibrium is the central prediction tool: a pair of strategies is a Nash equilibrium if and only if neither player wants to unilaterally deviate, given the other's strategy.
5. Multiple Nash equilibria create genuine ambiguity: Nash equilibrium tells you which outcomes are stable, but not which stable outcome will be reached. Schelling's focal points can help when equilibria are distinguishable by salience or shared convention.
6. In attack-defense (zero-sum) games, no pure-strategy equilibrium exists — players must randomize to make themselves unpredictable. The equilibrium randomization is chosen to make the opponent indifferent between all their options.
7. The penalty-kick study shows that professional athletes in high-stakes settings actually behave according to mixed-strategy equilibrium predictions — game theory is not just theoretical.
8. Nash equilibria are often Pareto-suboptimal: rational individual play can leave everyone worse off than some feasible alternative. This gap between private and social optimality is a fundamental challenge for policy, regulation, and mechanism design.
9. Iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies can predict game outcomes from pure rationality, without needing the equilibrium concept — and the surviving strategies always include the Nash equilibria.
10. Dynamic games require the extensive form to fully capture their structure. The normal form can obscure whether threats are credible, introducing spurious Nash equilibria that would not survive backward induction.

## 🗺️ Mental Model / Framework

Think of game theory as a hierarchy of solution concepts, each applying when the previous one fails:

**Level 1 — Dominant Strategy:** Does one strategy always beat all others, no matter what the opponent does? If yes, play it. Prediction is certain and requires no reasoning about the opponent. (Prisoner's Dilemma, original Exam-Presentation game.)

**Level 2 — Iterated Deletion:** No dominant strategies exist, but some strategies are dominated. Remove them. In the reduced game, are there new dominated strategies? Remove those too. Keep going until you reach a stable set. If a unique outcome survives, that is the prediction. (Facility Location Game — both firms end up in the center.)

**Level 3 — Nash Equilibrium:** No iterated deletion process pins down a unique outcome. Look for strategy pairs that are mutual best responses — neither player wants to deviate given the other's choice. This is a stable "resting point" in beliefs. (Three-Client Game: both approach client A.)

**Level 4 — Multiple Nash Equilibria:** Several equilibria exist. Use Schelling focal points (social convention, symmetry breaking, payoff dominance) to predict which one. Coordination games require matching; Hawk-Dove games require differentiation.

**Level 5 — Mixed Strategy Equilibrium:** No pure-strategy equilibrium exists (typical in zero-sum attack-defense games). Players randomize so as to make opponents indifferent. Compute the indifference condition to find equilibrium probabilities.

**Level 6 — Dynamic/Extensive Form:** The game is sequential. Use backward induction (work up from terminal nodes). Check whether Nash equilibria of the normal form correspond to credible strategies at each decision node.

## 💡 "Aha!" Moments

1. **Your stronger weapon should be used less than you think.** In the Run-Pass Game, the offense's stronger weapon (passing, worth 10 yards) is used only 1/3 of the time in equilibrium, while the weaker option (running, worth 5 yards) is used 2/3 of the time. The paradox dissolves when you realize the defense responds: overusing the powerful option forces the opponent to defend against it, eliminating the advantage. The optimal play is to keep the opponent indifferent, which means using the powerful option sparingly. The threat of passing is what does the work — not the pass itself.

2. **Rational people can be trapped in outcomes they know are bad for everyone.** The Prisoner's Dilemma is not a failure of intelligence. Both suspects know that mutual silence is better. The problem is structural: neither can commit to silence without risking exploitation. This reframes how we think about cooperation failures in the real world — the problem is not that people are irrational or evil, but that the payoff structure makes cooperation individually risky even when collectively optimal.

3. **A threat that is rational to make in advance may be irrational to carry out.** In the Market Entry Game, Firm 2's threat to retaliate is a Nash equilibrium of the normal form, but it is not credible when you look at the actual decision node: if entry occurs, cooperating strictly dominates retaliating. This shows that the normal form can hide structure that matters enormously for real strategic situations. Pre-commitment devices (public announcements, contracts, burning bridges) exist precisely to convert non-credible threats into credible ones.

## 📝 In My Own Words (ELI5)

Imagine you and your friend both have to decide, separately and without talking, whether to bring snacks or drinks to a party. If you both bring snacks, everyone is okay but nobody is super happy (88 points each). If you both bring drinks, same thing — but you each get 90 points. But if one person brings snacks and the other brings drinks, the party is perfect — the drink-bringer gets 92 points and the snack-bringer gets 86.

Here's the trap: no matter what your friend is going to bring, you personally do better by bringing drinks. If they bring snacks, you get 92 (vs. 90) by bringing drinks. If they bring drinks, you get 90 (vs. 88) by also bringing drinks. So drinks is the "dominant" choice — the safe, selfish choice. Your friend reasons the same way. Result: you both bring drinks, and the party is just okay (88 points each), even though you both knew that if you'd just coordinated on snacks, everyone would have been happier (90 points each).

That trap — where "what's best for me" leads to "what's bad for everyone" — is called the Prisoner's Dilemma. It's why countries build up nuclear weapons they'd rather not have, why athletes take performance-enhancing drugs even knowing the risks, and why competing businesses sometimes race to the bottom.

Game theory gives us tools to think about these traps. The most important tool is Nash equilibrium: a situation where neither person wants to change what they're doing, given what the other is doing. In the snack/drink game, both bringing drinks is a Nash equilibrium — if you're already bringing drinks and your friend is bringing drinks, switching to snacks makes you worse off. So nobody has a reason to change, and you're stuck there.

Sometimes games have multiple Nash equilibria, which creates a different problem. Think about PowerPoint vs. Keynote: you both need to use the same software, but you can't talk to each other first. Both (PowerPoint, PowerPoint) and (Keynote, Keynote) are fine — either equilibrium works — but if you choose differently, you're both stuck with incompatible slides. The theory doesn't tell you which to pick. That's why social conventions exist: in the U.S., everyone drives on the right, not because the right side is special, but because everyone expects everyone else to drive on the right, so it becomes a self-reinforcing expectation.

In games like soccer penalty kicks, there's no stable pure choice at all — if the kicker always aimed left, the goalie would always dive left. So both sides have to randomize, and the right mix turns out to be exactly what professional players actually do in real games. The theory predicts they'll kick left 39% of the time; data shows 40%. That's remarkable.

Finally, game theory asks: are the equilibria we reach actually good for society? Often, they're not. The rational outcome can be worse for everyone than some achievable alternative. Understanding this gap — between what's individually rational and what's collectively best — is essential for understanding why we have laws, contracts, regulations, and cooperative institutions. They exist to change the game so that individual self-interest and collective good point in the same direction.
