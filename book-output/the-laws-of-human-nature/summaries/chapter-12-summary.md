# Chapter 12: Change Your Circumstances by Changing Your Attitude (The Law of Self-sabotage)

## Core Thesis
Each person perceives the world through a personal attitudinal lens that is not a passive filter but an active force: it shapes what we see, what we experience, how others respond to us, and ultimately what outcomes we create. Because attitude is malleable, deliberately moving it from constricted and fearful to expansive and generous is the single most powerful lever available to a human being for transforming their life circumstances.

---

## Detailed Breakdown

### Attitude as the Lens of Reality

- **What it is:** Attitude is not a mood or an opinion; it is what Carl Jung defined as "a readiness of the psyche to act or react in a certain way" — a pre-conscious orientation that determines which stimuli we register, how we interpret them, and what emotional background we carry through the day. It is the filter through which every experience passes before we are even aware of it.
- **Why it matters:** Because attitude is pre-conscious, most people treat their perception of the world as objective reality. They believe Paris is cold, their coworker is hostile, or their situation is hopeless — not realizing these are outputs of their own attitudinal processing. Recognizing this illusion is the necessary first step toward change.
- **How it works:** In the course of a day the brain responds to thousands of stimuli. Depending on psychological wiring and prior experience, certain stimuli trigger stronger neural firings. Someone with a depressive disposition will disproportionately notice signals that confirm sadness; someone suspicious will amplify any facial ambiguity into a perceived slight. These firings accumulate into an overall emotional background — what we experience as our general mood or worldview. The process is circular: the attitude selects what we notice, and what we notice reinforces the attitude.
- **Key quote or example:** Greene presents two students sent to Paris for a year. The timid, insecure young man finds the weather gloomy, the Parisians cold, even Notre Dame disappointing. The adventurous young woman finds the language a pleasant challenge, the city enchanting. Same city, same weather, same people — radically different realities produced entirely by the attitudinal lens each brings.
- **Connection:** This is the cognitive foundation for every other concept in the chapter; the five constricted attitude types and the four dimensions of the expansive attitude are all specific applications of this core mechanism.

---

### The Self-Fulfilling Dynamic of Attitude

- **What it is:** Attitude does not merely color experience passively — it actively produces the circumstances it anticipates. A negative attitude triggers behavioral and interpersonal patterns that generate negative outcomes, which then confirm the original attitude. A positive attitude triggers the reverse chain. Greene calls this the "self-fulfilling dynamic."
- **Why it matters:** This is the mechanism of self-sabotage. People with hostile or anxious attitudes do not simply suffer more; they unconsciously engineer the very betrayals, rejections, and failures they fear most — and because they cannot see their own role, they treat the outcomes as evidence that the world is against them.
- **How it works:** The insecure student in Paris reacts defensively to language mistakes, which makes learning harder, which reduces social contact, which deepens depression, which lowers energy, which makes him less engaging to others — a tightening spiral. The hostile person subtly provokes tension in every interaction, collects the reactions as proof of persecution, and escalates. The anxious person micromanages to the point of sabotage. The avoidant person exits every endeavor before it reaches a stage where judgment is possible. In each case, the person never sees the initiating role their own attitude played.
- **Key quote or example:** "The way we think about people tends to have a like effect upon them. If we feel hostile and critical, we tend to inspire critical emotions in other people."
- **Connection:** Directly extends the attitudinal lens concept and underpins the practical advice to run a "positive thoughts experiment" as a diagnostic for one's own hostile tendencies.

### The Origins of Attitude: Genes, Early Experience, and Trauma

- **What it is:** Attitude is shaped by two main forces — genetic predispositions that manifest even in infancy (tendencies toward hostility, empathy, greed, or adventurousness visible before any socialization), and early attachment experiences including parental voices, traumas, and internalized authority figures.
- **Why it matters:** Understanding origin is essential for changing attitude, because the attitude feels like reality itself rather than a learned lens. Knowing it was constructed — from a frightened child's survival strategy, from an abusive parent's voice internalized — creates the psychological distance needed to examine and revise it.
- **How it works:** Early childhood trauma or neglect creates constricting psychological defenses. A child who could not rely on consistent parental warmth learns to narrow expectations as a way to avoid repeated disappointment. A child who was beaten learns either to become the source of hostility (to control it) or to expect the world as punishing. These defensive structures stabilize into persistent attitudinal patterns. Positive experiences — admired mentors, loving peers — can reshape the attitude in a more expansive direction by providing new internalized voices.
- **Key quote or example:** The Chekhov siblings all endured the same father's beatings, yet Anton developed an ironic, detached perspective that let him eventually reframe his father as a helpless victim rather than a tyrant. His siblings, lacking that genetic predisposition toward detachment, remained enmeshed in the father's brutality their entire lives. "This would seem to indicate something different in the way Anton's brain was wired."
- **Connection:** Links to Chapter 4's discussion of attachment schemas and toxic types; the anxious attitude section explicitly references the Pleaser type from that chapter.

### The Five Constricted (Negative) Attitude Types

Greene identifies five specific forms of the narrowed, fear-based attitude. Each is worth understanding both to recognize in oneself (in weakened form) and to navigate in others.

**1. The Hostile Attitude**

- **What it is:** A worldview organized around the expectation of attack. The hostile person projects their own aggressive feelings onto others and reads hostility into neutral or friendly behavior.
- **How it works:** They become experts at provoking the very hostility they claim to suffer — arriving late, doing poor work, making bad first impressions, delivering cutting "jokes." When others eventually react with frustration or anger, this confirms the hostile person's original belief that people are against them. They never see themselves as instigating.
- **Key example:** A husband accuses his wife of not loving him. If she protests, he sees it as defensive cover. If she goes quiet, he sees it as confirmation. Either path proves his premise. Heads I win, tails you lose — this is the closed logical loop of the hostile attitude.
- **Practical guidance:** Do not react with the antagonism they expect. Maintaining neutrality confounds the game; they feed off your hostility. For self-examination, deliberately approach strangers with warm thoughts — if they do not respond with hostility, the source of any hostile reactions in your life is you.

**2. The Anxious Attitude**

- **What it is:** Fear of losing control manifesting as an obsessive need to limit what can happen. Anxious types constrict the world to what they can predict and manage.
- **How it works:** They micromanage, demand rituals, dominate domestic space, and sabotage themselves when circumstances exceed their control envelope. They can disguise the need for control as love and concern.
- **Key example:** When Franklin Roosevelt contracted polio, his mother Sara tried to confine him permanently to one room, ending his political career, cloaking her controlling anxiety as maternal care. Eleanor Roosevelt had to fight her off to let Franklin rehabilitate and return to public life.
- **Practical guidance:** For self-treatment, pour energy outward into work and deliberately expose yourself to manageable amounts of chaos and uncertainty; discover the fears were exaggerated. When dealing with anxious others, radiate calmness — manner works better than words.

**3. The Avoidant Attitude**

- **What it is:** Fear of judgment and failure expressed as a systematic strategy of never fully committing to anything — always maintaining an exit, always preserving the illusion of unrealized potential.
- **How it works:** The avoidant person quits jobs at exactly the moment their limitations might become visible, breaks off relationships before real intimacy, develops convenient psychosomatic illnesses before high-stakes moments, delays until there is a ready-made excuse for mediocre results. They are equally afraid of success because it brings responsibility and the very scrutiny they dread.
- **Key example:** These types present themselves as noble idealists propagating grand ideas they will never have to enact, maintaining a "holier-than-thou" aura. The ideals are always conveniently too lofty to be tested in reality. "Look at their actions, the lack of accomplishments, the great projects they never start on."
- **Practical guidance:** For self-treatment, take on a small project and see it through to completion — if you fail, the pain will be less than feared and self-esteem rises from having tried; if you succeed, all the better. Never form partnerships with avoidant types — they will slip away at the wrong moment and leave you holding the failure.

**4. The Depressive Attitude**

- **What it is:** A deep-seated sense of unworthiness constructed in childhood as a defense mechanism — it was too frightening for the unloved child to believe their parents were flawed, so they internalized the judgment that they themselves were undeserving of love.
- **How it works:** As adults, depressive types anticipate abandonment and manufacture it by withdrawing and delivering subtle wounds to those who love them. They can attract others through their sensitive air, then push-pull them into guilt and confusion. They also sabotage their own successes because success feels undeserved and dangerous.
- **Key example:** Hans von Bülow, the German conductor, unconsciously encouraged his wife Cosima's affair with Richard Wagner, then after she left bombarded her for years with letters cataloguing his suffering and self-blame — letters that Cosima described as "a sword twisted in my heart." He then repeated the exact pattern with his second wife.
- **Practical guidance:** For self-treatment, recognize depression's temporary and cyclical nature; use solitary withdrawal productively by channeling sensitivity into creative work. Never preach about the wonderfulness of life to depressive types — go along with their gloomy view while gently drawing them into experiences that elevate mood and energy without direct appeal.

**5. The Resentful Attitude**

- **What it is:** A chronic sense of not receiving sufficient recognition or respect, transformed into vigilant scanning for disrespect and eventual cold, plotted revenge.
- **How it works:** Resentful types see everything in relation to themselves; another person's success is a personal affront. Unlike hostile types, they do not explode — they incubate, letting the sense of injustice grow until they enact subtle sabotage or passive aggression. They carry themselves with an air of arrogance, project onto others as oppressors, and tend to become leaders of the disaffected.
- **Key example:** Emperor Tiberius is the archetypal case — his tutor called him "a pitcher molded with blood and bile." Suetonius described him as always silent, head held too high, disdainful. As emperor he methodically destroyed those who had slighted him, grew increasingly isolated, eventually retreated to the island of Capri for his last eleven years, and died to city-wide celebration.
- **Practical guidance:** For self-treatment, learn to explode anger in the moment rather than stewing; recognize that most people are indifferent to you, not antagonistic. When dealing with resentful types, exercise extreme caution — the longer you are around them, the more material you provide for them to eventually turn against you. Avoid if possible.

### The Expansive (Positive) Attitude: Five Domains

Greene structures the antidote to constriction across five specific domains of life perception. Together they constitute a complete attitudinal renovation.

**How to view the world — the Explorer Mindset**

- **What it is:** Approaching life with genuine curiosity rather than certainty. The explorer holds ideas lightly, welcomes contradiction, and never stops learning.
- **Why it matters:** By age thirty most people have calcified their worldview, replacing curiosity with conviction, protecting the ego from the discomfort of being wrong. This freezes psychological growth. The explorer stance returns the person to childlike openness — before being right became more important than connecting with reality.
- **How it works:** The explorer deliberately seeks ideas that challenge existing beliefs, explores knowledge from all cultures and periods, and treats the unconscious (dreams, repressed desires, moments of tiredness) as another frontier rather than something to fear or suppress. Holding ideas with less attachment unleashes creative powers.

**How to view adversity — Obstacle as Teacher**

- **What it is:** Reframing every setback, failure, and imposed hardship as raw material for growth and expansion rather than evidence of defeat.
- **How it works:** The crucial distinction is not between people who face adversity and those who do not — everyone faces adversity. The distinction is in the response. The constricted attitude treats obstacles as threats to avoid; the expansive attitude treats them as precisely the experience needed.
- **Key examples:** Joan Crawford, assigned by a vindictive Irving Thalberg to make Westerns (a career dead-end for actresses), chose to become an expert rider, study the folklore of the Old West, and master the genre — turning the punishment into an expansion of her skills. Malcolm X, beginning an eight-to-ten year prison sentence at twenty, treated prison as the best possible school: time for deep reading, developing oratorical discipline, and building the focus he had always lacked. He emerged stronger than the person who entered.

**How to view yourself — Expansive Self-Belief**

- **What it is:** Refusing to accept the internalized limits and criticisms that accumulate over years. Believing that what you are capable of now is less than what you are genuinely capable of — and that this belief, held sincerely, creates the dynamic that makes it true.
- **Why it matters:** Conventional culture valorizes false humility and ironic detachment. Greene argues this cool posture is fear in disguise — fear of embarrassment, failure, and showing genuine emotion. It is a dead end. "Such humility is not a virtue but is rather a value that people promote to help keep you down."
- **How it works:** Great historical leaders often believed themselves descended from gods — not because this was literally true but because the self-belief produced extraordinary confidence that others fed off. Feeling destined for something significant gives resilience against opposition and stops you from internalizing doubt. This connects to Maslow's "peak experiences" — moments of transcendence that permanently expand a person's sense of their possibilities.

**How to view energy and health**

- **What it is:** Treating vitality as responsive to attitude and will rather than fixed by biology or age.
- **How it works:** Love, excitement, and genuine challenge demonstrably increase energy and accelerate recovery from illness. Depression and chronic stress demonstrably deplete immunity and accelerate aging. The mind-body system is one integrated whole. Accepting physical limits creates a self-fulfilling cycle of decline; pushing past what you think are your limits — fueled by genuine engagement with a project — reveals energy reserves that seemed unavailable. Those who age well maintain physical engagement, merely adjusting its form.

**How to view other people**

- **What it is:** Depersonalizing the actions of others so that their behavior is understood as a product of their own wounds, history, and attitudinal lens — not as a personal statement about you.
- **Why it matters:** Taking everything personally is the most reliable source of petty resentments, defensive battles, and the accumulation of bitterness that eventually calcifies into one of the constricted attitude types.
- **How it works:** When someone criticizes or acts against you, recognize that "more often than not it stems from some deep earlier pain they are reliving; you become the convenient target of frustrations and resentments that have been accumulating over the years." Treat people as facts of nature — as diverse as flowers or rocks, each playing a role in the social ecology. From this neutral stance, genuine curiosity about the other person becomes possible, as Chekhov demonstrated by analyzing his father as a character in a story rather than as a personal tormentor. This understanding produced the overwhelming rush of love and forgiveness that freed him.

### The Soul as a Metaphor for Attitudinal Depth

- **What it is:** Greene connects the modern concept of attitude to the ancient concept of the soul — the idea found across pre-modern cultures that individuals contain a spiritual force that gives depth, individuality, and vitality to their experience of life.
- **Why it matters:** The soul metaphor captures something the clinical language of attitude misses: that there is a qualitative difference in depth between people who are closed and those who are open — a difference others feel viscerally when they are in the presence of someone truly expansive.
- **How it works:** In Greene's modern reformulation, the "external spiritual force" of traditional soul-talk becomes the life force itself — the irreducible complexity, unpredictability, and diversity of existence. People who try to control and narrow this force cut themselves off from it and become "dead inside." People who embrace it — welcoming uncertainty, maintaining curiosity, feeling generous even toward enemies — fill themselves with vitality. Their soul expands to the contours of life itself.
- **Key quote or example:** "On the one side we find people whose goal in life is to inhibit and control this life force... Everything is about them and their petty needs and personal problems... All of this renders them dead inside as they close themselves off to the richness of life."
- **Connection:** This section synthesizes the entire chapter's argument into a single evaluative framework: measure people — including yourself — by the depth and expansiveness of their soul, and associate preferentially with those of the expansive variety.

### The Chekhov Paradigm: Crisis as the Gateway to Attitude Transformation

- **What it is:** Greene uses Anton Chekhov's life as a sustained case study in how radical attitude change actually happens — not through incremental self-help but through crisis-induced forced confrontation with reality.
- **Why it matters:** Most people wait for external circumstances to improve before changing their attitude. Chekhov's story demonstrates the inversion: the attitude changes first, and then the circumstances follow.
- **How it works:** Chekhov underwent three distinct crises, each of which forced a deeper level of attitudinal liberation. (1) Being abandoned at sixteen in Taganrog with no money, no family, and no support, he discovered — through necessity — that work brought dignity rather than shame, that his corner of a room was his kingdom rather than his prison, and that his father was a helpless victim of history rather than an all-powerful tyrant. The change was not in his circumstances (still trapped in a dull backwater in a corner of a room) but in his lens. (2) Thirteen years later, depressed by the pettiness of literary rivals, he voluntarily reproduced the Taganrog conditions in reverse — traveling alone and vulnerable to Sakhalin Island to interview the most hardened criminals in Russia's worst penal colony. The deliberate exposure to genuine suffering restored his sense of proportion and his generosity of spirit. (3) The terminal tuberculosis diagnosis was the final crisis. He chose to live at full tilt rather than submit to the sanatorium's diminishment — the ultimate freedom from the fear of death itself. Everyone who met him in this final period described an inexplicable radiance.
- **Key quote:** "Write about how this young man squeezes the slave out of himself drop by drop and how one fine morning he awakes to find that the blood coursing through his veins is no longer the blood of a slave but that of a real human being." (Chekhov on himself, in a letter to a friend.)
- **Connection:** Chekhov's story is the chapter's through-line and the proof-of-concept for every abstract principle Greene articulates. Each key concept in the "Keys to Human Nature" section traces back to a specific moment in the Chekhov narrative.

## Key Takeaways

1. Your attitude is not your perception of reality — it is the pre-conscious filter that constructs your personal version of reality before you are even aware of perceiving anything.
2. Negative attitudes do not just make you feel bad; they actively engineer the failures and rejections they fear, then hide from you your own role in causing them. This is the mechanism of self-sabotage.
3. There are five recognizable forms of the constricted attitude (hostile, anxious, avoidant, depressive, resentful), each with a distinct childhood origin, a distinct self-sabotaging pattern, and a specific antidote. Knowing the type clarifies both what to work on in yourself and how to handle it in others.
4. The same external event (a prison sentence, a forced career detour, an abusive childhood) can either narrow a person's attitude further or expand it enormously — the determining factor is the response, not the event.
5. False humility and ironic detachment are not virtues; they are defensive postures that prevent growth. Believing sincerely that you are capable of more than you currently produce is not delusion — it is the attitude that makes it true.
6. Taking other people's behavior personally is almost always a misattribution. Most of what people do flows from their own unresolved wounds and accumulated frustrations; you are a convenient target, not the actual cause.
7. Crisis — being stripped of comfort, routine, and support — is the most reliable catalyst for genuine attitude change because it forces direct confrontation with the gap between what you fear and what is actually true.
8. Attitude has a measurable effect on physical health, energy levels, and recovery from illness. Excitement and genuine engagement with a challenge are not merely emotional states; they are physiological ones that tap wellsprings of vitality.
9. The "soul" is Greene's term for attitudinal depth: people who are expansive, curious, and generous have more of it; people who are closed, self-absorbed, and controlling have less. This depth is felt by others and determines who is worth spending time with.
10. Chekhov's formula — "work and love" — is the practical summary of the entire chapter. External action directed outward (work) combined with genuine generosity toward other people (love) is the mechanism by which the expansive attitude is built and maintained.

## Mental Model / Framework

**The Aperture of the Attitudinal Lens**

Think of your attitude as the aperture of a camera lens. A narrow aperture lets in very little light and produces a sharp but cramped image — you see a small slice of what is actually there, and what you see is dominated by whatever is most directly in front of you (your own fears, grievances, and needs). A wide aperture lets in abundant light and captures a broad, rich image — more of the world becomes visible, including things at the periphery that a narrow lens would miss entirely.

The five constricted attitudes are five different ways of keeping the aperture nearly shut:
- The hostile attitude is permanently set to detect threats in the periphery — it magnifies dark edges.
- The anxious attitude is locked down to maintain a perfectly controlled frame — nothing unexpected can enter.
- The avoidant attitude keeps the shutter from opening all the way, ensuring no image is ever fully exposed.
- The depressive attitude is focused on shadow — it filters out light before it arrives.
- The resentful attitude is a lens aimed perpetually inward at the self as wronged center — everything else is background.

The expansive attitude is the practice of progressively widening the aperture across five domains:
- World: become an explorer, welcome unfamiliar light.
- Adversity: let the difficult image in rather than closing down.
- Self: believe the lens itself is capable of more than it has yet produced.
- Health and energy: recognize that a wider aperture is physically invigorating.
- Other people: stop reading every image as being about you.

The profound insight Greene builds toward is that you are not just the photographer passively choosing an aperture — you are also the thing being photographed. A narrow aperture does not just limit what you see; it limits what others see in you, and it narrows what actually happens to you. Widen the aperture and the world does not just appear richer — it becomes richer, because your expanded attitude creates different dynamics with every person you encounter.

## "Aha!" Moments

**1. The prison you live in has no walls — it is made entirely of attitude.**
Anton Chekhov's objective circumstances when he was abandoned in Taganrog were objectively worse after his family left than before: less money, less support, a corner of a room in a house he no longer owned, no prospect of escape. But he experienced this period as liberation. Nothing changed except the lens. This forces a genuinely disorienting realization: virtually every situation you experience as a trap, a limitation, or an inescapable reality might be the same corner of the same room — and you are the one holding the key. The walls are not external; they are attitudinal.

**2. Hostile, resentful, and anxious people are not responding to the world — they are creating it, then pretending to find it.**
The most counterintuitive aspect of the self-fulfilling dynamic is that the person with a constricted attitude is the primary author of the very circumstances they complain about. The hostile husband constructs the wife's defensiveness through his own accusations. The resentful Tiberius generates the hatred of Rome through his cold vindictiveness. They experience themselves as victims of a persecuting world; they are in fact the architects of their own persecution. This is not a moral judgment — it happens entirely unconsciously — but it completely reframes what "helping" such a person would actually require. Changing their circumstances (giving them more recognition, more love, more success) cannot solve a problem generated by the attitude itself.

**3. False humility is not modesty — it is cowardice in disguise.**
The cool, ironic, self-deprecating posture that modern culture treats as sophisticated is, by Greene's analysis, the avoidant and anxious attitudes wearing stylish clothes. Never too enthusiastic, never too ambitious, always affecting nonchalance — this is not hard-won wisdom about life's disappointments; it is the defensive crouch of someone afraid to be judged, afraid to fail visibly, afraid to want something genuinely and not get it. The expansive, boldly self-believing attitude that culture mocks as naive or grandiose is, paradoxically, the more courageous and more progressive stance. The cool posture will age poorly; the explorer's posture compounds.

## Connections to Other Chapters

**Builds on Chapter 4 (Attachment and Toxic Types):** Chapter 4 introduced the attachment schemas formed in early childhood — the ways children internalize parental dynamics and carry them into adult relationships. Chapter 12 picks this up directly, showing how those early attachment patterns become the foundations of the five constricted attitude types. The depressive attitude mirrors Chapter 4's dynamic of the person who seeks love through victimhood and subtle manipulation.

**Builds on the general pattern of unconscious forces (Chapters 1-3, 5):** The book's opening chapters established that humans are largely governed by unconscious forces they cannot directly observe — shadow selves, repressed desires, social masks. Chapter 12 extends this into the domain of attitude: just as the shadow operates without our awareness to sabotage our conscious intentions, the attitudinal lens operates without our awareness to shape every perception and interpersonal interaction. The solution in each case is the same: bringing the unconscious mechanism into consciousness.

**Sets up later chapters on dealing with people:** The five attitude type profiles — hostile, anxious, avoidant, depressive, resentful — function as a practical typology that will carry forward. When Greene addresses power dynamics, social interactions, and dealing with difficult personalities in later chapters, recognizing which attitudinal type you are dealing with determines which strategy applies. The general principle established here — maintain neutrality and do not give hostile types the fuel of your own emotional reaction — is a precursor to more detailed strategic advice.

**The soul concept as a summative framework:** The chapter's closing discussion of "soul" as attitudinal depth ties together all of the book's prior analysis of human nature. The entire project of understanding human nature — the shadow, the masks, the childhood wounds, the tribal instincts, the self-destructive patterns — can be reframed as the study of what makes a soul contract or expand. This positions the remaining chapters as further dimensions of the same core question.

## In My Own Words (ELI5)

Imagine you are wearing special glasses that you have had on since you were a little kid. The lenses on these glasses are not clear — they are tinted. If the lenses are, say, fear-colored, then everything you see looks a little threatening. You walk into a room and think "people are judging me." You make a friend and think "they'll probably turn on me eventually." You get an assignment and think "I'll probably fail, so why really try?" The tinted lenses make you behave differently — you act defensive, or you quit before finishing, or you snap at people before they can hurt you. And here is the crazy part: because you act that way, people actually do get annoyed with you, or you do fail because you didn't try, or your friend does pull away because you were cold. So now your glasses tell you: "See? I was right all along." The glasses made the thing happen, and then pretended they just saw it coming.

Robert Greene says: those glasses are your attitude. Most people do not know they are wearing them. They think they are just seeing the world clearly. But two people can be in the exact same city, with the same weather and the same people, and one person has a wonderful time and one person has a miserable time — because of their glasses, not because of the city.

The glasses started being tinted when you were a kid. Maybe you had a parent who was hard on you, and you decided the world is a scary place. Maybe you were never quite loved enough, and you decided you are not really worth loving. These childhood decisions became the tint in your lenses. You forgot you made the decision.

The good news: you can change the tint. Chekhov grew up being beaten by his father and working in a freezing shop as a little boy. He could have grown up with very dark glasses. But when he was alone for the first time at sixteen, he discovered something: when he changed how he looked at his situation — when he stopped seeing the corner of the room as a prison and started seeing it as his kingdom — his life actually got better. Not because anything outside changed. Because the glasses changed.

Greene describes five ways the glasses go really dark (hostile, anxious, avoidant, depressive, resentful — each one a different kind of tint), and then he gives you a guide to cleaning the lenses in five areas of life: how you look at the world (with curiosity, like an explorer), how you look at hard times (as teachers rather than punishments), how you look at yourself (as capable of more, not less), how you look at your body (as capable of more energy than you think), and how you look at other people (as doing their own thing, not targeting you personally).

The whole chapter comes down to this: the biggest thing you will ever build in your life is not a business or a relationship or a career — it is the attitude you look at everything through. Build it carefully, deliberately, and keep expanding it, or it will shrink on its own.
