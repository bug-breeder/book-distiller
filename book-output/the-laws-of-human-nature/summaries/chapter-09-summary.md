# Chapter 9: Become an Elusive Object of Desire (The Law of Covetousness)

## Core Thesis
Human beings are hardwired to desire what they do not have and to lose interest in what they possess — and by understanding and deliberately engineering this psychology, you can transform yourself and your work into objects of intense, self-sustaining desire.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Grass-Is-Always-Greener Syndrome
- **What it is:** A universal psychological tendency where the mind is perpetually drawn toward what it lacks and idealizes distant or unattainable things while devaluing what it currently possesses. Greene calls it the psychological equivalent of an optical illusion — get close enough to the greener grass and it turns out to be just as dry as yours.
- **Why it matters:** This is the bedrock mechanism that all strategies for creating desire rest upon. You cannot play this game without first understanding why humans are built this way.
- **How it works:** The moment we obtain something, our minds begin drifting toward something new. Distant objects are idealized because we never test them against reality. Once we do — once we get the relationship, the job, the revolution — we discover the illusion and immediately begin seeking the next horizon. The syndrome is chronic and cyclical.
- **Key quote or example:** The biblical Exodus story serves as Greene's oldest illustration. The Hebrews, freed from Egyptian slavery, immediately began romanticizing their bondage — the melons, the cucumbers, the familiar meats — the moment reality in the desert became hard. Even God's miracles could not compete with the nostalgic fantasy of a past they had already lived through and suffered under.
- **Connection:** This is the raw psychological material that the entire chapter's strategies are designed to exploit. It also links directly to the chapter's closing section on self-mastery — recognizing the syndrome in yourself is the antidote to being manipulated by it in others.

### The Three Brain Mechanisms Behind Covetousness
- **What it is:** Three deep neurological and evolutionary reasons why humans cannot stop coveting. Greene names them: induction, negative evolutionary bias, and the brain's inability to distinguish imagination from reality.
- **Why it matters:** These are not character flaws or moral failures. They are features baked into the architecture of the human mind. Understanding them explains why these strategies work so reliably across cultures and centuries.
- **How it works:**
  1. **Induction** — Just as the eye seeing red intensifies perception of its opposite (green), the mind seeing or imagining something automatically conjures its opposite. Every prohibition or "no" instantly calls up the forbidden desire. This is why banning pornography created the first pornographic industry: outlawing a thing forces the mind to dwell on it constantly.
  2. **Negative evolutionary bias** — Complacency would have been lethal on the savanna. Our ancestors survived by never fully settling into contentment, always scanning for what could go wrong. We no longer live among predators, but our brains are still wired this way, expressing as chronic restlessness, complaining, and discontent with the status quo.
  3. **Imagination as near-reality** — fMRI studies show that imagining something activates nearly identical brain circuitry as actually experiencing it. This means fantasy carries almost the emotional weight of real experience, which is why people can be moved so powerfully by things they have never touched or owned. Desire for the unattained can feel as vivid and urgent as desire for the tangible.
- **Key quote or example:** "What is real and what is imagined are both experienced similarly in the brain." This is the scientific justification for why marketing, mystique, and manufactured scarcity work — the brain cannot cleanly separate the story you tell about an object from the object itself.
- **Connection:** These three mechanisms directly underpin each of the three strategies that follow. Induction justifies the transgressive/taboo strategy; negative bias explains why withdrawal and scarcity work; imagination-as-reality explains why mystery and open-endedness are so potent.

### The Chanel Case Study: Reverse Perspective
- **What it is:** The pivotal insight Greene draws from Coco Chanel's career — that the path to becoming an object of desire requires a fundamental shift from focusing on what you want to focusing on what others are repressing and fantasizing about.
- **Why it matters:** Chanel began as a chronic coveter — always wanting what was just out of reach (aristocratic life, stage fame, the partners of other women). The transformation happened when she accidentally became the object others coveted, and then consciously reverse-engineered that effect.
- **How it works:** Chanel identified several levers: (1) she wrapped herself in mystery by never revealing her true past, manufacturing contradictory stories; (2) she associated her designs with something transgressive — the freedom and power of masculinity, the flouting of gender roles; (3) she ensured ubiquity, giving her clothes away to beautiful, well-connected women so that her look appeared everywhere and triggered competitive desire. The objects themselves — boater hats made from department-store materials, clothes cut from cheap jersey fabric, a perfume blended from ordinary jasmine and chemicals — were intrinsically unremarkable. The psychological framing did all the work.
- **Key quote or example:** "In truth, the boater hats she originally designed were nothing more than common objects anyone could buy in a department store. The clothes she first designed were made out of the cheapest materials. The perfume was a mix of ordinary flowers, such as jasmine, and chemicals, nothing exotic or special. It was pure psychological magic that transformed them into objects that stimulated such intense desires to possess them."
- **Connection:** This case study is the practical proof of concept for all three strategies that follow. Chanel used all of them simultaneously: withdrawal and mystery, competitive/social desire, and induction through transgression.

### Strategy 1 — Know How and When to Withdraw
- **What it is:** The deliberate practice of becoming less available, less legible, and less present — both psychologically (by maintaining ambiguity and emotional distance) and physically (by absenting yourself at the right moments).
- **Why it matters:** Overexposure kills desire. When people can fully read you, predict you, and take you for granted, they unconsciously begin to lose respect and interest. Scarcity raises perceived value; ubiquity lowers it.
- **How it works:** Start with a baseline "touch of coldness" — the signal that you could do without others. This is not cruelty but a refusal to show desperate need. Maintain ambiguity about your values, opinions, and inner life so that others project their own fantasies onto you (the movie-star effect). Once people are hooked, use physical absence: let days or a week pass without contact. The emptiness you leave becomes occupied by their imagination, which always runs hotter than reality. They come to want more, not less.
- **Key quote or example:** Michael Jackson managed his public presence with extreme precision — spreading album releases years apart, controlling interviews, neither confirming nor denying leaked rumors (hyperbaric chambers for eternal youth). He made himself an object of perpetual mystery and desire by refusing to over-define himself. Greene calls this "strategic elusiveness."
- **Connection:** Withdrawal is also the antidote to the chapter's closing warning: once someone possesses you or your work, respect decays. Continuous withdrawal, surprise, and renewal of the chase maintains power over time.

### Strategy 2 — Create Rivalries of Desire
- **What it is:** Leveraging the social, competitive nature of human desire. We rarely want things in isolation — we want what others want, and the fact that others want something is itself evidence of value.
- **Why it matters:** Desire is not private; it is contagious. The object desired by many becomes more desirable. The person desired by others becomes more attractive. This dynamic has roots in early childhood competition for parental attention, and it never leaves us.
- **How it works:** Create the impression — or the reality — that others desire you or your work. Encourage widespread visibility, even piracy, as Chanel deliberately did with her fashion designs. Feed rumors through media. Get tastemakers to validate. Bring in competing parties in any negotiation to create a bidding dynamic. As an individual, let others observe the attention you receive from third parties — never brag, but let it be visible. Controversy and negative press activate the same mechanism: people are drawn toward the negative and toward that which is being talked about.
- **Key quote or example:** "If we saw our siblings or friends receive something — a gift or a favor — it sparked a competitive desire to have the same thing. If some object or person was not desired by others, we tended to see it as something indifferent or distasteful — there must be something wrong with it."
- **Connection:** This strategy is the social amplifier for the individual mystery created by Strategy 1. Withdrawal makes you scarce; visible social desire proves the scarcity is real and worth competing over.

### Strategy 3 — Use Induction (The Transgressive and Forbidden)
- **What it is:** Associating your object with something slightly illicit, transgressive, forbidden, or politically charged — exploiting the brain's automatic tendency to desire whatever it is told it cannot have.
- **Why it matters:** We live in an era of more regulation, surveillance, and social constraint than ever before. People are secretly chafing under these constraints and hungry for something that acknowledges or defies them. The forbidden is the most reliably covetous category.
- **How it works:** You do not need to be genuinely dangerous or scandalous — the association with transgression is enough. Chanel linked her clothes to gender-role violations. JFK positioned himself as the antidote to Eisenhower-era conformity: voting for him meant youth, vigor, and a tacit rebellion against the father figure. You can also tap into the universal voyeuristic desire — the sense that you are revealing secrets that should not be shared, giving people access to what happens behind closed doors. Finally, offer the fantasy of conquering life's hardest limits: recovering youth, achieving success without sacrifice, becoming someone new, even defeating death. Because we can imagine these things (the brain's imagination-as-reality flaw), the desire for them feels urgent and real even when they are impossible.
- **Key quote or example:** "Every no sparks a corresponding yes. (It was the outlawing of pornography in Victorian times that created the first pornographic industry.)"
- **Connection:** Induction is the deepest of the three strategies because it operates at the neurological level. It works synergistically with withdrawal (which makes you forbidden) and social desire (which makes others' prohibition of you more visible).

### The Self-Mastery Counterweight: Embracing Your Circumstances
- **What it is:** The chapter's closing argument that the same syndrome you are learning to exploit in others is a trap you must consciously resist in yourself. Chronic covetousness in your own life leads to wasted time, serial restlessness, and an inability to build anything lasting.
- **Why it matters:** Greene is careful not to write a chapter that simply teaches manipulation. The law has a self-directed dimension: if you cannot see this pattern in yourself, you will be the one perpetually manipulated by others who understand it.
- **How it works:** You must distinguish between productive restlessness (which motivates genuine growth and innovation) and chronic discontent (which is just boredom and self-absorption cycling endlessly). In relationships, accept that no person is perfect and invest in depth over novelty. In career decisions, make targeted adjustments rather than wholesale reinventions driven by vague dissatisfaction. Cultivate deeper connection to your actual environment — the people around you, the place you live, the history embedded there — rather than always chasing the distant and exotic. The deepest form of desire worth cultivating is the desire for a closer relationship with reality itself.
- **Key quote or example:** "In the end what you really must covet is a deeper relationship to reality, which will bring you calmness, focus, and practical powers to alter what it is possible to alter."
- **Connection:** This section mirrors Chapter 4's argument about being locked in character — you cannot escape yourself, so instead of running toward fantasy, run deeper into who and where you actually are. It also bookends the Chanel story: she was herself a chronic coveter before she mastered the art of making others covet. The law cuts both ways.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Desire is not triggered by quality — it is triggered by psychology.** Chanel's hats were cheap department-store items. Her perfume was ordinary jasmine and chemicals. What made them objects of intense desire was the mystery, transgression, and social proof she wrapped them in.

2. **Overexposure is the single greatest killer of desire.** The more readable, available, and predictable you become, the faster interest fades. Strategic scarcity — of access, information, and presence — is the prerequisite for being coveted.

3. **Prohibition is the most reliable desire-generator in human psychology.** The brain cannot hear "no" without also hearing the whispered "yes." Every ban, restriction, or social taboo automatically creates desire for the forbidden thing.

4. **Desire is social, not private.** Nobody wants something in a vacuum. The fact that others desire something is itself the most powerful signal of value. Create visible social proof — or at least the credible impression of it.

5. **The pleasure of desire lives in the pursuit, not the possession.** Once obtained, almost every coveted object disappoints. The practical implication: never fully give yourself or your work away. Maintain the chase by continually withdrawing, surprising, and renewing the game.

6. **Mystery is not dishonesty — it is the oxygen that desire breathes.** Leaving room for people's imaginations to fill in blanks produces deeper engagement than any amount of transparency. Over-explaining, over-sharing, and over-clarifying kills the imaginative pleasure that sustains desire.

7. **The brain cannot cleanly distinguish imagination from reality.** This is why stories, mystique, and manufactured fantasies about an object can produce desire just as intense as actual experience with the object. You are not selling a product; you are selling an imagined experience.

8. **Recognize chronic covetousness in yourself as a source of wasted energy, not ambition.** Distinguish between genuine goals and vague restlessness born of the grass-is-greener illusion. Most people making big life changes are escaping boredom, not pursuing clarity.

9. **The most powerful positioning is transgressive but not dangerous.** You want the slight edge of rule-breaking, gender-crossing, generational-boundary-defying — enough to activate induction, not enough to genuinely alienate. Chanel was not actually masculine; she was feminine with a masculine edge.

10. **Going deeper into your reality is the antidote to being enslaved by this law in your own life.** The exotic you chase is almost always a projection. What you already have — your relationships, your environment, your work — contains more depth and fascination than the imagined horizon ever will.

## Mental Model / Framework

**The Desire Engine: A Three-Stage Model**

Think of human desire as an engine that needs three components to run:

**Stage 1 — FUEL: Mystery and Scarcity**
You cannot desire what you fully understand and freely possess. The engine needs something to reach for. This is created by deliberate ambiguity (who are you, really?), physical absence (you are not always there), and open-ended messages (what does this mean?). Without fuel, the engine stalls.

**Stage 2 — IGNITION: Transgression and Induction**
The fuel needs a spark. That spark is the sense that what you are reaching for is slightly forbidden, unconventional, or ahead of its time. The brain's induction mechanism ensures that any boundary or prohibition automatically generates desire for what lies on the other side of it. This is the activation energy.

**Stage 3 — ACCELERATION: Social Proof and Rivalry**
A running engine in isolation eventually slows. Social desire — the visible fact that others are also chasing this object — pours accelerant on the fire. Competitive desire is exponentially more powerful than solitary desire. The goal is to trigger a self-sustaining chain reaction where each new person's desire draws in the next.

**The Kill Switch: Possession and Over-Exposure**
The engine shuts off the moment the object is fully obtained, fully explained, or fully saturated into the market. This is why the game must be continuously renewed. Withdraw before they fully possess you. Surprise before they fully predict you. Evolve before they fully categorize you.

**Before vs. After:**
Before understanding this law — you try to attract people by being maximally open, available, and honest; you wonder why interest fades.
After understanding this law — you see that availability and transparency are the enemies of desire, and you build your presence and your work around managed mystery, strategic absence, and visible social proof.

## "Aha!" Moments

1. **Honesty and self-exposure are not virtues in the domain of desire — they are laziness dressed up as ethics.** Greene makes an uncomfortable but precise argument: when people say they are "just being authentic" by sharing everything about themselves constantly, they are not practicing a deep moral commitment to truth. They are taking the path of least resistance (it requires no effort to simply be oneself) and then complaining that others lose interest. The effort required to maintain mystery, to see yourself from the outside, to think about what others need rather than what you want to share — that is the genuine discipline. "Do not swallow the easy moralism of the day, which urges honesty at the expense of desirability."

2. **The thing you desire is almost never as good as the not-yet-having of it.** The chapter's Beaumarchais epigraph nails the paradox: "Pleasure isn't in the fulfillment, but in the pursuit." This reframes the disappointment we feel after getting what we want not as a defect of the specific thing we wanted, but as a structural feature of how human desire works. You were never really chasing the object — you were chasing the state of chasing it. This explains why achievement so frequently triggers depression, why relationships cool after the conquest phase, and why possessing something great often feels less exciting than wanting it did.

3. **The deepest relationship you can cultivate is with your own reality — not the fantasy on the other side of the fence.** Most self-help tells you to dream bigger, want more, reach further. Greene inverts this: the chronic coveter who is always dreaming of the exotic place, the better partner, the different career, is actually running from themselves and will never arrive anywhere. The alternative he proposes — going deeper into the people, places, and work already around you — sounds modest but is profoundly reorienting. Reality, genuinely explored, is stranger and richer than any imagined alternative. This is the chapter's most quietly radical idea.

## Connections to Other Chapters

**Builds on Chapter 2 (Self-Absorption/Narcissism):** Greene explicitly cross-references this earlier chapter when he notes that increasing self-absorption makes it harder for people to reverse their perspective and think about what others need. The failure to create desire is often just an inability to exit one's own head — a symptom of the narcissistic dynamic analyzed earlier.

**Builds on Chapter 4 (Character/Fate):** Greene cross-references Chapter 4's argument that we are locked inside our own character and cannot become someone else. This feeds the induction strategy (offer the fantasy of self-transformation) and also grounds the self-mastery section (accept your character and environment rather than fantasizing about escape).

**Sets up later chapters on persuasion, seduction, and influence:** The mechanics established here — scarcity, social proof, transgression, mystery — are foundational to any later discussion of how to move people. This chapter provides the psychological infrastructure (desire) that later chapters will build tactics upon.

**Mirrors the chapter on the law of grandiosity:** Chronic covetousness and grandiose self-belief share the same root: an unwillingness to accept limits, a fantasy life that overrides engagement with reality. The antidote in both cases is deeper investment in actual circumstances.

## In My Own Words (ELI5)

Imagine you have a toy. While you don't have it, you really, really want it — you think about it all day, you dream about owning it. Then your birthday comes and you get it. You play with it for a week. Then it just sits on the shelf and you've already started thinking about the next toy.

That's just how human brains work. We always want what we don't have, and once we have it, it stops being as exciting. Scientists have actually looked at brain scans and confirmed this — imagining something fires almost the same brain circuits as actually doing it. So in a way, the wanting feels almost as good as the having, and the having almost always disappoints compared to the wanting.

Robert Greene says: once you understand this, you can use it.

Here's how smart people create desire:

**Make yourself a little mysterious.** Don't explain everything about yourself. Don't be available all the time. The less people can fully figure you out, the more their imagination fills in the gaps — and their imagination will always make you more interesting than reality.

**Make people see that others want you.** We copy each other's desires. If a kid in class has a toy and everyone wants it, you want it too — even if you didn't care about it before. If you can get people to see that others are chasing you or your work, they automatically want to chase it too.

**Be a little bit forbidden or rule-breaking.** The brain is wired so that when you're told you can't have something, you want it more. If you can link yourself or your work to something slightly edgy, unconventional, or ahead of the times, people's minds automatically pull toward you.

Coco Chanel was the master of this. She made clothes out of the cheapest fabric and hats out of materials anyone could buy — but she made herself mysterious, she got the coolest women in France to wear her stuff everywhere, and her clothes had this slightly rebellious, gender-bending edge. Suddenly everyone was desperate to have them.

The flip side? Greene says you need to watch for this trap in your own life. We waste so much time always dreaming about something better — a better job, a better partner, a more exciting life somewhere else. But the moment we get those things, we're already looking for the next thing. He says the real goal is to stop running away from your actual life and go deeper into it instead. The people around you, the place you live, the work you do — there's more depth and richness there than in any fantasy on the horizon.
