# Chapter 19: Make Them Want to Follow You (The Law of Fickleness)

## Core Thesis
People's loyalty to leaders is inherently ambivalent and volatile — they simultaneously want to be led and to resist authority — and the only durable antidote is to deliberately earn genuine authority through vision, empathy, and selfless service to the group, rather than expecting deference as an entitlement of rank.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Entitlement Curse
- **What it is:** The unconscious assumption that respect, trust, and loyalty are owed to you by virtue of your position, accomplishments, or identity — rather than earned continuously through your actions.
- **Why it matters:** Greene argues this is the single most corrosive force in leadership. It breeds passivity, insensitivity, and an inflated self-image that slowly bleeds away the very loyalty a leader takes for granted.
- **How it works:** Entitlement operates as a feedback loop. The leader stops straining to earn trust; the group senses this and begins to disengage. The leader, seeing only surface smiles and nods, misreads the room. Resentment accumulates invisibly. Then, at the first sign of weakness or mistake, it erupts with a suddenness that shocks the leader who never saw it coming. Elizabeth's Essex embodies this arc perfectly — he was gifted, brave, popular, and utterly destroyed by his sense that the queen owed him power and forgiveness.
- **Key quote or example:** Greene on the modern version: "We feel entitled to respect for our work, no matter how little we have actually accomplished. We feel people should take our ideas and projects seriously, no matter how little thought went into them or how meager our track record." Henry VIII and Mary Queen of Scots are cited as historical examples of entitlement-blinded rulers who made catastrophically selfish decisions.
- **Connection:** Links directly to the Law of Self-Sabotage (the Shadow) and to the book's recurring theme of self-absorption as the root of most human failure.

### The Fundamental Ambivalence Toward Leaders
- **What it is:** A deep, universal, and mostly unconscious tension humans feel toward authority figures. We simultaneously crave strong leadership and resent the dependence and inferiority that comes with it.
- **Why it matters:** Most leaders operate in complete ignorance of this dynamic. They see only applause and compliance. But beneath every cheerful employee and loyal follower is a current of envy, suspicion, and readiness to turn — not because of personal animus, but because of human nature itself.
- **How it works:** Greene traces this back to childhood: even loving parents generated in us both love and resentment of dependence. We needed them and simultaneously wanted autonomy. That same emotional template gets projected onto every authority figure thereafter. Leaders are unconsciously cast as parental figures, inheriting all of that old ambivalence. When leaders show weakness, incompetence, or selfishness — any of these tips the ambivalence toward the negative — and the previously invisible hostility surfaces with full force.
- **Key quote or example:** "Look at the news to see how quickly leaders in any field can lose support and respect, and how quickly they are judged by their latest success or failure." Greene notes that even ancient chiefs were ritually executed when they showed signs of aging or when a drought struck — the fickleness predates democracy entirely.
- **Connection:** Builds on earlier chapters about the unconscious and emotional contagion. This chapter shows how ambivalence is the specific mechanism by which groups turn on leaders.

### Authority vs. Power (The Roman Model)
- **What it is:** A crucial distinction between authority — the earned, augmented presence that makes people willingly follow you — and raw power, which is compliance extracted through force, rewards, punishments, or rhetoric.
- **Why it matters:** The less force, bribery, and speechifying you need to get people to follow you, the more true authority you possess. Raw power is expensive, brittle, and generates hidden resentment. Authority is self-reinforcing and resilient.
- **How it works:** The word comes from the Latin *auctoritas*, meaning "to increase or augment." The Romans applied it to leaders whose every word and action carried extra weight — not because of their rank but because they had demonstrated an alignment with the highest ideals of the republic. People followed them because they trusted their judgment. Greene's modern examples: Lincoln, MLK, Warren Buffett, Angela Merkel, Steve Jobs.
- **Key quote or example:** Xenophon leading ten thousand stranded Greek soldiers out of Persian territory in 401 BC — he had zero military rank or experience, yet his comprehensive strategic vision was so complete and confidently communicated that the soldiers chose him as their leader. Rational, elevated thinking — not position — was the source of his authority.
- **Connection:** This is the chapter's central conceptual upgrade. All the strategies that follow are ways to build *auctoritas*, not mere compliance.

### The Elizabeth I Model: Turning Weakness Into Authority
- **What it is:** A detailed historical case study of Queen Elizabeth I as the supreme practitioner of earned authority. She began with every possible disadvantage — young, female, politically inexperienced, ruling a bankrupt country — and built an authority that ultimately humiliated Philip II of Spain and unified England.
- **Why it matters:** It shows that authority is not a byproduct of favorable circumstances. It is a deliberate practice that can overcome almost any structural disadvantage.
- **How it works:** Elizabeth's strategy rested on several pillars: (1) She rejected entitlement entirely, understanding her actual weakness. (2) She worked harder than her ministers, mastered the details of governance, and held herself to higher standards than anyone around her. (3) She demonstrated that her primary loyalty was to England, not her own ego or personal feelings. (4) She actively built emotional bonds with both commoners (the coronation procession, clutching the rosemary sprig) and ministers (empathy alternating with disciplined toughness). (5) She turned her ministers' desire to please her into an engine of national productivity. (6) She deployed strategic patience — her endless marriage negotiations were deliberate geopolitical instruments, not indecision.
- **Key quote or example:** Pope Sixtus V, who had excommunicated her and backed the armada, said after Spain's catastrophic defeat: "Just look how well she governs! She is only a woman, only mistress of half an island, and yet she makes herself feared by Spain, by France, by the Empire, by all!"
- **Connection:** Elizabeth's handling of the Essex rebellion is equally instructive as a negative case: she had to destroy someone she cared for the moment he proved ungovernable, because tolerating him threatened the group's welfare. This is the "merciless with saboteurs" principle in practice.

### The Six Strategies for Establishing Authority

#### 1. Find Your Authority Style (Authenticity)
- **What it is:** Your authority must emerge from your genuine character, not from imitating someone else's style. Greene identifies archetypes: the Deliverer (Moses, MLK), the Founder (entrepreneurs, innovators), the Visionary Artist (Picasso, Coltrane), the Truth Seeker, the Quiet Pragmatist, the Healer, the Teacher.
- **How it works:** When your style feels inevitable — as if it springs from your DNA rather than tactical choice — it reads as authentic. People cannot dismiss it as opportunistic. The earlier you identify and develop your natural archetype, the more convincing and powerful your authority becomes over time.

#### 2. Focus Outwardly (The Attitude)
- **What it is:** A radical reorientation away from self-absorption toward genuine attentiveness to others. Three components: (1) honing listening skills and reading nonverbal cues; (2) actively earning respect rather than expecting it; (3) treating leadership as a responsibility, not a platform for attention.
- **How it works:** This outward focus is rare enough that it makes you stand out. People feel genuinely seen and understood. That emotional connection is what opens them to your influence. Paradoxically, you gain more attention by not craving it.

#### 3. Cultivate the Third Eye (The Vision)
- **What it is:** The ability to elevate your perspective above the immediate emotional turbulence of events and perceive the larger pattern — trends, consequences, the shape of things to come.
- **How it works:** You train yourself to disconnect from the group's panic and emotion. You force yourself to see the global picture, entertain the enemy's perspective, game out multiple futures, and think thoroughly about how things could go wrong. This is a skill developed through practice, not innate talent. Napoleon appeared to read opponents' minds — he merely thought further ahead. Goethe seemed to predict trends — he had studied more deeply. Once you have a vision, you work backward to the present to create a flexible implementation plan. Confidence in your vision is itself infectious and persuasive.

#### 4. Lead from the Front (The Tone)
- **What it is:** Setting the highest personal standards and being visibly the hardest worker, the first to sacrifice, and the most accountable for mistakes.
- **How it works:** When the leader embodies the values they preach, the group internalizes those values and lifts itself to meet that standard. This is far more powerful than speeches, incentives, or threats. First impressions are critical — if you start soft, you signal you are a pushover and people will maneuver around you mercilessly. Initial toughness preserves space for warmth later; the reverse does not work.

#### 5. Stir Conflicting Emotions (The Aura)
- **What it is:** A deliberate cultivation of paradox and mystery in your persona. Rather than presenting one consistent, predictable face, you show qualities that are subtly contradictory — kind but with an undertone of intolerance for certain behaviors; prudent but capable of sudden audacity; folksy but regal.
- **How it works:** The paradox forces people to pause their instant categorizations and think more about who you really are. The more they think about you, the larger your presence. Greene cites Elizabeth (folksy + regal), MLK (spiritual + earthy pragmatism), and parents (love + clear limits) as models. Equally important: balance presence with absence. Too much familiarity makes you ordinary. Too much absence makes you unrelatable. Lean slightly toward absence — when you appear, you generate excitement. In an age of social media oversharing, strategic withdrawal is a profound competitive advantage.

#### 6. Never Appear to Take, Always to Give (The Taboo)
- **What it is:** Protecting people's sense of security about what they already possess — money, rights, time, promises you have made.
- **How it works:** Even the hint of taking away what people assumed was theirs triggers existential questions about your legitimacy: "What more will you take? Have you been fooling us?" Even symbolic sacrifices must be real and visible. Equally damaging: overpromising. People have excellent memories for broken promises, and each failure compounds. Say less, deliver more — that is the formula for authority through giving.

#### 7. Rejuvenate Your Authority (Adaptability)
- **What it is:** Actively fighting the natural drift toward rigidity, entitlement, and irrelevance that comes with age and established success.
- **How it works:** Two steps. First, maintain the visceral empathy for people's moods that you cultivated at the start — losing this sensitivity is the first sign of decay. Second, seek new audiences and markets that force you to adapt your style. Picasso, Hitchcock, and Chanel demonstrate that flexibility in artists past fifty creates the impression of a spirit that remains alive and renewing itself. Adaptability in later career signals divinity — the immortal quality of someone still growing.

### The Inner Authority
- **What it is:** A personal code of conduct derived from the higher self — the voice of conscience, discipline, and purpose that must be cultivated and listened to daily as a counterweight to the lower self's pull toward indolence, distraction, and self-absorption.
- **Why it matters:** External authority over others is only sustainable if grounded in internal authority over yourself. Leaders who cannot govern their own impulses, maintain their own standards, and answer to their own conscience will eventually expose their hollowness.
- **How it works:** The inner authority operates through four directives: (1) You have a responsibility to contribute to your culture and times, not merely consume what others have built. (2) You must cultivate what is genuinely unique about you — judge for yourself, develop your distinctive gifts, resist external definitions of identity. (3) In a world of infinite distractions, you must ruthlessly focus and prioritize, avoiding low-value people and activities. (4) You must hold your work to the highest possible standards, treating first drafts as incomplete, never allowing attachment to initial ideas, and operating with urgency because your time is limited. Leonardo da Vinci's motto — *ostinato rigore*, "relentless rigor" — is the model.
- **Key quote or example:** "You no longer need the comfort and guidance of a parent or leader. You have become your own mother and father, your own leader, truly independent and operating according to your inner authority."
- **Connection:** This section closes the loop on the entire chapter: you cannot build genuine authority over others without first having built genuine authority over yourself. The two are the same practice, pointed in different directions.

## Key Takeaways

1. Human ambivalence toward leaders is hardwired and universal — it is not a modern phenomenon, not a sign of ingratitude, and not something that can be eliminated. It can only be managed by earning genuine trust.
2. Entitlement is the single most corrosive force in leadership. The moment you stop actively earning loyalty, you have begun losing it — you just cannot see it yet.
3. Authority is not the same as power. Power compels. Authority persuades without needing to. The less force, money, and rhetoric you need, the more true authority you possess.
4. Elizabeth I's greatest insight was to turn her weakness (no entitlement, no credibility, no precedent) into a strategy: she would simply work harder, know more, sacrifice more, and care more about England than any of her ministers or rivals.
5. Vision is the core of authority. Leaders who elevate their thinking above the emotional noise of the moment and see the larger picture tap into something that feels almost divine to those still trapped in the present. This skill can be deliberately trained.
6. The aura of authority requires managed paradox — showing qualities that are slightly contradictory keeps people thinking about you. Predictability breeds contempt. Mystery breeds fascination.
7. Presence and absence must be balanced. Strategic withdrawal makes your appearances powerful. Constant availability makes you ordinary. In the age of social media oversharing, the leader who withholds some of themselves stands out.
8. Never take from people what they believed was already theirs. This is the one act that most rapidly destroys accumulated authority and triggers existential suspicion.
9. Inner authority must precede outer authority. Leonardo da Vinci's relentless rigor — holding himself to the highest standards with no external pressure — is the template for both personal excellence and credible leadership.
10. The anti-authority bias of democratic culture — consensus leadership, the minimal leader, parents as friends, teachers as entertainers — is itself a fear of responsibility disguised as principle, and it produces fake authority figures (the strongman, the panderer, the chummy leader) who are far more dangerous.

## Mental Model / Framework

Think of authority as a bank account. Every action that demonstrates vision, sacrifice, empathy, and higher-purpose orientation makes a deposit. Every act of entitlement, self-interest, inconsistency, or broken promise makes a withdrawal. People's visible behavior toward you (smiles, compliance, applause) reflects the balance they show you — not the actual balance. The actual balance is hidden, accumulating in silence.

Most leaders think the account is full because they see smiles. They do not realize that for years, every missed deposit and unnoticed withdrawal has been quietly draining the account. Then, at the first real crisis, they discover the account is empty — and the sudden collapse feels inexplicable.

Elizabeth kept making deposits for decades: working harder than her ministers, reducing court expenses, learning languages to handle diplomacy directly, suffering personally to execute Mary for England's sake, and resisting the temptation to press her advantage after defeating the armada. By the time Essex tried his coup, the account was so full that Londoners simply stayed home and ignored him.

The framework has three phases:
- **Phase 1 (Build):** Aggressive depositing. No entitlement. Highest standards. Outward focus. Visible sacrifice. Clear vision communicated confidently.
- **Phase 2 (Maintain):** Active vigilance against the drift toward entitlement that success naturally produces. Sustained empathy. Adaptability. Continued earning.
- **Phase 3 (Renew):** When age or changed circumstances erode relevance, seek new audiences and challenges that force genuine adaptation — not cosmetic change.

## "Aha!" Moments

1. **People never actually show you their real feelings toward you as a leader.** Everyone in a position of dependence shows deference — not because they respect you but because their fate is in your hands and they cannot afford to show you the truth. The smiles and nods you receive as a leader are almost always performance, not reality. This means that by the time the ambivalence surfaces openly, it has been building for a very long time, and you had no warning signs because you were misreading the data entirely.

2. **Entitlement is a form of theft.** When you expect others to give you trust and respect without earning it, you are implicitly negating their autonomy — their right to decide for themselves whether you are worthy. People feel this as an affront to their dignity. The resentment entitlement generates is not irrational oversensitivity; it is a reasonable response to having one's judgment pre-emptively overruled. This reframes entitlement not as merely ineffective but as a kind of invisible aggression.

3. **The democratic rejection of authority is itself a dangerous form of immaturity.** Greene's argument that progressive anti-authority culture — minimal leaders, parents as friends, consensus over vision — is actually a fear of responsibility in disguise is deeply counterintuitive in the current cultural moment. His point is that without genuine authority figures, we do not achieve freedom from authority; we become vulnerable to far cruder and more dangerous fake authority figures (strongmen, panders, social media mobs) who offer the illusion of direction without the substance.

## In My Own Words (ELI5)

Imagine you are on a class trip and the teacher gets sick. Suddenly there is no adult in charge. Two students try to step up. One of them starts immediately telling everyone what to do, acts annoyed when people ask questions, takes the best seat, and acts like it is obvious that everyone should listen to him because he is the loudest. The other student gathers everyone together, calmly explains the situation, asks what people are worried about, comes up with a plan, and is clearly thinking about how to get everyone home safely — not about making herself look cool.

Who do people actually follow? The second one. Not because she has some official title, but because she has *earned* it in real time.

That is what this chapter is about. Robert Greene calls it "authority" — the kind of power where people actually want to follow you, not because they have to but because they trust your judgment.

Here is the twist: everyone secretly resents leaders a little bit, even the good ones. It is like how you love your parents but also sometimes want to do the opposite of what they say just to prove you can. That tension never fully goes away. Good leaders know this and keep earning trust. Bad leaders forget it and start assuming people will always be loyal.

Elizabeth I was a queen in the 1500s who had everything working against her — she was young, a woman (which was considered disqualifying at the time), had no experience, and her country was broke and divided. But instead of sitting back and acting royal, she worked harder than anyone, learned everything, sacrificed her own income, and always made decisions based on what was good for England, not what was good for Elizabeth. Over time, her people trusted her so deeply that when Spain sent the biggest navy in the world to invade England, the English fought as if their lives depended on it — because they believed in her.

The opposite example is Essex — a popular, charming nobleman who kept expecting the queen to give him more power just because he was popular and charming. When she set limits, he threw tantrums. When she refused to bend, he literally tried to march an army to her house. Nobody followed him. He ended up beheaded, confessing in his last hours that she was right all along.

The lesson: nobody owes you their respect. You have to earn it every day, by showing that you care more about the group than about yourself, by having a clear vision of where things are going, and by holding yourself to higher standards than anyone else. Do that consistently, and people will follow you even through hard times. Assume they owe it to you, and one day you will be shocked by how fast they turn.
