# Practice Exercises: Chapter 11 — Soften People's Resistance by Confirming Their Self-opinion (The Law of Defensiveness)

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Greene argues that people's self-opinion rests on three near-universal pillars. What are they, and why does violating even one of them cause resistance to harden rather than soften?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The three universals are: "I am autonomous and acting of my own free will," "I am intelligent in my own way," and "I am basically good and decent." Each pillar functions as a psychological load-bearing wall — challenge any one of them and the person's entire sense of identity feels under threat. Because the ego cannot afford to acknowledge the challenge directly (that would mean admitting weakness), it converts the threat into stubbornness and counter-argument. The person stops evaluating your ideas on their merits and starts defending their identity instead. Violating all three at once — as in a heated argument where you imply someone is wrong, foolish, and acting badly — produces nearly impenetrable resistance that no amount of logic can dissolve.

</details>

---

**Q2:** Greene distinguishes three outcomes when you try to convince someone of something. What are they, and which does he argue you should deliberately engineer — and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The three outcomes are: (1) you inadvertently challenge the person's self-opinion, making walls go up that never come down; (2) you leave their self-opinion in a neutral, unchallenged state, producing dubious but workable resistance; or (3) you actively confirm their self-opinion, which lowers defenses completely and opens the mind to suggestion. Greene argues you must deliberately engineer the third outcome because the second is merely damage limitation — people remain resistant and need to be moved by pure rational argument, which rarely works on its own. The third outcome replicates the psychological state of being drunk with friends, swept up in a rally, or in love: the person feels accepted, directs attention outward rather than inward, and becomes genuinely susceptible to ideas they would otherwise reject.

</details>

---

**Q3:** Lyndon Johnson's rise in the Senate is the chapter's central case study. Greene's core interpretive claim is that Johnson's power came from reversing the normal logic of influence. What is that normal logic, and what did Johnson do instead?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The normal logic of influence is to put attention on yourself: display your accomplishments, express your ideas forcefully, ask for what you want, and project confidence. This strategy backfires because it makes others turn inward in self-defense — their focus narrows to their own interests rather than yours. Johnson reversed every element: he suppressed his ambition and adopted the posture of an eager student; he let others do most of the talking; he made each senator feel their wisdom was uniquely valuable; he gave favors before asking for any; and when he did want something he insinuated rather than requested. By making others feel like the star of the show, he lowered their defenses completely. By the time people realized he had accumulated near-total control of Senate Democrats, it was too late.

</details>

---

**Q4:** Greene describes a technique he calls "mental judo" for dealing with particularly resistant or stubborn people. What is the underlying mechanism, and what are the three ways he shows it being applied?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The underlying mechanism mirrors judo itself: rather than meeting resistance with counter-force (which intensifies the resistance), you agree with or amplify the resistant person's direction, then redirect their own energy toward the outcome you wanted. The three applications are: (1) Use their emotions — as with the suspended teenager who was told that doing his homework poorly would prove the principal right; his desire for revenge energized exactly the behavior the therapist wanted. (2) Use their language — as Erickson did with the husband seeking a "friendly divorce," working entirely within the husband's framing to engineer a situation that made the marriage work. (3) Use their rigidity — as Hakuin did with the miser pawnbroker, turning religious chanting into a business transaction that exploited the man's love of money, until the chanting itself converted him. In each case, the person's own force defeats their original resistance.

</details>

---

**Q5:** Greene includes a section on developing a flexible mind as a "self-strategy." Why does he treat your own defensiveness as part of the same problem as others' defensiveness, and what specific intellectual practices does he recommend?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Greene's point is that the same mechanism — self-opinion defending itself against challenge — operates in you just as it does in everyone you are trying to influence. As we age our minds tighten around our accumulated beliefs; a growing sense of superiority closes us to new influences, limiting creative and intellectual growth. To counter this, Greene recommends three practices drawn from history: first, adopt Socrates's key insight — that awareness of your own ignorance is the beginning of real wisdom, which means regularly questioning your assumptions rather than defending them. Second, adopt Nietzsche's technique of deliberately reading or engaging with ideas you find inimical with maximum charity, suspending the critical faculty long enough to find the "soul of the thing" before evaluating it. Third, cultivate ironic distance from your own self-opinion — acknowledge that you are not as autonomous, as intelligent, or as purely motivated as you believe, which removes the compulsive need for external validation and creates genuine mental independence.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: Overriding a Colleague's Entrenched Position**
You are leading a project team. One senior colleague, Marcus, has been doing a particular workflow process the same way for eight years and is openly hostile to the new system you want to introduce. Every meeting where you explain the system's benefits, Marcus raises objections and the team follows his lead. Your deadline to adopt the new system is six weeks away.

*What should you consider?*
- Which of Marcus's three self-opinion pillars (autonomy, intelligence, goodness) is most threatened by a top-down rollout of new software, and how can you restructure your approach to confirm rather than challenge it?
- How could you use the "deep listener" strategy in your next one-on-one with Marcus — specifically, what would you do differently compared to your current behavior in meetings?
- Is there a version of the "use their rigidity" judo move available to you — a way to position the old workflow and the new system so that Marcus's resistance redirects itself toward adoption?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Marcus's primary threatened pillar is autonomy: the new system is being handed down rather than chosen. His intelligence pillar may also be at stake — eight years of mastery in the old workflow is a form of expertise, and the new system implicitly devalues it. Start by abandoning the meeting format entirely. Request a private conversation and spend the first twenty minutes listening. Ask Marcus to walk you through the current workflow from his perspective, what it does well, what problems he has solved with it over the years. Reflect his insights back to him in your own words. This does two things simultaneously: it confirms his intelligence and expertise, and it gives you detailed knowledge of the specific friction points in the new system that he is actually worried about.

From there, frame the adoption not as a replacement but as an extension of what he already knows. Ask for his advice on how the rollout should be structured — which team members would need the most support, what the biggest risks are. When his ideas are incorporated into the plan, he has chosen the system on some level. If he remains resistant, try a version of the judo move: tell him you have heard his concerns and that perhaps it makes sense to run both systems in parallel for the first two weeks so the team can compare them properly. This removes the threat of forced change (autonomy restored) and creates conditions where the new system's advantages can demonstrate themselves on a level playing field — which is likely what you wanted all along.

</details>

**Scenario 2: Winning Over a Gatekeeping Superior**
You want a promotion and the decision rests largely with a department director, Patricia, whom you rarely interact with. She is known to value experience and has passed over younger candidates before. You have a performance review scheduled in three weeks.

*What should you consider?*
- How does the sequencing of your actions matter here — what should happen before the performance review, not during it?
- What is the difference between flattering Patricia on her stated strengths versus the qualities she may be privately uncertain about, and which is more effective according to Greene?
- How can you give Patricia small favors that create a cycle of reciprocity without making the strategic purpose visible?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The worst mistake is to walk into a performance review and present a case for yourself. That puts all focus on your own agenda, triggers Patricia's autonomy reflex (she must independently reach the conclusion that you deserve promotion), and confirms nothing about her self-opinion. The preparation window of three weeks is more important than the meeting itself.

In the two weeks before the review, find two or three genuine opportunities to interact with Patricia in low-stakes contexts — a hallway conversation, a working group she leads, a request for her perspective on an industry challenge relevant to your work. In these interactions, practice deep listening. Ask a substantive question about her area of expertise, then listen fully without preparing your response while she speaks. Reflect something she says back accurately in your own words. Let the conversation be eighty percent hers. This confirms her intelligence and experience, and it makes you memorable in a positive, non-threatening way.

On the flattery question: praising Patricia for the well-known strengths she is confident about will register as transparent because she has heard it many times. Instead, look for qualities she seems to care about but may feel underappreciated for — perhaps her mentorship of junior staff, or a strategic call she made that did not receive much internal recognition. A single, specific, well-timed comment about one of these is worth more than a dozen generic compliments. In the review itself, frame your ambitions in terms of how you can advance goals Patricia has already articulated publicly. You are not asking to be promoted; you are asking to take on more responsibility in service of a direction she has already chosen.

</details>

**Scenario 3: Changing a Family Member's Entrenched Behavior**
Your adult sibling, David, has been isolating himself since a difficult breakup two years ago, declining social invitations and spending most of his time alone. You are worried. Every time you raise it directly — pointing out that isolation is bad for him, urging him to get out more — he becomes defensive and the conversation ends badly.

*What should you consider?*
- How does the chapter's framework explain why your direct approach has been making the problem worse, not better?
- David's resistance is rooted in a low self-opinion ("I am not worthy of connection right now") — how does Greene's caveat about low self-opinion alter the strategy compared to the standard approach?
- What would a "use their language" or "use their emotions" judo move look like in this situation?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Your direct approach fails for two compounding reasons. First, by framing isolation as a problem David needs to fix, you imply that his current choices are wrong — challenging his autonomy pillar (he is choosing to be alone) and implicitly his goodness pillar (he is not taking care of himself, which is a form of failure). His defensiveness is not irrational; it is a natural response to feeling criticized. Second, each failed confrontation adds a layer of shame to the dynamic, deepening his low self-opinion rather than lifting it.

Greene's caveat is critical here: a low self-opinion cannot be reached by cheerful encouragement, because optimism clashes with how David currently understands his situation. Telling him things will get better, or that he just needs to push himself, will feel invalidating — as if you do not understand how bad things are. The entry point is empathy and mirroring, not correction. Begin by acknowledging the difficulty of what he went through without immediately pivoting to solutions. Let him feel genuinely heard — that the isolation makes sense given how painful the past two years were. Once he feels understood rather than managed, his defenses soften slightly, and you have a small amount of latitude.

The judo move available here uses his existing desires rather than fighting his isolation. If David has expressed any residual interest — in a sport, a show, a type of food — propose something that is low-stakes, short-duration, and involves only the two of you (not a social group, which would feel overwhelming). Frame it not as "getting him out" but as something you want his company for: "I want to see that film and don't want to go alone." This appeals to his goodness pillar (he can do something for you) and removes the threat to autonomy (this is a favor to you, not a treatment for him). The goal is one small positive social experience, not a transformation — and that small experience is what eventually builds momentum.

</details>

## ✍️ Reflection Prompts

1. Think of a time when you tried to change someone's mind or behavior through direct argument — explaining your reasoning, presenting evidence, perhaps expressing frustration when they resisted. Looking back now, which of their three self-opinion pillars (autonomy, intelligence, or goodness) were you inadvertently threatening with each move you made? What would you do differently if you understood that winning the argument was the wrong goal entirely?

2. Think of a person in your life — a boss, a colleague, a family member — whose defensive or stubborn behavior you find genuinely exhausting. What do you know, or could find out, about that person's specific insecurities and the personalized self-opinion they use to manage those insecurities? Now that you understand the connection between those insecurities and their behavior, what would it look like to confirm rather than challenge their self-opinion in your next interaction with them?

3. Think of a belief you hold strongly — about politics, your profession, a relationship, or your own character — that you have not seriously questioned in several years. Apply Nietzsche's technique: for one week, engage with the strongest version of the opposing view with "all possible love," suspending judgment and looking for the "soul of the thing." What would you need to discover to consider updating this belief? What does the strength of your resistance to doing this exercise reveal about your own self-opinion?

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the concept of "self-opinion" and why confirming it is the master key to influence — in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

Every person carries a core image of themselves — that they are free to choose their own path, intelligent in their own way, and basically a good person — and this image is so central to their identity that anything threatening it triggers an automatic wall of resistance, regardless of how logical your argument might be. When you instead confirm this self-image — making someone feel that their choices are respected, their knowledge valued, and their character admirable — their defenses drop in the same way they drop when someone falls in love or joins a cause they believe in, and their minds become genuinely open to your ideas. The paradox of influence is therefore this: the less you focus on yourself and the more you focus on validating the other person's self-image, the more power over them you quietly accumulate.

</details>

## 📋 Action Items

1. Before your next meeting or one-on-one conversation where you need something from the other person, spend five minutes beforehand writing down one answer to each of these three questions: What does this person believe about their own intelligence or expertise that I can genuinely confirm? What cause or value do they see themselves as supporting? What would make them feel that whatever I am asking of them was their own idea? Use those answers to reframe your opening rather than leading with what you need.

2. Identify one relationship in your life — professional or personal — where you have been making your case repeatedly and hitting the same resistance. This week, have one conversation with that person in which your only goal is to listen. Ask one substantive question about their perspective, then let them talk. Do not defend your position, offer counterarguments, or pivot to your agenda. Afterward, write down two things you learned about their self-opinion that you did not know before, and one way that knowledge changes your approach.

3. Choose one opinion, belief, or judgment you hold about a person in your life that has calcified over time. Apply the Nietzsche technique from the chapter's self-strategy section: spend fifteen minutes reading, watching, or listening to the strongest version of the opposing view with deliberate openness — looking only for what rings true, not for what you can refute. Write a single sentence capturing the "soul of the thing" you found. The goal is not to change your mind but to notice what it feels like when your own defensive walls come down.
