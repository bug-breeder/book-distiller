# Practice Exercises: Chapter 19 — Make Them Want to Follow You (The Law of Fickleness)

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Greene argues that human ambivalence toward leaders is not a modern phenomenon but a permanent feature of human nature. What are the two competing psychological forces driving this ambivalence, and why can't leaders simply eliminate it through good performance?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The two forces are the simultaneous need for and resentment of leaders. People crave leadership because without it groups dissolve into competing narrow interests and bad decisions go unmade — they feel disoriented and even hysterical without a leader. Yet they also resent the dependence, envy the privileges, and fear the abuse that power invites. This mirrors the parent-child dynamic established in childhood, where love and resentment coexist. Because both drives are structural — rooted in vulnerability, dependence, and willfulness — no amount of good leadership erases the negative pole. It can only be managed. Leaders who ignore this dynamic are blindsided when people turn on them; the ambivalence was always there, just masked by deference.

</details>

---

**Q2:** Greene distinguishes between authority derived from position and authority earned through character and action. Why does positional authority alone create fragility, and what specifically does Elizabeth I's approach reveal about a more durable alternative?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Positional authority rests on the assumption of obedience — people comply because they must, not because they genuinely follow. This produces thin emotional connection, ministers focused on appeasement rather than creative work, and loyalty that evaporates the moment the leader shows weakness or makes a mistake. Elizabeth began her reign with zero positional credibility — young, female, inexperienced, inheriting a bankrupt country — yet she built lasting authority by demonstrating that everything she did served England rather than herself. She worked harder than her ministers, mastered the actual details of governance, made personal financial sacrifices, and earned trust incrementally through results. Her credibility was causally tied to her actions rather than her title, which meant it compounded over time rather than eroding from unmet expectations.

</details>

---

**Q3:** Greene introduces the concept of the "entitlement curse" as particularly dangerous for leaders. Explain the specific mechanisms by which entitlement undermines leadership effectiveness, going beyond the observation that it makes leaders seem arrogant.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Entitlement operates through at least four distinct mechanisms. First, it makes leaders passive — they wait for loyalty and respect to arrive rather than actively generating it, so the emotional bond with followers never deepens. Second, it makes them lazy about standards — if you already believe you are exceptional, there is no urgency to refine your work or thinking. Third, it makes them insensitive — leaders notice only the smiles and nods that people show out of self-preservation, missing the underlying resentment building beneath. Fourth, it makes leaders defensive and blame-prone — mistakes are attributed to others or circumstances, which destroys accountability and signals that the leader's ego takes priority over the group's welfare. Each mechanism accelerates the others in a reinforcing cycle, until the tipping point where followers turn with shocking suddenness.

</details>

**Q4:** Greene describes "the third eye" as the visionary capacity that is the most essential element of authority. How does this concept differ from simply having good ideas or being smart, and what does Xenophon's story illustrate about how it is developed and expressed?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The third eye is not intelligence or cleverness but the disciplined capacity to detach from the emotions and panicked reactions of the present moment and perceive the larger picture — trends, terrain, consequences, and possibilities that others cannot see while they are overwhelmed by the crisis at hand. Xenophon was not a military expert, yet he led ten thousand soldiers out of Persian captivity because he had trained himself through years of philosophical study to think at the level of global patterns rather than immediate fears. He gamed out the entire retreat in a single night: terrain, enemy positions, the principle of speed over security, and contingency paths. The key development method Greene describes is practicing deliberate elevation of perspective — forcing yourself to consider the enemy's view, outsiders' ideas, future scenarios including failure modes, and the gestalt shape of the situation. It is a cognitive habit that can be trained, not a gift.

</details>

**Q5:** Greene argues that the modern prejudice against authority — expressed through calls for leaderless consensus, minimal hierarchy, and "the parent as friend" — has unintended consequences that are worse than the problems it tries to solve. What are those consequences, and how does he connect them to the rise of false forms of authority?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Without legitimate authority in the arts, there is no strong prior tradition to rebel against or surpass, producing only ephemeral trends. Without parental authority, adolescents cannot go through the critical rebellion needed to forge a genuine identity and grow up searching externally for direction. Without respected teachers and masters, students cannot absorb deep experience and wisdom they might later transcend. At the organizational level, leaders who believe their job is merely to enable consensus never provide the far-reaching vision that groups actually need. The vacuum that results from all this is not freedom but disorientation, and humans — who have always needed authority figures — fill it with false substitutes: the strongman who projects control without real vision, the panderer who mimics public desire, the chummy leader who offers the illusion of fairness, and the tyranny of group opinion amplified by social media. Each false form produces more chaos and worse decisions than the imperfect real thing it replaced.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Inherited Team**
You have just been promoted to lead a team of eight people, three of whom were candidates for your role. You have strong technical credentials but no prior management experience. One of the passed-over candidates is visibly cold toward you and has already begun subtly framing your ideas as impractical in team meetings.

*What should you consider?*
- How do the principles of "leading from the front" and setting tone from day one apply here, and what does Greene warn about starting soft?
- How should you interpret the cold colleague's behavior in light of the chapter's framework on ambivalence — and what distinguishes someone who can be won over from a "bad apple"?
- How can you begin building the credibility of earned authority without positional posturing or social maneuvering?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Greene's most urgent instruction here is about first impressions: if you let people get the early impression they can maneuver you, they will do so mercilessly. This means you cannot open with excessive warmth, consensus-seeking, or deference — that signals pushover. Instead, from the first days you lead from the front: demonstrate mastery of the work itself, set visible standards for your own output that are higher than what you ask of others, and be specific and confident in articulating direction. This doesn't require aggression; it requires unmistakable seriousness.

On the cold colleague: the chapter distinguishes between people whose ambivalence can be managed through demonstrated competence and genuine fairness, and "bad apples" — passive-aggressive types who will undermine any leader regardless. The key diagnostic question is whether the hostility is about you specifically or is a habitual pattern with any authority figure. Watch how this person behaved under the previous manager and how they treat peers. If they are situationally reactive to you, engage them directly and privately — ask for their perspective on a real challenge, giving them visibility and genuine influence on something that matters. If they are structurally oppositional, marginalize them quickly and deliberately before they infect the group dynamic.

The path to authority runs through results. Pick one concrete problem the team has been stuck on, apply your full cognitive resources to it, and solve it visibly. Demonstrate the "third eye" quality — show that you can see the larger picture. Over weeks, your credibility will compound through evidence rather than self-promotion.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Overcommitting CEO**
You are a director reporting to a CEO who regularly promises the board and customers things the team cannot deliver on the stated timeline. When deadlines are missed, the CEO blames execution — meaning your team — publicly. Morale is degrading, your best people are starting to look for other jobs, and you have limited direct power to change the CEO's behavior.

*What should you consider?*
- How does Greene's "taboo" strategy — never appear to take, always to give — explain the mechanism of trust destruction happening here?
- What does the chapter say about leaders who overpromise, and how does that compound over time?
- What can you do within your sphere, as a sub-leader, to preserve your own authority and buffer your team from the effects of illegitimate authority above you?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Greene's analysis maps cleanly onto this situation. Overpromising violates the taboo because people have acute memory for promises — when delivery fails, it feels like something actively taken away rather than simply not given. The first miss creates doubt; the second begins eroding authority sharply. The CEO's additional move of publicly blaming the team compounds this: it broadcasts that personal ego protection trumps accountability, which is one of the clearest signals that a leader's self-interest has consumed their concern for the group. People working under such leaders gradually disengage — they stop channeling their best creative energy into work for someone who will claim credit for wins and redirect blame for losses.

As a sub-leader your authority runs parallel to, not through, the CEO. Your task is to demonstrate, within your team, the qualities the CEO lacks: accountability (own mistakes, explain your reasoning, show what you learned), realistic commitment (make promises you can keep and keep them with consistency), and genuine focus on the team's welfare. This creates a zone of trust that partially insulates people from the toxicity above. You should also become the honest broker of expectations — not undermining the CEO publicly, but privately coaching your team on what is achievable, and privately pushing back upward on timelines before promises are made rather than after. Greene would advise maintaining your own principled standard even when those above you do not. Your authority with your direct team is built on your actions, not your title.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Long-Tenured Expert**
You have led a nonprofit organization for twelve years with considerable success. A new generation of staff members has arrived who seem to treat your established methods with polite skepticism. In meetings they defer to you, but you sense that outside meetings they are developing an alternative vision for the organization's direction. You feel frustrated that your track record is not generating the automatic respect it once did.

*What should you consider?*
- What does Greene's section on "Rejuvenate Your Authority: Adaptability" say about the specific danger that long tenure creates, and how does entitlement re-enter even for leaders who earned their authority legitimately?
- How does the balance of presence and absence apply to your situation?
- What concrete moves would Greene recommend to re-establish the emotional connection with this new generation rather than simply expecting them to honor your history?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Greene is explicit: authority that was earned can become rigid and stodgy with time. Long-tenured leaders become the "father figure who starts to seem oppressive by how long he has monopolized power." The danger is not that you stop working hard or caring — it is that success gradually generates the very entitlement you once consciously rejected. You begin expecting the level of deference you previously earned, which is functionally identical to never having earned it in the first place. The new generation is immune to your historical aura; they evaluate you in real time against their own needs and standards, and they detect immediately when your confidence has curdled into assumption.

On presence and absence: twelve years of constant availability and familiarity may have made you "too banal" in Greene's terms — too known, too predictable, not mysterious enough to generate fascination. Deliberately creating some strategic absence — delegating more, stepping back from certain decisions, making your appearances at important moments more impactful — can restore the drama of your presence.

The deeper move is to do what Elizabeth did late in her reign: remain genuinely attuned to what the new generation actually needs and values, without condescension. Ask them real questions about the future direction and genuinely incorporate the good ideas, not performatively. Show your third eye by demonstrating that your long experience gives you the ability to see further ahead than they can — not by telling them this, but by producing a compelling and specific vision for where the organization should go in the next five years that they could not have generated without your pattern recognition. Make yourself necessary again through demonstrated forward thinking, not by invoking the past.

</details>

## ✍️ Reflection Prompts

1. Think of a leader or authority figure — a boss, teacher, parent, or mentor — whose authority you genuinely accepted and even wanted to follow, where you found yourself working to earn their approval rather than simply complying with their directives. What specific behaviors or qualities created that effect in you? Now that you understand Greene's framework of vision, empathy, leading from the front, and the aura of mixed signals, can you identify which of these mechanisms were operating? What would you do differently in your own leadership to replicate those qualities deliberately rather than accidentally?

2. Think of a time when you felt entitled to respect, credit, or loyalty — from a team, a relationship, a community, or a project — and were surprised or hurt when it was not given. Looking back through the lens of Greene's entitlement curse, what signals of that entitlement might the people around you have been picking up on that you were not aware of? What would you do differently now, knowing that earned authority requires active and continuous demonstration that the group's welfare comes before your ego?

3. Think of a moment when you were in a leadership or influential role and sensed that you were losing people's trust or respect — not through a single dramatic failure but through a slow drift, a mounting distance, people nodding and then acting differently. At the time, how did you explain it to yourself? Now that you understand the mechanics of fickleness, ambivalence, and the invisible friction created when leaders fail to provide vision and genuine empathy, what do you think was actually happening, and what specific actions could you have taken earlier to course-correct before the drift became irreversible?

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the difference between positional authority and earned authority — and why the distinction matters for anyone who leads or influences others — in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

Positional authority is the kind you get automatically from a job title or role — people follow you because they have to, not because they genuinely trust your judgment, and that compliance is shallow and disappears the moment you stumble. Earned authority is something deeper: it is the influence you build when people come to believe, through consistent evidence over time, that you are working for their benefit and the group's benefit rather than your own, that you see further ahead than they do, and that you hold yourself to the same standards you ask of them. The distinction matters because positional authority gives you obedience but never the willing engagement of people's best thinking and effort, while earned authority creates a group that actively wants to realize your vision — which is the only way leaders ever accomplish anything genuinely significant.

</details>

## 🧩 Synthesis Challenge

**Exercise:** Chapter 19 argues that genuine authority requires leaders to maintain a paradoxical dual focus: a firm, unwavering vision for the group's larger mission combined with acute, visceral empathy for the shifting emotional states of individual members. Chapter 2 (The Law of Narcissism) describes how deep narcissistic wounds make people chronically self-absorbed, unable to genuinely attend to others, and prone to using relationships as mirrors for their own validation rather than as genuine connections.

Design a self-assessment exercise in which you identify one specific leadership relationship in your life — current or past — where your own narcissistic tendencies (as described in Chapter 2) may have degraded your capacity for the empathic attunement that Chapter 19 identifies as essential to authority. Describe in writing: (a) the specific behaviors or patterns that might have reflected narcissistic self-absorption rather than genuine other-focus; (b) what signals from the other person or group you likely missed or dismissed because you were attending to your own emotional needs; and (c) using the strategies from Chapter 19 — particularly "Focus outwardly: the Attitude" — what concrete practice you would adopt to interrupt the narcissistic pull and rebuild genuine attunement.

**Chapters involved:** Chapter 19 + Chapter 2

## 📋 Action Items

1. Before your next team meeting or group interaction this week, spend fifteen minutes writing down what you believe each person in the room is currently worried about, frustrated by, or hoping for — not in relation to you, but in their own work and lives. During the meeting, pay attention to nonverbal cues and cross-check your predictions. After the meeting, note where you were wrong and why. This is the beginning of the outward-focus habit Greene calls essential to authority.

2. Identify one promise, commitment, or expectation you have created in the last month — with a team member, colleague, collaborator, or client — that you are at risk of not delivering on. This week, proactively reach out to that person, own the situation directly and without excuses, describe what you will actually deliver and when, and ask what they need. Do not wait for them to bring it up. This single act of accountable follow-through does more for your earned authority than a dozen successful outcomes delivered silently.

3. On Friday of this week, sit for thirty uninterrupted minutes and write out your answer to this question: "What is the most important thing this group (team, family, organization) needs to accomplish in the next six months that only I, given my position and knowledge, can see clearly enough to push for?" This is the beginning of the visionary practice Greene describes — forcing yourself to elevate your perspective above the daily operational noise and articulate the larger picture. Write it as specifically as possible: not a value or aspiration, but a concrete direction with a reason and a risk if ignored.
