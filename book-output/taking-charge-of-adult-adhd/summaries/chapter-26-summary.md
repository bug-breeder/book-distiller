# Chapter 14: The Nonstimulants

## Core Thesis
When stimulants are not the right fit — due to side effects, substance abuse history, comorbid conditions, or simply a different neurobiological profile — a set of well-researched nonstimulant medications can manage ADHD symptoms through entirely different brain mechanisms, offering genuine alternatives rather than inferior fallbacks.

## Detailed Breakdown

### Atomoxetine (Strattera): The First Nonstimulant Approved for Adult ADHD

- **What it is:** Atomoxetine is a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) — it blocks nerve cells from reabsorbing norepinephrine after it is released, leaving more of that chemical messenger available to act on neighboring cells. It was FDA-approved in 2003 for children, teens, and adults, making it the first and still the only nonstimulant specifically approved for adult ADHD treatment.
- **Why it matters:** It fills a critical gap: roughly 25% of people with ADHD respond better to atomoxetine than to any stimulant, and another large group cannot tolerate stimulant side effects (especially insomnia). Having an approved nonstimulant means these patients are not left without an evidence-based pharmaceutical option.
- **How it works:** Norepinephrine normally activates prefrontal cortex circuits responsible for executive function — the same circuits that are underactive in ADHD. By preventing reuptake, atomoxetine keeps norepinephrine levels elevated in those frontal circuits. As a secondary effect, it also raises dopamine specifically in the frontal cortex (but not in the brain's reward or addiction centers), which means it addresses dopamine-related ADHD deficits without generating abuse potential.
- **Key quote or example:** "By affecting a different brain chemical, it may work better than the stimulants for some people. It all depends on where your particular ADHD neurological deficits lie. We're all biologically unique, especially in the organization and functioning of the brain."
- **Connection:** Barkley established in Chapter 13 that stimulants primarily target dopamine. Atomoxetine's norepinephrine-first mechanism is the pharmacological explanation for why some people respond to one class but not the other — different ADHD patients have deficits in different neurotransmitter systems.

### Atomoxetine's Effectiveness Relative to Stimulants

- **What it is:** A quantified comparison of how well atomoxetine works versus stimulants across the population of people with ADHD.
- **Why it matters:** Patients choosing between medication classes need realistic expectations, not just binary "works / doesn't work" framing.
- **How it works:** On average, 75% of people experience positive effects from either stimulants or nonstimulants. Within that broad response rate, roughly 50% respond well to both; 25% respond better to a stimulant than to atomoxetine; and 25% respond better to atomoxetine than to any stimulant. The drug is generally considered slightly less potent than the most powerful stimulants (e.g., amphetamine) but is sufficient for mild-to-moderate ADHD and cases where stimulant side effects are a dealbreaker.
- **Key quote or example:** "Some studies suggest that while 50% of people respond positively to both types of medications, 25% may respond better to a stimulant than to atomoxetine, while the remaining 25% may respond better to atomoxetine than one of the stimulants."
- **Connection:** This probabilistic breakdown directly extends the individualized-treatment theme running through the entire medication section. No single drug works for everyone; matching the drug to the person's neurobiological profile and lifestyle is the goal.

### Why Atomoxetine Is Not Abused (and Why That Matters Practically)

- **What it is:** An explanation of why atomoxetine carries no meaningful abuse potential and the concrete real-world conveniences that flow from that fact.
- **Why it matters:** Stimulants are DEA Schedule II controlled substances — they require paper prescriptions, cannot be called in to a pharmacy, must be kept under lock and key, and raise legitimate concern for patients with personal or household substance abuse histories. Atomoxetine eliminates every one of these barriers.
- **How it works:** Although atomoxetine raises dopamine in the frontal cortex, it does not raise dopamine in the limbic reward centers (nucleus accumbens, ventral tegmental area) that produce drug-seeking behavior and euphoria. Without that reward-center activation, there is no "high," no craving, and no diversion appeal. Studies confirm that people addicted to drugs do not preferentially seek out atomoxetine — they rate it no more desirable than antidepressants.
- **Key quote or example:** "One study of adult alcoholics with ADHD showed that atomoxetine improved ADHD and reduced heavy drinking at the same time."
- **Connection:** This dual benefit — treating the ADHD while actually reducing harmful drinking — is a powerful argument for atomoxetine as a first-line choice whenever comorbid substance use is part of the clinical picture, something Barkley addresses when discussing ADHD's frequent co-occurrence with addiction throughout the book.

### Safety Profile of Atomoxetine

- **What it is:** A frank, evidence-based accounting of what can go wrong and how likely each risk actually is.
- **Why it matters:** Barkley's approach throughout the book is to give patients accurate risk information so they can make genuinely informed decisions rather than either dismissing all medication or accepting it uncritically.
- **How it works:** The serious risks are extremely rare: liver inflammation occurs in approximately 1 in 1 million patients (and causal attribution is uncertain in most reported cases). The FDA warning about increased suicidal thinking applies only to children and even there is considered methodologically questionable — it showed up at only 1 of the 23 study sites and data collection methods were imperfect. The drug does raise heart rate and blood pressure, but less so than stimulants. The more common, manageable side effects are nausea, dry mouth, dizziness, drowsiness, constipation, sweating, decreased libido or erectile dysfunction, and occasional irritability. A critical drug interaction: never combine with MAOIs (monoamine oxidase inhibitors) — the combination can be life-threatening.
- **Key quote or example:** "Liver complications occur once in every million people treated — one to four cases out of 4.5 million treated."
- **Connection:** This mirrors Barkley's earlier safety discussions of stimulants in Chapters 12–13, where he consistently uses base-rate reasoning to put rare risks in perspective rather than letting rare events generate disproportionate fear.

### Dosing and Titration of Atomoxetine

- **What it is:** The practical timeline and approach for finding the right atomoxetine dose.
- **Why it matters:** Patients who expect instant results (as they sometimes see with stimulants, which often produce noticeable effects on day one) may abandon atomoxetine prematurely, missing out on a drug that could work well for them.
- **How it works:** Atomoxetine is taken once daily (or split into two doses if drowsiness is a problem). Because raising norepinephrine too quickly causes nausea and drowsiness, doctors titrate the dose slowly — typically over 2–4 weeks. This slower ramp-up requires patience but is inherent to the drug's mechanism, not a flaw. The extended-release formulation lasts all day without needing a separate ER version (unlike methylphenidate or amphetamine). A related nonstimulant, viloxazine (Qelbree), was FDA approved in 2021 for children and may titrate faster; adult approval was pending at the time of writing.
- **Key quote or example:** "You've been dealing with ADHD for years, so a few weeks to achieve good control of your symptoms is a small price to pay for the advantages this drug can provide."
- **Connection:** The titration contrast with stimulants (which show effects within hours) reinforces the book's broader message that selecting an ADHD medication requires weighing multiple factors — speed of onset, side-effect profile, abuse potential, comorbidities — rather than choosing whichever drug works fastest.

### When Atomoxetine Is Clearly the Better Choice

- **What it is:** A specific set of clinical circumstances in which atomoxetine should be favored over stimulants, even though stimulants are generally more potent.
- **Why it matters:** Patients and clinicians need a clear decision framework, not a vague instruction to "try different things."
- **How it works:** Atomoxetine is the preferred choice when: (1) ADHD is mild to moderate and maximum potency is unnecessary; (2) multiple stimulants have been tried without sufficient improvement; (3) stimulant-induced insomnia has been intolerable — atomoxetine rarely causes sleep problems; (4) there is a personal or household substance abuse history; (5) all-day coverage is needed without mid-day dosing; (6) comorbid anxiety, depression, tics, OCD, or nervous mannerisms are present — atomoxetine does not worsen these conditions and may improve tics or anxiety. For anxiety and depression specifically, bupropion (discussed below) is another candidate.
- **Key quote or example:** "Atomoxetine doesn't worsen these problems; in some cases, tics or anxiety might actually improve."
- **Connection:** ADHD rarely exists in isolation. The co-occurrence of ADHD with anxiety disorders, mood disorders, and tic disorders is addressed repeatedly throughout the book, and this list of "better choice" scenarios is a direct practical application of that comorbidity reality.

### Guanfacine XR (Intuniv): A Third-Line Option with a Different Brain Mechanism

- **What it is:** An extended-release formulation of an antihypertensive medication (guanfacine) that has been reformulated to treat ADHD. It is considered a third-line agent — less effective than stimulants and probably slightly less effective than atomoxetine.
- **Why it matters:** Some patients fail or cannot tolerate both stimulants and atomoxetine; guanfacine XR provides an additional pharmacological option. Its completely different mechanism of action also makes it combinable with other ADHD drugs in some cases.
- **How it works:** Rather than changing the amount of neurotransmitter at synapses (as stimulants and atomoxetine do), guanfacine XR works by "fine-tuning the transmission of signals through nerve cells." Each nerve cell axon contains alpha-2 receptor ports; when these ports are open, nerve signals are weakened and disrupted. Guanfacine XR closes these ports, strengthening the signal as it travels down the axon, which makes each nerve cell communicate more reliably with its neighbors — effectively amplifying the signal rather than boosting the chemical supply. Unlike stimulants, guanfacine XR stays in the body longer than 24 hours before being metabolized. The tablet must be swallowed whole (not crushed) to preserve the extended-release mechanism.
- **Key quote or example:** "Those change the amount of neurotransmitter available in the brain at the synapse or gap between nerve cells... Guanfacine XR achieves its effects by fine-tuning the transmission of signals through nerve cells."
- **Connection:** The three distinct mechanisms described across Chapters 13–14 — dopamine/norepinephrine reuptake inhibition (stimulants), selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibition (atomoxetine), and alpha-2 receptor modulation (guanfacine XR) — illustrate that ADHD is a heterogeneous disorder at the neurobiological level, which is why no single medication works for everyone.

### Side Effects and Practical Considerations for Guanfacine XR

- **What it is:** The clinical tradeoffs of choosing guanfacine XR, including the distinguishing feature that its antihypertensive action creates unique side effects.
- **Why it matters:** A drug that lowers blood pressure will have different risks than a drug that raises it (like stimulants) — patients and clinicians need to understand this distinction to avoid dangerous mismatches.
- **How it works:** Common side effects include dizziness, drowsiness, headache, irritability, low blood pressure, nausea, stomachache, dry mouth, and constipation. Uncommon but serious effects include very low blood pressure, slow heart rate, and fainting. Sedation is especially prominent when starting the medication. Like atomoxetine, dose titration is gradual and takes patience. Like atomoxetine, guanfacine XR has no significant abuse potential because it does not activate brain reward centers. A related drug, clonidine XR, was approved for children and potential adult approval was pending. Due to its lower efficacy relative to other options, guanfacine XR is most useful when other options have failed.
- **Key quote or example:** "It is often viewed as a third-line medicine for managing ADHD, given that it is less effective than the stimulants and greater patience is needed while adjusting the dose to achieve optimal results."
- **Connection:** The cardiovascular considerations here (blood pressure dropping) contrast directly with the stimulant cardiovascular concerns discussed in Chapter 13 (blood pressure rising) — reinforcing that each drug class requires monitoring on the same dimension but in opposite directions.

### Bupropion (Wellbutrin): An Off-Label Option for ADHD with Comorbidities

- **What it is:** An antidepressant (not FDA-approved for ADHD) that is sometimes prescribed off-label for adults with ADHD, especially when anxiety or depression is also present. Off-label use means using a drug for a purpose not formally approved by the FDA but supported by clinical evidence and professional judgment.
- **Why it matters:** Many adults with ADHD also have depression or anxiety — conditions for which bupropion is FDA approved. When a single drug can treat multiple diagnoses simultaneously, it reduces pill burden and potential drug interactions.
- **How it works:** Bupropion raises norepinephrine in the brain (the same mechanism as atomoxetine), but it also affects dopamine and serotonin. The dopamine effect may contribute to ADHD symptom reduction; the serotonin effect differentiates its side-effect profile from pure norepinephrine drugs. Research indicates significant benefits for ADHD symptoms, though the evidence base is thinner than for atomoxetine because it was studied for depression, not ADHD. If ADHD is the only diagnosis, a stimulant or atomoxetine is generally preferred.
- **Key quote or example:** "Using a medication to treat a condition for which it is not FDA approved is called 'off-label use' and can be perfectly safe (and even the best choice). But always ask your doctor to explain the pros and cons and why one drug is preferred over the alternatives."
- **Connection:** Bupropion represents the broader principle, introduced early in the book, that ADHD management is highly individualized and that clinical wisdom (not just FDA labels) should guide treatment decisions, particularly in the complex reality of ADHD with comorbidities.

### Modafinil and Other Nonstimulants: Promising but Unproven

- **What it is:** A brief accounting of other drugs that have been explored for ADHD but have not received FDA approval or sufficient evidence for recommendation.
- **Why it matters:** Patients often encounter these drugs discussed online or by practitioners and need accurate information about their actual evidence status.
- **How it works:** Modafinil (Provigil), an antinarcoleptic/wakefulness agent, showed early promise in children but results were inconsistent across studies, and no adult ADHD research exists. A rare severe allergic skin reaction in an initial FDA study halted the approval process, and the manufacturer declined to fund further trials. Barkley notes candidly that the approval process is unlikely to be completed. Other medications — antidepressants, antianxiety drugs, antihypertensives, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics — are sometimes combined with ADHD medications to address comorbid conditions, not ADHD itself.
- **Key quote or example:** "The manufacturer, as of this writing, seems unlikely to invest any more time and money in this approval process."
- **Connection:** This frank disclosure about how pharmaceutical economics influence which drugs get studied and approved is consistent with Barkley's throughout-the-book insistence on scientific honesty, including acknowledging the limits of current knowledge.

## Key Takeaways

1. Atomoxetine is the only nonstimulant FDA-approved for adult ADHD and is a genuinely effective — not just a fallback — option for roughly 25% of adults who respond better to it than to any stimulant.
2. The 75% positive response rate holds across both stimulants and nonstimulants; the key is identifying which specific drug class matches a given individual's neurobiological deficit profile.
3. Atomoxetine's non-abuse status is not a minor footnote — it means no controlled-substance paperwork, phone-in refills, no household diversion risk, and a likely simultaneous reduction in substance abuse for patients who struggle with it.
4. Patience is non-negotiable with atomoxetine: 2–4 weeks of gradual titration is required, and abandoning the drug before reaching a therapeutic dose means never knowing whether it would have worked.
5. Atomoxetine is the drug of choice when comorbid anxiety, depression, tics, or OCD are present — it does not worsen these conditions and may actively improve some of them.
6. Guanfacine XR works through an entirely different mechanism — strengthening nerve signal transmission rather than boosting neurotransmitter levels — which makes it pharmacologically unique but also less potent than stimulants and roughly comparable to or slightly below atomoxetine in efficacy.
7. Guanfacine XR lowers blood pressure (the opposite of stimulants); this creates a distinct side-effect profile including dizziness, sedation, and rare fainting that requires its own monitoring protocol.
8. Bupropion is a rational choice only when ADHD co-occurs with depression or anxiety — for pure ADHD it is outperformed by stimulants and atomoxetine.
9. Off-label prescribing is a legitimate, evidence-based clinical practice — the absence of FDA approval for a specific indication does not mean a drug is unsafe or ineffective for that use.
10. Modafinil and other nonstimulants lack sufficient adult ADHD evidence and should not be chosen over approved options; market economics, not just science, explain why some promising drugs never complete the approval process.

## Mental Model / Framework

**The ADHD Medication Selection Ladder**

Think of ADHD medication choices as a tiered ladder, where each rung represents a different class with different strengths, mechanisms, and appropriate use cases:

**Rung 1 — Stimulants (amphetamine / methylphenidate):** Most potent, fastest onset, strongest evidence base. First choice when there are no contraindications.

**Rung 2 — Atomoxetine (Strattera):** Slightly less potent on average, but the right first choice (not a consolation prize) when stimulants have failed, caused intolerable side effects, or when comorbidities (anxiety, tics, substance abuse) tip the risk-benefit calculation.

**Rung 3 — Guanfacine XR (Intuniv):** Less potent than both Rungs 1 and 2. Unique mechanism (signal amplification rather than neurotransmitter boosting). Used when the first two rungs have failed or in combination strategies.

**Rung 4 — Off-label options (bupropion, clonidine XR, viloxazine):** Appropriate in specific comorbidity scenarios or when all approved options have failed.

**The key insight the ladder captures:** Moving down a rung is not failure — it is precision. A patient on Rung 2 may be exactly where their neurobiology places them. The goal is to match the mechanism to the deficit, not to achieve the "strongest" drug.

A second useful framework: **the abuse-potential axis.** Stimulants sit at one end (scheduled, abuse potential, reward-center activation). Atomoxetine and guanfacine XR sit at the other (unscheduled, no reward-center activation). This single axis predicts: prescription convenience, household safety considerations, and suitability for patients with substance abuse histories.

## "Aha!" Moments

1. **Atomoxetine raises dopamine — just in the wrong place for addiction.** Many patients assume that because atomoxetine doesn't target dopamine, it is irrelevant to dopamine-based ADHD deficits. In reality, it does raise dopamine — but only in the prefrontal cortex, not in the reward centers. This is a precise neurobiological distinction, not a vague claim: the same chemical in a different brain region produces cognitive benefit without producing euphoria or craving. Dopamine location, not just dopamine quantity, is what separates treatment from addiction.

2. **Guanfacine XR fixes ADHD by doing nothing to the neurotransmitter supply — it improves signal transmission instead.** Every other ADHD drug in the chapter works by changing how much of a chemical is available at the synapse. Guanfacine XR ignores the chemical supply entirely and instead closes the "leaky valves" (alpha-2 ports) along the nerve cell's signal pathway, making existing signals travel more reliably. This is a completely different level of the same nervous system — like fixing a leaky pipe versus adding more water pressure. The existence of two pharmacologically independent routes to the same clinical outcome (better ADHD control) reveals that ADHD is not a single neurochemical problem but a signal-reliability problem that can be attacked at multiple points.

3. **The "25% respond better to atomoxetine" finding reframes what it means to "try a stimulant first."** Standard practice is to try stimulants first because on average they are more effective. But "on average" masks the 25% for whom atomoxetine is actually the superior drug. If a patient fails one stimulant, the evidence-based interpretation is not "medication doesn't work for me" — it is "I may be in the 25% for whom norepinephrine-targeted treatment is the better match." The failure of a stimulant is data pointing toward atomoxetine, not evidence against medication in general.

## In My Own Words (ELI5)

Imagine your brain is a factory where workers (nerve cells) need to pass notes to each other to get things done. In ADHD, the notes either get lost, arrive too late, or the workers just don't have enough paper to write them. That's why it's hard to focus, plan ahead, and control impulses.

Stimulants — the most common ADHD drugs — work by flooding the factory with more "paper" (dopamine), so the notes are easier to write and send. That works great for most people. But about one in four people either can't handle stimulants (they cause bad insomnia or anxiety), or their factory's problem is a different kind of paper shortage (norepinephrine instead of dopamine).

That's where nonstimulants come in.

**Atomoxetine (Strattera)** is like a recycling system that catches the used "norepinephrine paper" before it gets thrown away, so there's always more of it available for the workers. It works more slowly than stimulants — you have to wait 2–4 weeks to get the full effect — but it lasts all day without wearing off, and because it doesn't touch the part of the brain that causes drug cravings, nobody wants to abuse it. It's especially good if you also have anxiety, depression, or tics, because it doesn't make those worse and might actually improve them.

**Guanfacine XR (Intuniv)** works completely differently. Instead of adding more paper to the factory, it fixes broken "postal tubes" that the notes travel through. Each nerve cell has little valves along it that, when open, let the message leak out before it arrives. Guanfacine XR closes those valves so the message reaches its destination intact. It's the weakest of the three options, takes even more patience to adjust, and can make your blood pressure drop too low (making you dizzy). But it's another tool in the toolkit when the first two don't work.

**Bupropion (Wellbutrin)** is an antidepressant that also happens to help ADHD a bit. It's mostly useful if you have ADHD plus depression or anxiety, because one pill can help two problems. If ADHD is your only problem, there are better choices.

The big lesson: stimulants are not the only answer, and nonstimulants are not just "weak stimulants." They are genuinely different tools that fix different parts of the same factory — and which one you need depends on the specific way your factory is broken.
