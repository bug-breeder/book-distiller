# Chapter 22: Rule 7: Make Problems External, Physical, and Manual

## 🧠 Core Thesis
Because ADHD impairs working memory — the mental workspace needed to hold, juggle, and manipulate information — adults with ADHD must stop trying to solve problems inside their heads and instead offload thinking onto the physical world through visual, tactile, and auditory external tools.

## 📖 Detailed Breakdown

### The Working Memory Deficit at the Root of the Problem
- **What it is:** Working memory is the brain's ability to hold multiple pieces of information in mind simultaneously, manipulate them, and use them to reason, plan, and decide. ADHD damages both verbal working memory (holding and manipulating words and facts) and nonverbal working memory (holding and manipulating mental images).
- **Why it matters:** Almost every real-life problem requires working memory — weighing pros and cons, tracking steps in a multi-part task, analyzing options, forecasting consequences. When working memory is weak, none of these operations can be carried out reliably in the mind alone.
- **How it works:** Normally, the brain develops working memory during childhood, allowing people to take mental representations apart, recombine them, and generate solutions without external props. ADHD disrupts this development, leaving adults with a bottleneck between raw intelligence and practical execution. The person is not less intelligent — the machinery for internal mental manipulation is simply less reliable.
- **Key quote or example:** "It's not that you're incapable of logical analysis or you lack intelligence — far from it. It's just that you need to make the process tangible and external to move through it smoothly and successfully."
- **Connection:** This directly extends the book's foundational framework: ADHD is primarily a disorder of executive function, not attention or motivation. Working memory is one of the core executive functions affected, and Rule 7 is the direct compensatory strategy for that specific impairment.

### The Cascade from Working Memory Failure to Emotional Eruption
- **What it is:** When someone with ADHD tries to hold a complex problem in their head and the working memory system buckles, frustration builds rapidly. The emotional dysregulation that also accompanies ADHD then amplifies that frustration into overwhelm, shutdown, or angry outbursts.
- **Why it matters:** The failure is not just cognitive — it is also emotional. Trying to solve problems mentally when working memory is impaired is self-defeating in two ways: the problem doesn't get solved, and the emotional cost is high.
- **How it works:** Internal mental effort without progress generates a sense of spinning in place. Because ADHD also impairs the ability to inhibit emotional reactions, the frustration of cognitive stalling spills outward faster than it would in a neurotypical person. Externalizing the problem removes much of the cognitive strain, which in turn prevents the frustration spiral from starting.
- **Key quote or example:** "You need to make the problem external so that trying to solve it doesn't rely so much on your working memory — and so your emotions don't erupt with the frustration of trying to do it all in your head."
- **Connection:** This links Rule 7 to the book's earlier material on emotional self-regulation and impulsivity. The two problems feed each other; the solution addresses both simultaneously.

### Externalizing as a Universal Human Strategy — Not a Crutch
- **What it is:** Using physical tools to solve problems is not a workaround invented for people with disabilities — it is how humans have always solved complex problems. Blueprints, mockups, simulations, index cards, sticky notes, and whiteboards are all professional-grade externalizing tools used by engineers, architects, decorators, and tradespeople.
- **Why it matters:** Many adults with ADHD internalize the shame message that "by now you should be able to do this in your head." Barkley dismantles that message by pointing out that externalizing is standard practice in every skilled profession. Using external aids is not a sign of weakness — refusing to use them when your brain needs them is simply counterproductive.
- **How it works:** When a problem is represented physically — on paper, on cards, as objects, in a diagram, on a screen — the person can use their eyes, hands, and body to interact with the information rather than straining to keep it in a mental buffer that keeps dropping pieces. The senses become an extension of the mind.
- **Key quote or example:** Barkley describes an adult with ADHD who was designing a kitchen for his new house. Rather than trying to visualize the layout mentally or on paper alone, he built a full-scale mockup from moving boxes and physically walked through it — cooking, unloading groceries, reaching the fridge, loading the dishwasher — then revised the floor plan based on what he discovered. This is not "playing." It is rigorous, embodied problem solving.
- **Connection:** This reframes the stigma addressed in earlier chapters about ADHD adults being called immature or childish for needing props. The "props" are professional tools reframed for daily life.

### Rule 7 as an Expansion of Rule 2
- **What it is:** Rule 2 (discussed earlier in the book) advised externalizing things you need to remember — writing them down, posting signs, creating physical cues so memory doesn't have to do all the work. Rule 7 extends the same logic from remembering to problem solving.
- **Why it matters:** The principle is the same — get information out of the fragile internal system and into the stable external world — but Rule 7 applies it to the active, generative process of working through a problem rather than just preserving a fact.
- **How it works:** Rule 2 is about storage (don't rely on memory to hold information). Rule 7 is about processing (don't rely on working memory to manipulate information). Together they cover the two main jobs of working memory: retaining and operating on information. The tools for both overlap: paper, cards, devices, diagrams, physical objects.
- **Key quote or example:** "Think of this as the 'advanced' version of Rule 2."
- **Connection:** This explicit callback anchors Rule 7 in the book's cumulative structure and makes clear that the rules build on each other rather than standing alone.

### Identifying Where Your Problems Are Hardest
- **What it is:** Before choosing external tools, the person with ADHD needs to identify the categories of problems that defeat them most often — work (budgeting, relationships with supervisors, teamwork), home (conflicts with partner, parenting), social life (dating, friendships, community), or practical domains (repairs, shopping, dealing with vendors).
- **Why it matters:** External aids are not one-size-fits-all. A tactile learner benefits most from physical objects they can move; a visual learner benefits from diagrams and written lists; an auditory learner may need to record their own voice talking through the problem. Matching the tool to both the problem type and the person's perceptual strengths is what makes the strategy work.
- **How it works:** Self-assessment is the first step. Barkley provides a structured set of questions across work, home, social, and practical domains to help the reader locate their highest-friction problem areas. Once the problem terrain is mapped, the person can build a toolkit proactively rather than scrambling in the moment.
- **Key quote or example:** "Knowing where you're most paralyzed by problems will help you develop the external aids most likely to make your life easier."
- **Connection:** This mirrors the book's consistent pattern of asking the reader to assess their own functioning before prescribing a strategy, reinforcing that ADHD management is individualized.

### The Perceptual Learning Style Factor
- **What it is:** People differ in which senses they learn through most effectively — visual (seeing material laid out), auditory (hearing it), tactile (touching and manipulating objects), or kinesthetic (learning through physical movement). Adults with ADHD, who must rely on their senses more heavily than neurotypical people, need to know their strongest perceptual channel and match their external tools to it.
- **Why it matters:** An auditory learner using only written notes may still struggle because the modality doesn't engage their strongest channel. A kinesthetic learner may need to walk through a problem physically (as in the kitchen mockup example) rather than just write about it.
- **How it works:** Once a person identifies their dominant learning mode, they can select tools that feed that mode: voice memos and talking problems through aloud for auditory learners; index cards, whiteboards, and diagrams for visual learners; 3D models, physical mockups, and tactile sorting systems for tactile/kinesthetic learners.
- **Key quote or example:** Barkley lists concrete tools used by adults he knows: computers for lists, sticky notes, magnets on a board, audiotapes, and graphic devices — the diversity of tools reflecting the diversity of perceptual strengths.
- **Connection:** This connects to the broader ADHD management principle throughout the book: compensatory strategies must be tailored to the individual, not applied uniformly.

### The Shame History and Permission to Use Tools
- **What it is:** Many adults with ADHD carry memories of being denied physical aids in school — beads for arithmetic, manipulatives for math — because educators believed children "should" be doing the work mentally by a certain age. This left lasting shame and an internalized prohibition against using tools.
- **Why it matters:** That internalized prohibition actively works against effective ADHD management. Adults must consciously discard it. Barkley grants explicit permission: "You're an adult now, and no one can tell you what tools you're allowed to use to solve the problems that come your way."
- **How it works:** The mechanism is reframing. The person who reaches for index cards, a whiteboard, or a physical mockup is not regressing to childish behavior — they are doing the same thing architects, engineers, and surgeons do when they use tools to extend their cognitive reach. The shame is historically conditioned and factually unwarranted.
- **Key quote or example:** "If they help you figure out where to spend your paycheck, how to talk your boss into a raise when company funds are tight, how to get a term paper done on time, how to keep the kids from bickering, or which home improvement project to tackle first, it's your obligation to use them."
- **Connection:** This continues the book's running theme of rehabilitating self-perception: ADHD adults are not lazy or deficient in character; they are wired differently and need different tools.

## 🔑 Key Takeaways

1. ADHD weakens working memory — the mental workspace that holds and manipulates information — making in-head problem solving unreliable regardless of intelligence.
2. The solution is not to try harder mentally but to move the problem out of your head and into the physical world where your senses can engage with it.
3. Trying to force mental problem solving when working memory is failing does not just fail cognitively — it also triggers emotional frustration and dysregulation, compounding the original problem.
4. Externalizing is not a crutch — it is the standard practice of every skilled professional who works with complexity: blueprints, mockups, simulations, and physical models are how humans solve hard problems.
5. Rule 7 extends Rule 2: Rule 2 externalizes memory (what you need to remember); Rule 7 externalizes processing (what you need to think through).
6. The tools that work best depend on your perceptual learning style — visual, auditory, tactile, or kinesthetic — so match the tool to your dominant channel.
7. Identifying which life domains defeat you most (work, home, social, practical) lets you build your external toolkit proactively rather than scrambling under pressure.
8. The schoolroom prohibition on physical aids ("by your age, you should do that in your head") installed unjustified shame. As an adult, you have both the right and the obligation to use every tool available.
9. The physical act of manipulating information — moving cards, rearranging sticky notes, walking through a mockup — engages the brain in ways that pure mental effort cannot replicate for someone with ADHD.
10. If those around you find your use of physical problem-solving tools strange, that is their misunderstanding, not your deficiency (Barkley even adds: "invoke Rule 8" — don't worry about others' judgments).

## 🗺️ Mental Model / Framework

Think of working memory as a small, crowded workbench. A neurotypical person has a medium-sized bench: they can spread out a dozen pieces at once, move them around, and keep track of all of them. An adult with ADHD has a small, wobbly bench: they can hold maybe three or four pieces before others fall off the edge, and the effort of keeping things from falling off leaves almost no energy for actually working with the pieces.

Rule 7's solution is not to get a bigger internal bench (you can't redesign your brain by willpower). The solution is to move the project off the bench and onto the floor — literally. Spread the pieces out on a table, pin them to a wall, write them on cards, build a model. Now your workbench only has to hold the current decision point, not the entire problem. Your eyes and hands become extensions of your cognition. The floor (the external world) is infinite.

The before state: "I have to solve this complicated problem. I'll think it through." Pieces keep dropping. Frustration builds. Progress stalls.

The after state: "I have to solve this complicated problem. Let me get it out of my head first." The problem is now physical, visible, touchable. You move through it with your hands and eyes. Solutions emerge that your overburdened internal bench could never have reached.

## 💡 "Aha!" Moments

1. The frustration that adults with ADHD feel when trying to mentally solve problems is not impatience or weakness — it is a direct, predictable consequence of trying to run a process (working memory manipulation) on hardware that is not equipped for it at full load. The frustration is the system warning light, not a character flaw.

2. Externalizing problem solving is not an accommodation invented for disabled people — it is the baseline strategy of every sophisticated professional field. The architect's blueprint, the surgeon's model, the engineer's simulation, the decorator's floor plan cutouts are all just externalized cognition. People with ADHD simply need to do deliberately what other professionals do as standard practice.

3. The childhood memory of being denied manipulatives in school "because you should know this by now" may have done lasting damage to problem-solving confidence — not because the child lacked ability, but because the most effective tool was removed. Adults with ADHD are not recovering from lack of intelligence; in some cases they are recovering from well-intentioned but harmful instructional practices that forced them to use their weakest cognitive channel.

## 🔗 Connections to Other Chapters

Rule 7 is explicitly positioned as an "advanced version of Rule 2," which introduced the principle of externalizing memory. Where Rule 2 focused on retention (writing things down so you don't lose them), Rule 7 focuses on active processing (laying things out so you can work with them). The two rules together cover the full scope of working memory's job.

The chapter also connects directly to the book's foundational material on executive function deficits. ADHD is framed throughout the book as a disorder of executive functions — inhibition, working memory, planning, time sense — not merely attention. Rule 7 is the most direct practical response to the working memory component of that framework.

The emotional regulation connection is also significant: earlier chapters established that ADHD impairs the ability to manage frustration and emotional reactions. Rule 7 reduces cognitive load, which reduces the frustration that triggers emotional dysregulation. The two problems are downstream of the same neurological impairment, and solving one mitigates the other.

Barkley's closing line — "invoke Rule 8" when others raise eyebrows at your tools — sets up the next chapter's focus on managing social perception and not letting others' judgments derail effective self-management strategies.

## 📝 In My Own Words (ELI5)

Imagine your brain has a small desk where it does all its thinking. When you need to solve a problem, you put all the information on that desk and try to move things around until you figure out the answer. Most people have a pretty big desk. People with ADHD have a small desk with wobbly legs — pieces keep falling off, and you spend so much energy trying not to lose anything that you barely have energy left to actually think.

The solution is simple but not obvious: stop trying to do everything on that small internal desk. Instead, dump the problem onto the actual physical world. Write each piece on a sticky note and put it on the wall. Draw a diagram. Build a model out of cardboard boxes. Record yourself talking through it. Lay index cards out on a table and physically move them around.

Now your eyes and hands are helping your brain instead of your brain doing all the work alone. You can see everything at once. You can touch it. You can rearrange it. The problem gets solved not because you worked harder inside your head, but because you got smarter about using your body and your environment as extensions of your thinking.

Some kids with ADHD got told in school that they couldn't use counting beads or other physical helpers "because kids your age shouldn't need those." That was wrong. It was like telling a carpenter they can't use a measuring tape because skilled carpenters should be able to measure in their head. The tool is not the problem — it is the solution. As an adult, nobody gets to take your tools away. Use them. All of them. That is not cheating. That is just being smart about how your brain actually works.
