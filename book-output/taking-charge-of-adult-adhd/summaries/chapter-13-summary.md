# Chapter 13: Where Can You Go to Get Help?

## 🧠 Core Thesis
Finding help for ADHD requires navigating a fragmented professional landscape strategically — starting with your primary care physician and working outward through layered referral networks — and screening candidates with pointed questions before committing to an evaluation.

## 📖 Detailed Breakdown

### Starting with Your Primary Care Physician
- **What it is:** Using your existing relationship with a family doctor, internist, or general practitioner as the first entry point into the ADHD diagnostic pathway.
- **Why it matters:** A doctor who already knows your history can rule out physical causes of ADHD-like symptoms (thyroid issues, sleep disorders, medication side effects) without requiring a redundant medical workup by the mental health evaluator. This saves time and money.
- **How it works:** The physician administers screening questionnaires to gauge whether a full specialist evaluation is warranted. If physical causes are ruled out, the doctor can refer you to a specialist — and because the referral comes from someone who knows both you and the local professional community, the match is more likely to be a good fit.
- **Key quote or example:** "In my experience, when you like and trust your doctor, you have a good chance of feeling the same way about anyone the doctor recommends."
- **Connection:** This ties directly to earlier chapters on the diagnostic process — Barkley emphasizes that a proper ADHD evaluation must rule out medical mimics, and starting with a PCP accomplishes part of that work upfront.

### The Referral Network: Where to Find an ADHD Specialist
- **What it is:** A tiered set of channels for locating professionals with demonstrated expertise in adult ADHD, ranging from professional associations to peer recommendations to online directories.
- **Why it matters:** ADHD specialists are not uniformly distributed, and general mental health practitioners frequently lack deep experience with adult ADHD specifically. Finding the wrong evaluator risks misdiagnosis or shallow treatment. The referral network exists precisely to surface the right people.
- **How it works:** Barkley provides a concrete checklist of sources, ordered roughly by reliability and specificity:
  1. State psychiatric (psych.org) or psychological (apa.org) associations — they maintain specialty-organized directories.
  2. CHADD (chadd.org) and ADDA (add.org) — the two major U.S. nonprofit ADHD advocacy organizations, which often maintain local chapters and professional referral lists.
  3. University medical school psychiatry departments — even remote ones know their regional referral ecosystems.
  4. Local hospital psychiatry departments and university psychology clinics.
  5. County mental health centers.
  6. Online searches targeting your city or state, including additudemag.com.
  7. Trusted personal contacts — friends or family already being treated for ADHD, or parents of children with ADHD whose pediatric specialist may also treat adults or know who does.
- **Key quote or example:** "Do you have a friend or relative you trust who is being treated for adult ADHD? If so, ask for a referral to that person's practitioner."
- **Connection:** This section operationalizes the book's broader argument that ADHD is underdiagnosed in adults partly because adults don't know where to look — knowing the map is itself a therapeutic act.

### Pre-Appointment Screening Questions
- **What it is:** A set of questions to ask a prospective evaluator before booking an appointment, designed to verify genuine adult-ADHD expertise rather than incidental familiarity with the disorder.
- **Why it matters:** Not every licensed psychiatrist or psychologist has meaningful experience with adult ADHD. An evaluator whose practice is primarily children, or who sees ADHD rarely, may miss adult presentations, under-weight comorbidities, or default to inadequate assessments. These questions filter for true specialists.
- **How it works:** The questions probe several independent dimensions of expertise and fit:
  - **Volume:** What percentage of the practice involves ADHD? What percentage are adults specifically?
  - **Tenure:** How long has the clinician been treating adults with ADHD?
  - **Credential type:** Is the specialty psychiatry, clinical psychology, neuropsychology, or behavioral neurology — the fields with direct ADHD jurisdiction?
  - **Board certification:** Is the clinician board-certified in their specialty? Board certification signals a higher bar than a state license alone.
  - **Continuity of care:** Does the evaluator also treat patients after diagnosis, or only diagnose and refer out?
  - **Ecosystem:** Are complementary resources (coaches, skills trainers, support groups) accessible nearby?
  - **Practical logistics:** How long is the wait for an appointment? What insurance is accepted?
- **Key quote or example:** "Board certification is a higher level of certification than a state license to practice medicine or psychology."
- **Connection:** These questions link forward to treatment chapters — knowing in advance whether the evaluator also provides treatment, or whether coaching and skills training are co-located, shapes the treatment plan you'll be able to build.

## 🔑 Key Takeaways
1. Your primary care physician is the smartest first call — they can rule out physical causes and give a warm referral to a specialist who is likely to be a good fit.
2. CHADD (chadd.org) and ADDA (add.org) are the two most direct nonprofit resources for finding adult ADHD specialists; use them before generic search engines.
3. University medical school psychiatry departments are underused resources — even if they're far away, they know the regional referral landscape.
4. Personal referrals from trusted people already in treatment carry outsized reliability, because you can vet the experience directly.
5. A state license to practice is the floor, not the ceiling — board certification signals meaningfully deeper expertise.
6. An evaluator who primarily treats children may not have adequate experience with adult ADHD presentations, which differ substantially from childhood presentations.
7. Asking whether the practitioner also provides treatment (not just diagnosis) is critical — fragmented care with a diagnosis-only evaluator adds friction to the treatment journey.
8. The ecosystem around the evaluator matters: co-located coaches, skills trainers, and support groups accelerate outcomes beyond medication alone.
9. If one referral channel comes up empty, try another — these channels are not redundant, they tap different professional networks.
10. Beginning the search before a full crisis hits allows you to be selective; desperation narrows your options.

## 🗺️ Mental Model / Framework
Think of finding an ADHD specialist like finding a good contractor for a major home renovation. You wouldn't hire the first name in the phone book — you'd ask your neighbor who already had their kitchen remodeled, call the local contractors' association, check for licensing and certifications, and ask pointed questions before signing anything.

The search follows a funnel:
- **Wide net (directories, websites, online searches):** Generates a list of candidates.
- **Filter by specialty depth (ADHD percentage of practice, adult vs. child ratio, years of experience):** Narrows to genuine experts.
- **Filter by credentials (board certification, specialty field):** Ensures professional rigor.
- **Filter by practical fit (insurance, wait time, continuity of care, treatment ecosystem):** Ensures you can actually use this person.

The PCP call is the shortcut that collapses much of this funnel — a trusted doctor's referral jumps you past the wide-net phase and into the filter-by-fit phase immediately.

## 💡 "Aha!" Moments
1. **The child's doctor as a backdoor to adult care.** Most adults searching for ADHD help think only about adult-focused resources. Barkley points out that pediatric ADHD specialists often know adult ADHD clinicians, or have themselves expanded into adult practice. If you know a parent whose child is treated for ADHD, that's an untapped referral channel most people never think to use.

2. **Diagnosis-only practices are more common than you'd expect.** Many evaluators specialize purely in assessment and then refer out for treatment. This split-care model isn't inherently bad, but discovering it only after your evaluation means scrambling to find a treatment provider separately. Asking the question upfront lets you decide whether you want an integrated provider from the start.

3. **Liking your doctor predicts liking their referral.** This is a psychologically astute observation Barkley makes almost in passing: therapeutic alliance — the quality of the relationship between clinician and patient — is one of the strongest predictors of treatment success. A doctor who knows you well enough to have earned your trust is also well-positioned to match you with someone whose communication style and approach will work for you. The referral isn't just logistical; it's relational.

## 🔗 Connections to Other Chapters
This chapter is a direct bridge between diagnosis and treatment. Earlier chapters establish what ADHD is, how it manifests in adults, and why getting an accurate diagnosis is non-negotiable — this chapter answers the practical "now what?" question that follows from that understanding.

The emphasis on board certification and specialty fields (psychiatry, clinical psychology, neuropsychology, behavioral neurology) connects forward to treatment chapters: these are the same professionals who will oversee medication management and psychological interventions. Knowing their qualifications upfront matters because different specialist types have different scopes of practice — psychiatrists can prescribe medication; psychologists typically cannot (in most U.S. states).

The mention of coaches, skills trainers, and support groups as part of the ecosystem anticipates later chapters on multimodal treatment — Barkley's framework throughout the book is that medication alone is insufficient and that behavioral, cognitive, and social supports are essential complements.

## 📝 In My Own Words (ELI5)
Imagine you've just found out you might have a learning difference that's been making your whole life harder, and now you need to find an expert who actually understands it. The problem is, not every doctor who says they treat that condition really specializes in it — some have only seen a handful of cases, and mostly in kids, not adults.

This chapter is basically a treasure map for finding the right expert.

Start with your regular doctor — the one who already knows you. They can run some quick checks, rule out other possible causes, and point you toward someone they trust. When your doctor recommends someone, you're more likely to actually like that person too, because your doctor knows your personality.

If your doctor doesn't know anyone, there are backup paths: call the professional associations (like a psychiatrists' club or psychologists' club for your state), check the websites of the two big ADHD nonprofits (CHADD and ADDA), call a nearby university hospital, or ask a friend who's already getting treated.

Once you have a name, don't just book the appointment — ask questions first. How much of their practice is actually adult ADHD? Have they been doing this for years, or just a few months? Are they board certified (the gold standard for expertise)? Will they also treat you after diagnosing you, or will they hand you off to someone else?

It's like checking reviews and asking for references before hiring someone to do an important job. A little homework upfront saves a lot of frustration later.
