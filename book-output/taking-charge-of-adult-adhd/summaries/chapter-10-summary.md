# Chapter 10: Step One — To Get Started, Get Evaluated

## Core Thesis
Chapter 10 is a structural interstitial — a section-opening gateway, not a standalone argument. Its single function is to mark the transition into Step One of the book's practical program and to immediately ground that transition in the lived reality of adults with ADHD through five raw, first-person voices.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Interstitial as a Deliberate Rhetorical Device
- **What it is:** Chapter 10 contains no analytical prose, no instructions, and no argument. It consists entirely of the "Step One" section label and five extended first-person quotations from adults who have ADHD. It is a prologue to the three substantive chapters that follow (Chapters 11, 12, and 13), which together form the full content of Step One.
- **Why it matters:** Barkley uses this device to shift the reader's stance from information-receiving to self-recognition. Before telling the reader what to do, he lets other adults with ADHD speak. The effect is identification: a reader who has been wondering "do I really have ADHD?" encounters voices that sound exactly like their own interior monologue. This is emotionally and diagnostically priming work.
- **How it works:** The five quotes are chosen to represent distinct symptom clusters — time blindness, mental disorganization, impulsivity and task-switching, the social pain of childhood ADHD, and disinhibited stimulus-driven behavior in adulthood. Together they form an experiential map of adult ADHD before any clinical framing is applied. The reader is invited to locate themselves inside these stories before being given the clinical vocabulary to describe what they are experiencing.
- **Key quote or example:** "I just go with the flow of what's happening around me and can't remember what it was I was supposed to get done, or I just blow it off as less interesting than what I might have a chance to do." This quote — the lawn-mowing story — is the most vivid: a man leaves home to get gas, runs into a friend, goes fishing, ends up at a bar, and returns hours later to find police at the gas station because his wife reported him missing. His car was left there. The original task — mowing the lawn — was completely displaced by moment-to-moment environmental stimuli. This is not carelessness or rebellion. It is the ADHD executive system failing in real time.
- **Connection:** Each quote anticipates a specific concept that Step One's substantive chapters will address. "Time escapes me" maps to Barkley's core theory of ADHD as time blindness. "My mind and my life are a jumbled mess" maps to working memory and organizational deficits. "I flit from one thing to another" maps to impulsive task-switching and the need to act immediately or lose the impulse entirely. The childhood quote maps to the diagnostic requirement that symptoms must have originated in childhood. The lawn-mowing story maps to stimulus-driven behavior and the absence of goal-directed persistence.

### The Five Voices as a Composite Portrait of Adult ADHD
- **What it is:** A curated set of five testimonials that collectively portray the subjective experience of adult ADHD across time management, organization, impulsivity, social history, and disinhibited real-world behavior.
- **Why it matters:** The diagnostic criteria for ADHD (covered in detail in Chapter 11) are clinical abstractions. These quotes translate those abstractions into lived experience. A reader who recognizes themselves in these stories is primed to engage seriously with the evaluation process that Step One recommends.
- **How it works:** Each quote targets a different domain of adult life affected by ADHD. The first quote ("Time escapes me") captures the experience of temporal disorientation — not merely being late, but existing in a perpetual present with no felt connection to the future. The second ("my mind and my life are a jumbled mess") captures the organizational and cognitive chaos that is invisible to others but overwhelming from the inside. The third ("I flit from one thing to another") captures the paradox of impulsive action-taking: the person knows they must act immediately on a thought or it will vanish, which is why they appear unreliable — they are actually trying to compensate for their own forgetting. The fourth (the childhood quote) captures the emotional weight of being a child with ADHD — the social exclusion, the shame of daily medication rituals, and the eventual discovery of an identity that works. The fifth (the lawn-mowing story) captures adult ADHD's most disruptive feature in relationships: the person is not choosing to abandon responsibilities; they are being pulled by the environment and cannot re-anchor to the original intention.
- **Key quote or example:** "I know that I flit from one thing to another and one project to another, and it drives the people I have to work with crazy. But I have to do things as soon as I think of them, because if I don't, I'll forget about them, and then they never get done." This is one of the most psychologically precise self-descriptions of ADHD impulsivity in the book. The speaker has developed a folk theory of their own deficit — immediate action is a compensatory strategy for deficient working memory — even without clinical knowledge.
- **Connection:** The childhood quote connects directly to the diagnostic criterion covered in Chapter 11: ADHD must originate in childhood (before age 16). The speaker's detailed memory of social exclusion and daily medication supports the retrospective childhood onset that clinicians look for in adult evaluations.

### Step One as a Structural Signal
- **What it is:** The "Step One" label marks the beginning of the book's practical program. The book is organized into five steps: (1) Get Evaluated, (2) Understand ADHD, (3) Treat ADHD, (4) Use Strategies, (5) Manage Problems. Chapter 10 is the opening gate of Step One.
- **Why it matters:** The step structure signals that this book is not merely educational but prescriptive. Barkley is giving the reader a sequence of actions, not just a set of facts. Step One's imperative — "Get Evaluated" — is the first and most foundational action, because without a formal diagnosis, everything else in the book is provisional.
- **How it works:** By titling the step "To Get Started, Get Evaluated," Barkley makes getting a professional evaluation the precondition for all subsequent steps. This is a deliberate and important clinical stance: self-diagnosis from reading a book is insufficient, and the chapters that follow (11, 12, 13) will systematically explain why professional evaluation is necessary, how to find help, and what the evaluation process involves.
- **Key quote or example:** The step title itself — "To Get Started, Get Evaluated" — functions as a command. It is the action verb of the entire section.
- **Connection:** This step directly precedes Step Two (Understand ADHD) and Step Three (Treat ADHD). Without the evaluation that Step One prescribes, a reader cannot access the medication treatments Barkley presents in Step Three, cannot receive official accommodations under disability law, and cannot have comorbid conditions identified. Everything downstream depends on completing Step One.

## Key Takeaways
1. Chapter 10 is a structural gateway, not a content chapter — its value is rhetorical and emotional, not analytical.
2. The five anecdotes are clinically chosen to represent the core symptom domains of adult ADHD: time blindness, cognitive disorganization, impulsive task-switching, childhood social impairment, and stimulus-driven disinhibition in adult life.
3. Adults with ADHD often develop informal folk theories of their own deficits — as shown by the speaker who acts immediately on impulses specifically because they know they will forget otherwise. This is compensation, not recklessness.
4. The lawn-mowing story is the chapter's most powerful illustration: a man disappears for an entire afternoon not because he is irresponsible, but because his executive system cannot hold the original goal in place against competing environmental stimuli.
5. Step One's core command — "Get Evaluated" — establishes that everything else in the book's program depends on professional diagnosis first.
6. The childhood social experience of ADHD (shame, exclusion, medication stigma) is a recurring theme that informs why many adults arrive at diagnosis late and already carrying significant emotional damage.
7. The speaker who describes themselves as "that outgoing, sometimes loud, intense, somewhat nerdy, sarcastic, funny girl" illustrates something Barkley develops throughout the book: ADHD traits, while genuinely impairing, can coexist with a personality that others find energizing and attractive. Impairment and identity are not the same thing.
8. The three-part structure of Step One (chapters 11, 12, 13) follows a logical sequence: Am I symptomatic? Can I handle this alone? Where do I go for help? — Chapter 10 is the threshold before that sequence begins.

## Mental Model / Framework
Think of Chapter 10 as the anteroom of a clinic. Before you see the doctor, you sit in a waiting room with other patients. You overhear their conversations. Some describe your exact experience. Suddenly you feel less alone, and you understand more clearly why you are there.

The five voices in Chapter 10 are that waiting room. Barkley is not yet diagnosing anyone. He is not yet explaining anything. He is simply letting people who have been through what the reader is experiencing speak first. The effect is that the reader arrives at Chapter 11 (the actual clinical content of Step One) already motivated, already identified with the material, and already emotionally prepared to take the step being asked of them.

The underlying framework: Recognition precedes motivation. Before a person will seek an evaluation, they need to recognize themselves in the problem. These five voices are the recognition mechanism. The chapters that follow are the action mechanism.

## "Aha!" Moments
1. **The impulsive actor is actually a compensator.** The person who says "I have to do things as soon as I think of them, because if I don't, I'll forget about them" is not being impulsive for pleasure. They have intuited that their working memory is deficient and have built a behavioral strategy — act immediately — to work around it. What looks like impulsivity from the outside is a coping mechanism from the inside. This reframes the moral judgment that "impulsive" people are selfish or careless.

2. **Environmental pull, not character failure, explains the lawn-mowing story.** The man who ends up at a bar three hours after leaving to get gas did not choose to abandon his wife or his lawn. His executive system could not maintain the original goal in the face of a compelling real-time alternative. This is what Barkley means by "stimulus-driven behavior" — when the environment becomes more salient than internal intentions, the ADHD brain follows the environment. Understanding this mechanism changes how family members, partners, and employers should interpret "unreliability" in adults with ADHD.

3. **The childhood social wound is still present in the adult.** The woman who describes being "that outgoing, sometimes loud girl" is describing an adult identity she has constructed — but embedded in her quote is a clear memory of being an outcast as a child, of the shame of daily medication, of not fitting in. Adult ADHD diagnosis often involves grieving a childhood that was harder than it needed to be. The clinical process of Step One is not just about present symptoms; it is also about finally naming what happened earlier in life.

## Connections to Other Chapters
- **Backward connection to Chapter 11 (Is It Possible That You Have ADHD?):** The five voices in Chapter 10 are the human faces of the diagnostic criteria Barkley presents in Chapter 11. The time blindness quote maps to his concept of ADHD as temporal nearsightedness. The impulsive task-switcher maps to the nine-symptom checklist. The lawn-mowing story maps to the impairment domains (work, family, relationships).
- **Backward connection to Chapters 1-9 (the book's science section):** Chapters 1-9 explain what ADHD is neurologically and scientifically. Chapter 10 marks the pivot from "here is the science" to "here is what you do about it." The anecdotes bridge that pivot by making the science personal.
- **Forward connection to Chapter 12 (Can You Handle the Problem on Your Own?):** The lawn-mowing story implicitly answers the question of Chapter 12: no, adults with this severity of ADHD cannot handle it alone. The social consequences (wife calling police, marital silence) are exactly the kind of impairments that professional evaluation and treatment can address.
- **Forward connection to Chapter 13 (Where Can You Go to Get Help?):** The childhood quote's reference to daily medication at the nurse's office foreshadows Chapter 13's discussion of professional resources, prescribing clinicians, and the role of medication in treatment.
- **Forward connection to Step Three (Medication):** The mention of childhood medication in the fourth quote anticipates Barkley's argument in Step Three that medication is the most evidence-based intervention for ADHD, and that many adults benefit enormously from finally receiving it after years without it.

## In My Own Words (ELI5)
Imagine you have been struggling your whole life — losing track of time, forgetting what you were doing, starting a hundred things and finishing almost none of them, saying things before you think, getting distracted by anything more interesting than the thing you were supposed to be doing. You have always assumed this was a character flaw. Maybe you are just lazy. Maybe you do not care enough. Maybe you are broken in some unfixable way.

Now imagine opening a book and reading five short stories — each one written by a real person describing their own life. And with each story, you feel a jolt of recognition. That is me. That is exactly what happens to me.

That is Chapter 10.

It is not trying to teach you anything yet. It is not explaining ADHD or giving you strategies. It is simply showing you five people who live inside the same kind of brain you do, in their own words, before any doctor or scientist has explained it to them. One person talks about being blind to time. One talks about needing to act on every idea immediately or lose it forever. One talks about being a kid who everyone thought was too loud and too much — and how she eventually found a way to be proud of that. One talks about how his mind is a jumbled mess that he cannot organize. And one tells the story of going to get gas and not coming home for three hours because his friend showed up and then there was fishing and then there was a bar — and his wife was so worried she called the police.

The point of all this is simple: before the book tells you to go get a professional evaluation, it wants you to feel certain enough that you might need one. It wants you to nod and say, "yes, that sounds like me." Because if you do not recognize yourself in these voices, you might skip the most important step of all — which is getting a real diagnosis from a real professional. And everything else in this book — the strategies, the medications, the coping techniques — only works properly if that first step happens.

Think of Chapter 10 as the waiting room before the first appointment. The real appointment starts in Chapter 11.
