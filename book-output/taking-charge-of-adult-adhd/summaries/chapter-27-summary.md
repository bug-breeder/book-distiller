# Chapter 15: What to Expect from Treatment

## 🧠 Core Thesis
ADHD medication is extraordinarily effective — with up to an 80–90% overall response rate — but finding the right drug, dose, and combination requires a structured, patient, iterative process in partnership with your doctor, and medication alone will never solve every problem in your life.

## 📖 Detailed Breakdown

### Pre-Medication Medical Clearance
- **What it is:** Before starting any ADHD medication, a physician conducts a physical exam focusing specifically on heart rate, blood pressure, and current medications.
- **Why it matters:** Stimulants increase cardiovascular activity. Undetected heart conditions or dangerous drug interactions (e.g., combining stimulants with MAOIs, or stimulants with nicotine) can produce serious harm. This screening step protects the patient before treatment begins.
- **How it works:** The doctor reviews cardiovascular history for the patient and their family. An ECG may be ordered — not always required, but described as an "inexpensive precaution" worth doing if you haven't had one recently. Current medications are cross-checked for interactions. Nicotine (from smoking) is specifically flagged because it also acts as a stimulant, and stacking the two can spike heart rate and blood pressure.
- **Key quote or example:** "Once you've started medication, immediately tell your doctor if you experience any significant heart functioning problems — palpitations, rapid heart rate, chest pain, dizziness, or fainting."
- **Connection:** This step directly follows the drug-safety information from Chapters 13 and 14, where stimulants and nonstimulants were described in detail. The pre-medication exam is the real-world gateway from understanding the drugs to actually using them.

### The Stimulant vs. Nonstimulant Decision
- **What it is:** The first major clinical decision is whether to start with a stimulant (methylphenidate- or amphetamine-based) or a nonstimulant like atomoxetine (Strattera) or guanfacine XR.
- **Why it matters:** These drug classes work through different mechanisms, carry different side-effect profiles, respond to different patient histories, and are governed by different prescription regulations. Choosing the wrong class wastes weeks and may harm the patient.
- **How it works:** Most doctors default to stimulants first for uncomplicated ADHD, especially when rapid improvement is urgently needed (job on the line, relationship failing, college failure imminent). However, Barkley provides a 15-item checklist of factors that shift the decision toward nonstimulants. Factors favoring atomoxetine include: prior bad response to stimulants, comorbid anxiety or depression, tic disorder, significant insomnia, drug abuse history (in the patient or household), concerns about Schedule II diversion (especially relevant for college students), morning irritability on stimulants, and emotional blunting on stimulants. The more "yes" answers on the checklist, the stronger the case for going nonstimulant first — or for combining both at lower doses to broaden coverage while minimizing side effects from either drug alone.
- **Key quote or example:** "Most doctors are likely to try a stimulant first if you don't seem to have another disorder besides ADHD, or it's urgent or a priority to get relatively rapid control over your ADHD."
- **Connection:** The checklist synthesizes the drug comparison content from Chapters 13 and 14 into a practical decision tool. It also foreshadows the comorbidity theme — that ADHD rarely travels alone.

### Finding the Right Dosage: The Titration Process
- **What it is:** Starting at the lowest effective dose and incrementally increasing it every week until symptoms are controlled or side effects prevent further increases — a process called titration.
- **Why it matters:** Dosage does not correlate with body weight in a predictable way. Some adults need child-level doses; others need doses far above average. There is no blood test that reveals the "correct" dose. Only systematic trial under physician supervision works.
- **How it works:** The doctor starts low and escalates weekly. Finding the optimal dose can take 2–3 weeks to 1–2 months. The statistical picture is encouraging: only a 10–25% chance of not responding to the first drug tried, and only a 3–10% chance of being entirely unable to tolerate it. If the first drug fails, the doctor progresses through a logical sequence: switch delivery systems within the same drug class (e.g., from Ritalin LA to Concerta to Daytrana), then cross to a different stimulant class (e.g., from methylphenidate to amphetamine-based Adderall XR or Vyvanse), then try nonstimulants (atomoxetine, guanfacine XR), and finally move to off-label options like bupropion only after exhausting FDA-approved options.
- **Key quote or example:** "You have about a 75% chance of responding to whatever ADHD drug is tried first... Overall, you have an 80–90% chance of responding positively to one of the various ADHD medications on the market."
- **Connection:** This process is the operational complement to the drug catalog in earlier chapters. It also introduces a critical nuance: the delivery system (how the drug is absorbed over time) matters as much as the molecule itself. Failing one formulation does not mean failing that drug class.

### Treating the Most Impairing Condition First
- **What it is:** A triage principle — when ADHD coexists with a more dangerous or disabling condition (e.g., bipolar disorder, severe depression), the more serious condition gets treated first, before ADHD medication is added.
- **Why it matters:** Starting a stimulant in a patient with untreated bipolar disorder can trigger a manic episode. Treating ADHD symptoms while severe depression goes unaddressed often produces little functional improvement. The sequencing of treatment in complex cases determines whether treatment helps or harms.
- **How it works:** The doctor identifies the hierarchy of impairment and addresses it top-down. ADHD medication comes after — sometimes weeks or months after — stabilization of the primary condition. This is not a delay tactic; it is medical safety and therapeutic logic.
- **Key quote or example:** "Your doctor should always treat the most impairing or life-threatening condition first."
- **Connection:** This connects directly to the book's earlier discussion of ADHD comorbidities (anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder) and explains why ADHD diagnosis and treatment must always be considered in the context of the whole person, not just the ADHD symptoms.

### Ongoing Monitoring and Dynamic Dose Adjustment
- **What it is:** After finding an effective dose, treatment is not static. Regular follow-up visits allow for recalibration as life circumstances, weight, and demands change over time.
- **Why it matters:** A dose that works for one job, one life phase, or one body weight may become inadequate or excessive under different conditions. ADHD impairs functioning across all domains of life — not just at work — so the goal is continuous optimization, not a one-time fix.
- **How it works:** In the early weeks, the doctor may want weekly contact. Once a stable dose is established, follow-up is every 3–6 months. Stimulants (Schedule II controlled substances) require a new monthly prescription in most U.S. states — this mandatory check-in serves as a built-in monitoring mechanism. Dose adjustment is typically prompted by: significant weight gain or loss, a major life change (e.g., returning to college, a job promotion requiring more paperwork), or a measurable worsening of symptoms. Barkley provides a concrete example: a computer salesperson might need lower doses on days making customer calls (social, stimulating) but higher doses on days buried in paperwork (demanding sustained attention).
- **Key quote or example:** "We've found that people often need additional dosage adjustments after the first 3–6 months on their medication, whenever they've had an unusual weight gain or loss, or when their life circumstances have changed dramatically."
- **Connection:** This concept reinforces the book's core theme that ADHD is a disorder of self-regulation across the entire lifespan. Effective treatment must be as dynamic as the person's changing environment and demands.

### When to Take Medication — and When Not To
- **What it is:** Barkley recommends year-round, 7-days-a-week medication for most adults with ADHD, while acknowledging that rare, specific situations may justify planned medication holidays.
- **Why it matters:** Many adults believe medication is only for work or school — that they can skip weekends, vacations, or evenings. Barkley challenges this directly. ADHD impairs driving, parenting, financial management, and intimate relationships — none of which are exclusive to the workplace.
- **How it works:** Continuous treatment reflects the reality that ADHD's impairments do not clock out. The exceptions are narrow and case-specific: Barkley gives two vivid examples. A poet found her emotional range and creative imagery were blunted by medication, so she took medication most days but stopped on designated writing days. A cellist in a symphony found that medication reduced the emotional expressiveness that made him an outstanding musician, so he stopped medication on performance days but took it for routine days. These are not excuses to avoid medication — they are principled, deliberate decisions made in consultation with the doctor, grounded in self-knowledge.
- **Key quote or example:** "Doctors typically recommend that adults take their medication 7 days a week, year-round, because the impairments ADHD can produce occur not just at work or in college but in many other areas of life, such as driving, raising children, home responsibilities, managing money, and social relationships."
- **Connection:** This section challenges the popular misconception that ADHD is primarily a school or work disorder. It also introduces the tension between medication's cognitive benefits and its emotional-blunting side effect — a real phenomenon that Barkley acknowledges even without strong research evidence for it.

### Medication Does Not Fix Everything: Separating ADHD Problems from Life Problems
- **What it is:** A reality check — medication treats ADHD symptoms, but many problems in an adult's life are not caused by ADHD and will not be resolved by ADHD medication.
- **Why it matters:** Unrealistic expectations lead to disappointment and premature abandonment of treatment. Patients who expect medication to fix their marriage, career trajectory, or social life will be disappointed and may incorrectly conclude the treatment is not working.
- **How it works:** Barkley provides a diagnostic framework using three paired examples: forgetting to call your girlfriend (ADHD-driven) versus just being incompatible with her (not ADHD); being passed over for promotion because of poor communication (ADHD-related) versus lacking the skills needed for the next level (a gap that requires training, not medication); debt problems caused by impulsive overspending (ADHD) versus simply not earning enough money (not ADHD). The harder problem is that years of untreated ADHD create secondary damage — a reputation for unreliability, defensive reactions to criticism, resentment, and shoot-yourself-in-the-foot habits — that persist even after ADHD symptoms are controlled. Medication can quiet the ADHD, but cannot undo years of relational damage or retrain ingrained defensive behaviors.
- **Key quote or example:** "Living with untreated ADHD for a long time can leave you with a history of mistakes you didn't need to make. Those mistakes can leave other people expecting bad behavior and poor decisions from you... Even now that you can function better, you might have trouble looking objectively at what's causing your problems."
- **Connection:** This section directly bridges to the next concept — the need for therapy and coaching — and foreshadows Steps Four and Five of the book, which address daily functioning strategies. It also connects to the book's foundational argument that ADHD is a disorder of self-regulation, not simply a knowledge deficit.

### Therapy and Coaching as a Complement to Medication
- **What it is:** Cognitive-behavioral therapy, counseling, and ADHD coaching are valuable adjuncts to medication — but not substitutes for it.
- **Why it matters:** Even with optimal medication, some symptoms persist, some time periods are uncovered by medication, and the secondary damage from years of untreated ADHD requires deliberate repair work that medication cannot accomplish alone.
- **How it works:** Barkley is unambiguous that no scientific evidence supports therapy or coaching as a standalone treatment for ADHD — medication is the foundation. However, therapists and coaches help in three ways: (1) they address unique personal situations medication cannot touch, (2) they fill in gaps when medication coverage is incomplete, and (3) they help rebuild the habits, attitudes, and relationships damaged by years of untreated ADHD. The chapter ends by pointing readers toward Steps Four and Five of the book itself as a practical alternative or supplement to professional coaching.
- **Key quote or example:** "Please understand, though, that there is no scientific evidence this kind of treatment will suffice on its own. It can, however, help you face the specific, unique issues you're dealing with."
- **Connection:** This is the transition point from Part Three (medication) to Parts Four and Five (daily life strategies). It establishes medication as the necessary foundation upon which behavioral and cognitive strategies build. The entire second half of the book is framed as the non-medication component of treatment.

### The ADHD Symptom Tracking Scale
- **What it is:** A standardized self-report tool included at the end of the chapter, covering 18 core ADHD symptoms rated 0–3 (never/rarely to very often), plus a second section rating impairment across 10 life domains.
- **Why it matters:** Memory is unreliable, especially for adults with ADHD. Patients who rely on recall during brief office visits give imprecise information that makes dose optimization slower and less accurate. A written record closes that gap.
- **How it works:** The patient completes the scale before and after medication trials, or after dose changes. The doctor receives precise, consistent data rather than impressionistic self-reports. The scale covers all DSM ADHD symptoms (inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity) and maps their impact onto real-life domains: home life, social relationships, community activities, school, sports and clubs, driving, leisure, money management, romantic relationships, parenting, and daily chores. This last category is important — it confirms that ADHD impairment is pervasive, not domain-specific.
- **Key quote or example:** The scale can "give your doctor more precise information about how medication may be improving your symptoms and whether additional dose increases might be necessary. It can also help you remember to pass on to your doctor far more specific information than you might recall without recording it."
- **Connection:** The impairment section of the scale operationalizes the book's argument that ADHD is a whole-life disorder affecting driving, relationships, finances, and parenting — not just school and work performance.

## 🔑 Key Takeaways

1. You have a 75% chance of responding to the first ADHD medication tried, and an 80–90% overall chance of finding an effective treatment — the odds are strongly in your favor.
2. Finding the right medication is a titration process, not a single decision. Expect weeks to months of systematic dose adjustment before reaching the optimal regimen.
3. If one formulation fails, it does not mean the entire drug class fails — switching delivery systems (e.g., from Ritalin LA to Concerta) within the same molecule often produces dramatically different results.
4. Pre-medication cardiac screening is not bureaucratic box-checking; stimulants have real cardiovascular effects, and undetected heart conditions or nicotine use can make them dangerous.
5. When ADHD coexists with a more dangerous condition like bipolar disorder or severe depression, that condition must be treated first — starting a stimulant in an unstabilized bipolar patient can trigger mania.
6. Most adults with ADHD should take their medication 7 days a week, year-round — ADHD impairs driving, parenting, finances, and relationships, not only work performance.
7. Years of untreated ADHD create secondary damage (defensive habits, damaged relationships, poor reputation) that medication cannot undo on its own — therapy and coaching are the tools to address this layer.
8. Medication is the scientifically supported foundation; coaching and therapy are evidence-based adjuncts. Neither alone is sufficient — the combination is optimal.
9. Many problems in your life are not caused by ADHD and will not respond to ADHD medication. Learning to distinguish ADHD-driven problems from other life problems is a skill worth developing.
10. Track your symptoms with a standardized scale between appointments — it produces better clinical data than memory-based self-report and speeds up dose optimization.

## 🗺️ Mental Model / Framework

**The Treatment Funnel: From 80–90% Success to Whole-Life Repair**

Think of treatment as a funnel with four stages:

Stage 1 — Medical Clearance (narrow the safe options): Screen for heart problems, drug interactions, and comorbid conditions. This is the safety gate.

Stage 2 — Drug and Dose Selection (find what works): Start with the most likely candidate (stimulant for uncomplicated ADHD, nonstimulant if the checklist says so). Titrate the dose weekly. Progress logically through delivery systems, then drug classes, then off-label options if needed. 75% respond to the first drug; 80–90% find something that works.

Stage 3 — Dynamic Monitoring (keep it working): Life changes, weight changes, and demand changes require periodic recalibration. Medication is not set-and-forget. Use the tracking scale. Stay in contact with your doctor.

Stage 4 — Whole-Life Repair (address what medication can't fix): Medication treats the neurological deficit. It does not repair the habits, relationships, reputation, and thought patterns damaged by years of untreated ADHD. Therapy, coaching, and the strategies in Steps Four and Five of this book address that layer.

The funnel metaphor captures the progression: from population-level statistics (80–90% response rate) down to a specific individual's optimal regimen, and from pure neurology down to the complex social and behavioral life that neurology shapes.

## 💡 "Aha!" Moments

1. **The delivery system is not the same as the drug.** Many patients who "failed methylphenidate" actually failed one particular delivery mechanism — the way the drug is released into the bloodstream over time. Switching from Ritalin LA (pellets) to Concerta (osmotic pump) to Daytrana (skin patch) can produce entirely different clinical outcomes even though all three use the same active molecule. This reframes "I tried Ritalin and it didn't work" as "I tried one form of Ritalin" — a much smaller failure with many unexplored alternatives still available.

2. **Medication can restrict the very things that make some people extraordinary.** Barkley's poet and cellist examples are not anecdotes about medication failure. They reveal that ADHD medication's mechanism of action — narrowing and stabilizing attention and emotional range — can genuinely suppress the emotional breadth and creative associativity that underlies artistic excellence. For most people this is irrelevant. But for individuals whose livelihood and identity depend on precisely those capacities, it is a real tradeoff that deserves thoughtful management rather than dismissal.

3. **Years of untreated ADHD change the people around you, not just you.** Medication improves your behavior starting today. But the people in your life have formed expectations, emotional scar tissue, and defensive patterns based on who you were before. They may keep reacting to the old you even after you've changed. This means that behavioral improvement alone is often insufficient to repair relationships — the other people need to update their model of you, which takes deliberate communication and time, not just improved self-regulation.

## 📝 In My Own Words (ELI5)

Imagine your brain is like a car engine that runs rough — it doesn't idle smoothly, it stalls at stop signs, and it lurches when you try to accelerate steadily. ADHD medication is like a fuel additive that helps the engine run more evenly. But you have to find the right additive and the right amount for your specific engine.

Here's how it works in practice:

First, your doctor checks that your engine can actually handle the additive safely — they look at your heart and check for other chemicals already in your system that might react badly.

Then they pick the most likely additive to try first. For most people, stimulants (like Ritalin or Adderall) are the first choice because they work fast and well. For some people — those with anxiety, sleep problems, or a history of substance abuse — a different kind of medicine called atomoxetine might be a better starting point.

They start with a very small amount and increase it slowly every week, watching how you respond. Most people — about 75% — find the first medicine they try works pretty well. If it does not, they try a different version of the same medicine, or a completely different medicine. Overall, about 80–90% of people find something that works.

Once you find the right dose, you have monthly check-ins (because stimulants have strict rules, like how you need a new prescription every month) and then every few months after that. If you get a new job, gain weight, or your life gets more demanding, your dose might need adjusting — just like you'd tune up the car if you started towing a heavy trailer.

Here is the important part: even when the medicine works perfectly, it doesn't fix everything. Some problems in your life were caused by ADHD — forgetting to call people, missing deadlines, making impulsive decisions. Medicine helps with those. But other problems have nothing to do with ADHD — maybe you and your partner just want different things, or you genuinely don't have enough money for your lifestyle. Medicine won't fix those. And some problems were caused by ADHD in the past, but now they have taken on a life of their own — people expect you to be unreliable, and you've developed defensive habits. Medicine helps your brain, but it cannot change other people's memories or undo years of learned habits. That's where therapy and coaching come in — they are like the mechanic who helps you learn to drive the newly tuned car well.
