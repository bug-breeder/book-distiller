# Chapter 21: Rule 6: Break It Down … and Make It Matter

## 🧠 Core Thesis
Because ADHD cripples the brain's ability to sustain internal motivation over time, adults with ADHD must artificially manufacture external motivation by splitting tasks into micro-sized chunks, making their progress visible to another person, and rewarding themselves immediately after each small win — bringing the distant future close enough to feel real right now.

## 📖 Detailed Breakdown

### Why the Future Feels Hopelessly Far Away
- **What it is:** ADHD impairs the brain's sense of time and its ability to hold future consequences in mind with enough emotional weight to drive present action. Goals that require waiting, sequencing, or extended effort feel not just hard but cosmically remote — as if the deadline belongs to a different lifetime.
- **Why it matters:** Without understanding this root cause, adults with ADHD interpret their avoidance as laziness or character failure rather than a neurological deficit in prospective motivation. Understanding the "why" is the first step toward choosing better tools.
- **How it works:** The ADHD brain has a weakened ability to mentally simulate the future vividly enough to produce the emotional signals (anticipation, urgency, desire) that motivate action. A neurotypical person imagining a looming deadline will feel a rising anxiety that pushes them to start; a person with ADHD often feels nothing until the deadline is hours away. When that discomfort hits, it floods in all at once, producing panic rather than productive momentum.
- **Key quote or example:** "ADHD can make the future seem hopelessly distant. A goal that requires a significant investment of time, incorporates waiting periods, or has to be done in a sequence of steps can prove so elusive that you feel overwhelmed." The chapter lists the avoidance behaviors this triggers: going AWOL, clowning, offloading responsibility to others, regressing to dependence on a willing helper.
- **Connection:** This directly extends the book's core neurological framework — the executive function model introduced in earlier chapters — explaining why visualization and self-talk alone (Rule 5) are insufficient for many adults with ADHD. Internal tools need external scaffolding.

### Strategy 1 — Break It Down (Chunking)
- **What it is:** The deliberate, systematic decomposition of any multi-step or time-extended task into the smallest practical units of work — micro-quotas small enough that the ADHD brain can hold them in focus without losing the thread.
- **Why it matters:** The distance between "now" and a far-off deadline is the enemy of ADHD motivation. Chunking collapses that distance by replacing one enormous future goal with a series of immediate, concrete, achievable present tasks. Each chunk has its own near-term endpoint, which the ADHD brain can actually perceive.
- **How it works:** The size of the chunks scales inversely with the length of the project — the more distant the deadline, the smaller the chunks must be.
  - For an end-of-day deadline: break into 1-hour or 30-minute blocks. Write each block's task down and highlight the current one to keep attention anchored.
  - For an end-of-week deadline: assign tasks by day first, then break each day into 30-minute to 1-hour segments. Consult past project records, a boss, or colleagues to figure out the right sequence.
  - For very long projects: plan the entire day in 15-minute increments, covering not just work but meals, rest, and leisure — exactly as Olympic swimmer Michael Phelps (who has ADHD) did during the 2016 Summer Olympics, where his daily schedule was shown on television broken into 15-minute units from wake-up to bedtime.
- **Key quote or example:** "Just do five at a time and do that six times in a row with short breaks in between, and you've got your 30 problems done." The chapter uses the analogy of a child with ADHD and math homework: asking for 30 problems is overwhelming; asking for 5, then a break, then 5 more is completely manageable. Professional athletes with ADHD apply the same logic to weightlifting sets.
- **Connection:** Chunking is the operational heart of Rule 6. It works hand-in-hand with the accountability and reward strategies below; without the micro-structure chunking provides, neither of the other strategies has anything to attach to.

### Strategy 2 — Make Yourself Accountable to Another Person
- **What it is:** Intentionally creating a social witness — a coworker, supervisor, partner, spouse, or neighbor — who knows your plan, knows your chunks, and will hear from you when each chunk is completed.
- **Why it matters:** Social accountability taps a motivational channel that is mostly intact in adults with ADHD: caring about what others think. The internal motivation to avoid disappointing someone, and the internal reward of receiving admiration, both function as real-time emotional fuel in a way that abstract future consequences do not.
- **How it works:** Tell the accountability partner specifically what you plan to do and by when (the chunked plan). Then actively report back each time you complete a subgoal. This doubles the motivational force: the small task now has both a near-term endpoint (the chunk) and a social consequence (telling someone). The chapter notes that emotions are activated — "letting someone else down doesn't feel good; receiving admiration feels great" — which imports internal emotional motivation through an external mechanism.
  - At work: use a coworker, supervisor, or mentor.
  - At home: use a partner, spouse, roommate, or neighbor.
  - Reward the helper: lunches, small gifts, their favorite coffee. A helper who feels appreciated stays engaged and willing to keep coaching.
- **Key quote or example:** "We are far, far more likely to follow through on the goals we set for ourselves if we tell someone else what we're doing and how we plan to do it… and then notify that person when we achieve each smaller objective."
- **Connection:** This strategy mirrors the broader book theme of using the social environment as a prosthetic for weakened internal regulation. It is the interpersonal counterpart to environmental structure strategies discussed in other rules.

### Strategy 3 — Give Yourself Immediate Small Rewards
- **What it is:** Self-administered micro-rewards — brief, pleasurable activities or experiences — delivered immediately after completing each chunk of work. These rewards are not promised for the end of the full project; they are given after each subgoal.
- **Why it matters:** The ADHD brain is particularly responsive to immediate, concrete rewards and particularly unresponsive to delayed, abstract ones. Waiting until a whole project is done to feel good is exactly the temporal gap that ADHD breaks. Small, immediate rewards reconstruct a working reward loop that mimics the token economies used successfully with children with ADHD.
- **How it works:** Choose rewards that are quick, accessible, and genuinely enjoyable — not so large they derail the work session but large enough to feel like a real payoff. The author provides a personal example from writing the book itself: after every five pages, he would allow himself a cappuccino, a few minutes of bass guitar practice, a Hershey's Kiss, a Diet Coke, or a moment looking out the window at the marsh behind his home. The reward is small, immediate, and self-chosen.
- **Key quote or example:** "That's how I wrote this entire book — brick by brick." He also cites Anne Lamott's concept from "Bird by Bird": her brother had an overwhelming paper on birds, so their father told him to write just a few sentences about one bird, tell him about it, and repeat. One bird at a time.
- **Connection:** This strategy is the affective and motivational completion of the chunking structure. Chunking creates the micro-goals; accountability makes them social; rewards make them emotionally satisfying. Together, the three strategies form a complete external motivation system.

### Putting It All Together — The Four-Step Completion Ritual
- **What it is:** A four-action sequence to perform at the end of every chunk of work, not just the full project.
- **Why it matters:** The ritual formalizes the reward process and ensures that each small accomplishment registers in the brain as a genuine win. Without this ritual, even chunked work can feel like an endless treadmill.
- **How it works:** After completing each chunk:
  1. Congratulate yourself.
  2. Take a short break (a few minutes).
  3. Tell someone what you've accomplished (if someone is available).
  4. Give yourself a small, brief reward.
- **Key quote or example:** "Brick by brick" and "bird by bird" — both metaphors anchor the idea that large-scale accomplishment is always the accumulated product of individually small, managed moments of action.
- **Connection:** This ritual is the behavioral bridge between Rule 6 and Rule 7, which will introduce further ways the external environment can be structured to boost internal motivation.

## 🔑 Key Takeaways
1. The ADHD brain does not experience the future as motivationally real — chunking is not a preference or a productivity hack, it is a neurological necessity.
2. The further the deadline, the smaller the chunks must be. Chunk size is a dial you calibrate based on how remote the endpoint feels.
3. Writing down the chunk you're working on and highlighting it physically keeps attention anchored to the present task rather than scattered across the whole project.
4. Breaks between chunks are not laziness — they are a required part of the system, just as they are for children with ADHD doing homework.
5. Accountability is a motivational amplifier: social consequences and social rewards are largely intact in ADHD and can be deliberately recruited to supplement weak internal drive.
6. Rewards must be immediate and small — given after each chunk, not held until project completion. Delayed rewards are functionally invisible to the ADHD brain.
7. Rewarding your accountability partner is not optional courtesy — it is what keeps the support system alive and sustainable.
8. You do not need to feel motivated before starting; the reward system creates motivation as a product of doing, not a prerequisite for it.
9. Even the author wrote this book using the exact system he prescribes — the method is not theoretical but lived.
10. The mantra "CHUNK IT, BABY!" is meant to be posted in your workspace as a visible, external reminder — an environmental prompt rather than a mental one.

## 🗺️ Mental Model / Framework

Think of a long project as a dark hallway. A neurotypical person can see light at the far end and walk confidently toward it. A person with ADHD stands in the same hallway but cannot see past the first few feet — the far end is invisible and produces no pull.

Rule 6's three strategies are equivalent to three tools for navigating that hallway:

- **Chunking** = placing a lamp every few feet, so you only ever need to see the next lamp, not the distant exit.
- **Accountability** = having someone stand at the next lamp calling your name, creating a social pull toward the immediate goal.
- **Rewards** = placing something enjoyable at each lamp — a reason to get there that is real, present, and pleasurable right now.

The four-step completion ritual (congratulate, break, report, reward) is what you do each time you reach a lamp: you acknowledge it, rest briefly, tell the person waiting, and receive your reward before moving to the next lamp. The hallway does not get shorter, but it stops being dark and becomes a series of achievable, rewarding steps.

Before Rule 6: One distant destination. No intermediate milestones. No social witness. No rewards until the very end. Result: paralysis.

After Rule 6: Many close destinations. A witness for each one. A reward after each one. Result: sustained forward movement, even with ADHD.

## 💡 "Aha!" Moments

1. **The problem is not willpower — it is temporal perception.** Adults with ADHD are not failing to try hard enough; they are failing to perceive the future with enough emotional reality to generate the feeling of urgency that produces effort. Chunking is not a crutch — it is a tool that compensates for a genuine perceptual deficit. Understanding this reframes "why can't I just do it" from a character question to an engineering question: how do I rebuild the motivational signal that ADHD is blocking?

2. **Rewards do not have to come from a teacher or a boss — you can give them to yourself, deliberately and without guilt.** Most adults are taught that self-reward is indulgent or that you should derive satisfaction from the work itself. For adults with ADHD, waiting for intrinsic satisfaction is waiting for a bus that rarely comes. Self-administered micro-rewards are a legitimate, researched, and effective prosthetic for a weakened internal reward system. The author — a world expert on ADHD — explicitly describes rewarding himself this way while writing the very book in which he teaches it. Permission granted.

3. **The Michael Phelps detail is not about Olympic-level discipline — it is about how a person with ADHD actually achieved Olympic-level results.** The instinctive assumption is that elite athletes succeed through sheer willpower and internal drive. The chapter reveals that Phelps structured his entire day into 15-minute units — not because he was a productivity zealot, but because that level of external structure is what the ADHD brain actually needs to function at its best. The lesson: the more granular and externally visible the structure, the higher the ceiling of performance, not the lower.

## 📝 In My Own Words (ELI5)

Imagine you have to eat a whole birthday cake. Not a slice — the whole thing. Right now. That sounds awful and impossible, right? You'd probably just stare at it and not know where to start.

But what if someone cut it into tiny pieces? And said: eat one piece. That's it. Just one. Then you can take a little break. Then eat another piece. After each piece, you get a sticker (or whatever you like). And your best friend is keeping count and cheering for each piece you finish.

Suddenly, eating the whole cake doesn't feel impossible. You're not thinking about the whole cake anymore — you're thinking about this one piece, right in front of you.

That's exactly what Rule 6 teaches people with ADHD to do with every big task in their life.

Here is why people with ADHD need this more than most people: their brain has a hard time "seeing" the future. When you tell most people "finish this project by Friday," their brain automatically starts feeling a little nervous and motivated, because it can imagine Friday arriving and the project not being done. People with ADHD often don't get that feeling. Friday feels fake and far away. So they don't start. Then Friday arrives suddenly, and it's a disaster.

Rule 6 fixes this with three moves:

First, chop the project into tiny pieces — so tiny that each one can be done in 15 or 30 minutes. Write them down. Highlight the one you're working on right now. Forget about the rest for now.

Second, tell someone your plan. Tell them each time you finish a piece. This matters because even if your brain doesn't feel urgency about the project, it does feel something about what another person thinks of you. That feeling becomes your fuel.

Third, give yourself a tiny reward after each piece. Not after the whole project — after each little piece. A snack. A song. A two-minute walk. Something small that you actually enjoy.

This is how the author — one of the world's top ADHD experts — wrote the very book this chapter comes from. Piece by piece. Reward by reward. Bird by bird.

The whole point is this: you don't need to feel motivated before you start. You just need a small enough first piece, someone who knows what you're doing, and something enjoyable waiting for you on the other side of it. Do that enough times in a row, and the cake gets eaten.
