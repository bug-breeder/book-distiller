# Practice Exercises: Chapter 13 — 3. Where Can You Go to Get Help?

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why does Barkley recommend starting with your primary care physician rather than going directly to a mental health specialist, and what are the two practical advantages this step provides?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Starting with a primary care physician offers two concrete benefits. First, the physician can administer screening questionnaires and rule out physical causes of ADHD-like symptoms, potentially eliminating the need for a separate medical exam that a mental health evaluator would otherwise require. Second, a doctor who already knows you well is better positioned to match you with a compatible specialist — Barkley notes that trust in your referring doctor tends to transfer to trust in whoever that doctor recommends, making the therapeutic relationship more likely to succeed from the start.

</details>

**Q2:** What professional fields does Barkley identify as genuinely equipped to evaluate and treat adult ADHD, and why does board certification matter beyond a standard state license?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The relevant specializations are psychiatry, clinical psychology, neuropsychology, and behavioral neurology — fields whose training explicitly covers ADHD and its commonly co-occurring psychiatric disorders. Board certification matters because it represents a higher, voluntary credential above and beyond the baseline state license to practice. A state license establishes minimum legal competency; board certification signals that a practitioner has sought and passed additional peer review of their expertise, making it a useful signal when comparing otherwise similar candidates.

</details>

**Q3:** Barkley lists several types of organizations — state associations, university departments, hospitals, county mental health centers, and nonprofit ADHD organizations — as referral sources. What is the strategic logic behind offering this entire network rather than a single definitive resource?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

No single resource has comprehensive, current information on every qualified adult ADHD clinician in every geographic area. State psychiatric and psychological associations maintain specialty-organized directories but may be incomplete. University medical schools and hospital psychiatry departments know local practitioners but focus on their own referral networks. CHADD and ADDA chapters have community-level intelligence that formal institutions miss. By triangulating across all these sources, a person is far more likely to surface qualified clinicians — and if any one avenue fails (e.g., no local CHADD chapter, a university that doesn't list ADHD as a specialty), the others remain. The network approach is a resilience strategy against the fragmented state of ADHD specialist directories.

</details>

**Q4:** When calling a prospective clinician before making an appointment, Barkley recommends asking what percentage of the doctor's practice consists of adults with ADHD. What does this question actually reveal, and why is it more informative than simply asking "do you treat adults with ADHD?"

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Asking for a percentage reveals the depth and frequency of a clinician's real-world exposure, not just their nominal willingness to treat ADHD. A practitioner who says "yes" to treating adult ADHD but whose caseload is 5% ADHD, mostly children, is qualitatively different from one where 50% of adult patients have ADHD. Volume matters because ADHD presentation in adults is highly variable, comorbidities are common, and diagnostic nuance develops through repeated clinical experience. The percentage question also implicitly surfaces whether the clinician primarily treats children (relevant if they cite adult ADHD experience that is actually pediatric experience), since Barkley separately recommends asking what portion of their ADHD patients are adults.

</details>

**Q5:** Barkley mentions that most mental health practitioners will not have coaching, skills training, and support groups on-site, but that psychologists in private practice often work in shared office parks where related professionals refer to each other. What clinical need does this ecosystem address, and why does it matter for adult ADHD specifically?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Adult ADHD requires multimodal treatment — medication management, psychoeducation, behavioral skills training, and often coaching or support groups. A single clinician typically cannot provide all of these. The informal referral ecosystem of co-located professionals means that a diagnosing psychologist can direct patients to a nearby ADHD coach, skills trainer, or support group without the patient having to start a new search from scratch. For adults with ADHD, who often struggle with executive function tasks like initiating new searches and navigating bureaucratic systems, having a warm referral network dramatically lowers the barrier to accessing the full range of services their condition typically requires.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: Geographically isolated, no obvious specialists nearby**
You live in a small city three hours from the nearest major metropolitan area. A search for ADHD specialists in your city turns up nothing. You have a primary care physician but they've never mentioned ADHD. You want a proper evaluation.

*What should you consider?*
- Your primary care physician can still be a productive first contact — even if they don't specialize in ADHD, they may know regional psychiatrists or can use screening tools to document your symptoms.
- University medical school psychiatry departments, even distant ones, typically know regional practitioners and can point you toward the nearest qualified evaluator.
- CHADD's online professional finder and additudemag.com allow geographic searches that may surface clinicians within driving distance who don't appear in a basic local search.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Start by calling your primary care physician and framing the conversation explicitly: "I think I may have adult ADHD — can you do an initial screening and refer me to a specialist in the region?" This accomplishes two things: the physician may administer a brief questionnaire that documents your symptoms (useful for any future evaluation) and may know of a psychiatrist or psychologist within reasonable driving distance even if not in your immediate city. If that yields nothing, call the psychiatry department of the nearest university medical school — even by phone, they maintain awareness of regional practitioners and can name someone who does adult ADHD evaluations. Simultaneously, search chadd.org and additudemag.com using your state rather than your city as the search parameter. Finally, if you belong to any online ADHD forums or communities, a crowd-sourced recommendation from someone in your state can surface practitioners not listed in any formal directory.

</details>

---

**Scenario 2: Multiple practitioners available, unsure how to compare them**
You live in a large city and have found four possible clinicians through different channels: a psychiatrist, a clinical psychologist, a neuropsychologist, and a general therapist who mentions ADHD on their website. You need to decide who to call first.

*What should you consider?*
- All four fields are not equal in their relevance: psychiatry, clinical psychology, and neuropsychology are the fields Barkley specifically names as appropriate for ADHD; a general therapist is not on that list.
- Board certification distinguishes the depth of credentialing within any of these fields and is worth asking about directly.
- Asking each candidate about the percentage of their practice dedicated to adult ADHD will quickly reveal who has genuine depth of experience versus who occasionally treats it alongside an unrelated primary specialty.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Set aside the general therapist for now — while a therapist can play a role in ADHD treatment, they are not the appropriate starting point for a diagnostic evaluation. For the remaining three, call each and ask: what percentage of your practice is adult ADHD patients, how long have you been treating adults specifically (not just ADHD in children), and are you board certified in your specialty? A psychiatrist who is board certified and reports that 40% of their adult caseload is ADHD is a strong candidate; a neuropsychologist whose ADHD work is 5% of their practice and is mostly pediatric is less so. Also ask whether each practitioner treats patients after diagnosing them — a clinician who diagnoses and then immediately refers out means you'll need to build a relationship with yet another provider. Prioritize the clinician who combines high adult ADHD volume, board certification, and the ability to continue treatment after diagnosis.

</details>

---

**Scenario 3: Referred by a friend, but the clinician seems to see mostly children**
A close friend whose child was diagnosed with ADHD gives you the name of the child's psychiatrist, saying she is excellent. When you call the office, the receptionist confirms the doctor does see adults. You're tempted to just book the appointment.

*What should you consider?*
- A clinician who primarily treats children with ADHD may have limited experience with adult presentation, which looks meaningfully different — adult ADHD involves more inattention relative to hyperactivity, greater occupational and relational impairment, and more complex comorbidity patterns.
- Barkley specifically recommends asking what percentage of the clinician's ADHD patients are adults, precisely to address this situation.
- The friend's trust and the doctor's excellence with children are real signals but not sufficient evidence of expertise with adults — the additional screening questions exist for exactly this scenario.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Before booking, ask the two clarifying questions Barkley recommends: what percentage of the doctor's overall practice is ADHD, and of those ADHD patients, what percentage are adults? If the doctor's adult ADHD caseload is substantial and they have years of adult-specific experience, the friend's referral is probably sound and you should proceed. If the honest answer is that the practice is overwhelmingly pediatric, thank them and ask whether they can refer you to a colleague who focuses on adults — a well-connected pediatric ADHD specialist often knows adult ADHD specialists. This approach honors the friend's trust without compromising the quality of your own evaluation; you're not dismissing the referral, you're screening it appropriately.

</details>

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain why asking "what percentage of your adult patients have ADHD?" is one of the most important questions you can ask a prospective clinician, to someone who has never thought about finding a specialist before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

When you're looking for help with something as nuanced as adult ADHD, a clinician's title tells you very little about their actual depth of experience with your specific condition. Asking for a percentage forces the clinician to reveal whether ADHD is central to their daily work or just an occasional case they handle on the side — and those two situations produce vastly different levels of skill, up-to-date knowledge, and diagnostic sensitivity. Just as you'd want a surgeon who performs a particular operation frequently rather than once a year, you want a clinician for whom adult ADHD is a core focus, not a footnote.

</details>
