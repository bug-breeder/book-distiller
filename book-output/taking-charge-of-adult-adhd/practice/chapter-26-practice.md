# Practice Exercises: Chapter 14 — The Nonstimulants

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Atomoxetine primarily targets norepinephrine rather than dopamine, yet Barkley notes it may still improve dopamine functioning. How does this happen, and why does it matter for understanding who might respond better to atomoxetine versus a stimulant?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By blocking norepinephrine reuptake, atomoxetine also indirectly increases dopamine levels — but only in specific brain regions like the frontal cortex, not in the brain's reward centers. This selective dopamine effect means atomoxetine can address dopamine-related ADHD deficits in executive function areas without triggering the reinforcing, abuse-prone stimulation of reward pathways. It matters because ADHD involves neurological deficits that vary by individual: some people's ADHD is driven more by norepinephrine shortfalls, others more by dopamine shortfalls. Since atomoxetine primarily addresses norepinephrine (with targeted indirect dopamine effects), it may be a better fit for people whose specific brain chemistry aligns with that profile — which is why roughly 25% of patients respond better to atomoxetine than to stimulants.

</details>

---

**Q2:** Why does atomoxetine require a slower dose-titration process than stimulants, and what practical implication does this have for a newly diagnosed adult starting the medication?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Atomoxetine raises norepinephrine systemically, and increasing the dose too quickly causes noticeable side effects — principally nausea and drowsiness — that make rapid adjustment impractical. The body needs gradual adaptation over 2–4 weeks to tolerate an effective dose, whereas stimulants act immediately and their dose effects are apparent the same day. For a newly diagnosed adult, this means setting realistic expectations: the drug will not produce noticeable ADHD relief right away, and judging it a failure after one or two weeks is premature. Barkley frames patience as a reasonable trade-off given the medication's unique advantages (no abuse risk, all-day coverage, no sleep disruption).

</details>

---

**Q3:** Guanfacine XR works through an entirely different neurological mechanism than either stimulants or atomoxetine. Explain that mechanism and why it makes guanfacine a third-line rather than first-line treatment.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Rather than increasing neurotransmitter availability at synapses, guanfacine XR closes alpha-2 ports (valves along nerve cell axons) that, when open, weaken or disrupt signal transmission. By closing these ports, guanfacine strengthens the electrical signals moving along the axon, improving cell-to-cell communication. However, because this mechanism is less potent at addressing core ADHD deficits than increasing dopamine or norepinephrine directly, guanfacine is considered less effective than stimulants and somewhat less effective than atomoxetine. Combined with its antihypertensive side effects (dizziness, drowsiness, risk of fainting) and a lengthy dose-adjustment period, these limitations make it a third-line option — useful when both stimulants and atomoxetine have failed or are contraindicated.

</details>

---

**Q4:** Atomoxetine is not a controlled substance and stimulants are. What is the neurological reason for this difference, and what are the three concrete practical benefits that flow from that distinction?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Stimulants flood the brain's reward centers with dopamine, producing euphoria and making them attractive to people seeking a high — hence controlled-substance status. Atomoxetine increases dopamine only in the prefrontal cortex (not the reward/limbic system), so it produces no euphoric effect and research shows people with drug addictions find it no more appealing than ordinary antidepressants. The three practical benefits are: (1) prescriptions can be called in by phone, eliminating the need for in-person visits for refills; (2) patients do not need to take special precautions to secure the medication from household members; and (3) it is the treatment of choice for ADHD patients who have a personal or household history of substance abuse, since there is no diversion or misuse risk.

</details>

---

**Q5:** Bupropion is used off-label for ADHD. What does "off-label" mean, why might bupropion be preferred over atomoxetine in specific circumstances, and why is it generally not the first choice for someone whose only diagnosis is ADHD?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"Off-label" means a drug is prescribed for a condition for which the FDA has not formally approved it — an entirely legal and sometimes clinically superior choice, but one that carries less regulatory evidentiary backing. Bupropion raises norepinephrine (like atomoxetine) but also affects dopamine and serotonin, making it potentially useful when a patient has co-occurring anxiety or depression alongside ADHD, since a single drug can address multiple conditions simultaneously. However, that same broader neurochemical profile also means it produces different and potentially more varied side effects. For a patient with ADHD only, stimulants or atomoxetine have stronger evidence bases and more targeted mechanisms, so bupropion is reserved for cases where comorbidities tip the benefit-risk calculation in its favor.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Stimulant-Intolerant Professional**
Marcus, a 34-year-old project manager, tried two different stimulants for his ADHD over six months. Both produced significant insomnia — he was sleeping only four to five hours per night even when taking the medication before noon. His ADHD symptoms were well controlled on the stimulants, but the sleep deprivation was making his work performance worse, not better. His psychiatrist is now considering nonstimulant options.

*What should you consider?*
- Atomoxetine is specifically noted as causing far less insomnia than stimulants — how does its different mechanism explain this, and how should Marcus's psychiatrist counsel him on the 2–4 week adjustment window?
- Marcus needs coverage throughout his workday and into evening meetings. How does atomoxetine's pharmacokinetic profile (no extended-release variant needed) compare to what he experienced with stimulants?
- Are there any personal lifestyle or household risk factors that would further reinforce atomoxetine as the right choice here?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Atomoxetine is a strong candidate for Marcus. Its norepinephrine-targeting mechanism does not stimulate the arousal pathways that cause stimulant-induced insomnia, which is why insomnia is listed as far less common with this drug. The psychiatrist should set clear expectations: Marcus will not see meaningful ADHD improvement for 2–4 weeks while the dose is gradually titrated upward to avoid nausea and drowsiness, so he should not judge the drug a failure in the first two weeks. Importantly, because atomoxetine's effects last most of the day without requiring an extended-release formulation, Marcus gets the all-day coverage his demanding schedule requires — something that with stimulants necessitated extended-release versions or afternoon booster doses that compounded his sleep problems. Since his only clear barrier to stimulants was insomnia (not substance abuse or access concerns), the convenience benefits of atomoxetine (phone-in refills, no controlled-substance restrictions) are a secondary bonus. The psychiatrist should also monitor his heart rate and blood pressure, since atomoxetine can modestly elevate both, though less so than stimulants.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Recovering Addict with ADHD**
Delia, a 41-year-old in recovery from alcohol use disorder for two years, is newly diagnosed with ADHD. Her addiction specialist is supportive of ADHD treatment but concerned about prescribing controlled substances. Her ADHD is moderate in severity, primarily affecting her organizational abilities and emotional regulation at work.

*What should you consider?*
- How does atomoxetine's neurological profile (no reward-center dopamine stimulation) directly address the addiction specialist's concern?
- Is there any evidence beyond theoretical reasoning that atomoxetine is safe for people with substance use history?
- Given that Delia's ADHD is moderate — not severe — how does the chapter's guidance about atomoxetine's efficacy relative to stimulants inform this decision?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Atomoxetine is the textbook choice here. The core concern with stimulants is that they stimulate the brain's reward centers, potentially reigniting cravings or substituting one dependency for another — atomoxetine explicitly avoids this by raising dopamine only in prefrontal regions, not limbic reward circuitry. This is not merely theoretical: the chapter cites a study of adult alcoholics with ADHD in which atomoxetine simultaneously improved ADHD symptoms and reduced heavy drinking, providing direct clinical evidence for exactly Delia's situation. The fact that it is not a controlled substance also eliminates the risk of diversion or misuse. On efficacy: the chapter notes atomoxetine is a better choice when ADHD is mild to moderate rather than severe enough to require the most potent amphetamine-based medications — which fits Delia's profile. The trade-off is accepting a slower titration process and potentially slightly less symptom reduction than a stimulant might provide, but given the substance-abuse history, that trade-off is clearly justified. The addiction specialist and prescribing physician should coordinate on monitoring mood and any signs of increased drinking during the titration period.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Patient Who Needs a Third Option**
Jerome, 29, has tried two stimulants (both caused intolerable anxiety and increased his existing tic disorder) and completed a full 8-week trial of atomoxetine at an adequate dose (he was patient and compliant), but his ADHD improvement was only marginal — about 30% reduction in symptoms, which is insufficient for him to function at work. His psychiatrist is reviewing remaining options.

*What should you consider?*
- Based on the chapter, what is the next pharmacological step down the hierarchy, and what new side effects should Jerome expect?
- Jerome has a history of anxiety. Does guanfacine XR carry the same risk of worsening anxiety as stimulants do?
- What non-pharmacological options does the chapter suggest for situations where medications are insufficient?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

With stimulants ruled out by anxiety and tics, and atomoxetine producing inadequate response, guanfacine XR is the logical next step — the chapter positions it as the third-line medication. Jerome should be counseled that guanfacine XR is less effective than both stimulants and atomoxetine, so he should temper expectations accordingly. New side effects to anticipate include dizziness, sedation (especially at the start), headache, low blood pressure, and — uncommonly — fainting; these should be monitored carefully, particularly given the dose-adjustment period required. Importantly, guanfacine XR does not worsen anxiety the way stimulants can (it is an antihypertensive, not a stimulant), and its mechanism of strengthening nerve signal transmission rather than flooding neurotransmitters makes it inherently calmer in its neurological effect. The tic concern also does not apply as it does with stimulants. The chapter also explicitly mentions that a clinician may combine atomoxetine with a stimulant at lower doses of each — this could be discussed if guanfacine proves insufficient. Beyond medications, Barkley refers to Steps Four and Five (non-medication strategies) and ADHD coaching as meaningful adjuncts for cases where pharmacological results are suboptimal.

</details>

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain why atomoxetine is not addictive — and what that means practically for someone with ADHD — in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never heard of this medication before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

Atomoxetine treats ADHD by increasing a brain chemical called norepinephrine, which improves focus and impulse control, but unlike stimulant medications it does not trigger the brain's reward or pleasure centers — the part of the brain responsible for making drugs feel good and creating cravings. Because of this, people who take atomoxetine don't experience a "high," don't build up a craving for it, and studies show that even people with drug addiction histories are no more drawn to it than to an ordinary antidepressant. In practical terms, this means the medication is not a controlled substance — your doctor can call a refill into the pharmacy over the phone, you don't need to worry about it being stolen or misused at home, and it can be safely prescribed even if you or someone in your household has struggled with substance abuse.

</details>
