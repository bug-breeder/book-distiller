# Practice Exercises: Chapter 34 — 21. Rule 6: Break It Down … and Make It Matter

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why does ADHD make future goals feel so much more distant than they actually are, and why does this lead to avoidance behaviors rather than progress?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

ADHD impairs the brain's ability to sustain attention across time and to mentally project oneself forward to experience the future as emotionally real. Because the future feels abstract and unreachable, the discomfort of the present moment (confusion, overwhelm, not knowing where to start) outweighs any motivation the distant reward can generate. When the gap feels unbridgeable, avoidance becomes the path of least resistance — people go AWOL, shift blame, clown around, or regress into helplessness — because escape is immediately rewarding in a way the distant goal is not.

</details>

**Q2:** What is the core logic behind breaking tasks into very small chunks, and how does the size of the chunk relate to the distance of the deadline?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The core logic is that shorter time horizons fall within the ADHD brain's motivational window — the near future feels real, the far future does not. By collapsing a multi-week project into half-hour or 15-minute units, you replace one overwhelming abstraction with a series of immediately actionable microtasks. Crucially, the relationship is proportional: the further away the deadline, the smaller the chunks must be. A same-day deadline might only need hour-long blocks; a week-long project needs daily goals broken into hourly segments; a multi-month project requires daily, then hourly, then 15-minute subdivisions. Michael Phelps's 15-minute daily schedule is the extreme illustration of this principle.

</details>

**Q3:** Why does making yourself accountable to another person add motivational power beyond what self-monitoring alone provides?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Accountability to another person introduces social judgment as an external motivator, which is neurologically distinct from purely internal self-regulation. Most people are far more distressed by the prospect of disappointing or embarrassing themselves in front of someone they respect than by privately failing a self-set goal. This social consequence generates an emotional charge — the discomfort of anticipated disapproval and the pleasure of anticipated admiration — that acts as an additional fuel source layered on top of any intrinsic motivation. For adults with ADHD whose internal motivation engine runs weak, this social layer can make the difference between starting and not starting.

</details>

**Q4:** The chapter describes an end-of-chunk ritual involving four specific steps. What are they, and why does each one matter for someone with ADHD?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The four steps are: (1) congratulate yourself — this provides the brain with an immediate emotional payoff that makes the completed chunk feel rewarding rather than merely finished; (2) take a short break of a few minutes — this prevents cognitive depletion and resets attention for the next chunk; (3) tell someone what you have accomplished — this activates the social accountability motivator mid-project, not just at the end; and (4) give yourself a small, brief reward — this functions as the concrete, external reinforcer that the brain's dopamine system needs but cannot generate purely from internal satisfaction. Together, these four steps turn each micro-completion into a mini-success event, sustaining momentum across the entire project.

</details>

**Q5:** Why must the rewards used in the self-reward strategy be small and brief rather than large and indulgent, and what goes wrong if the reward is disproportionate to the chunk completed?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Small, brief rewards serve as punctuation — they mark completion without consuming the time and energy that should carry the person into the next chunk. A large or indulgent reward defeats the structural purpose: it extends the break so long that re-entry becomes difficult, it drains the motivational tension built up around the next task, and it can itself become a source of dysregulation (e.g., a long video game break that is impossible to stop). The reward must be proportionate to the micro-scale of the work unit — a cappuccino, a few minutes with a guitar, a piece of chocolate — so that the work-reward cycle turns over quickly and frequently, keeping the person in a sustained rhythm rather than oscillating between long work sprints and long indulgences.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Week-Long Work Report**
Your manager assigns you a comprehensive quarterly performance report due in five business days. The report requires pulling data from three systems, writing analysis for six departments, and formatting everything into a slide deck. Every time you sit down to start, you stare at the screen for ten minutes and then find yourself doing email instead.

*What should you consider?*
- How would you sequence the report's components into daily goals, and then break each day's goal into 30- or 15-minute microtasks?
- Who at work could serve as an accountability partner, and what specifically would you report to them and when?
- What small, work-appropriate rewards could you build into the schedule to close out each chunk?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Start by mapping the five days to the five major phases: Day 1 — pull and organize all data from the three systems; Day 2 — write analysis for three departments; Day 3 — write analysis for the remaining three departments; Day 4 — build the slide deck structure and populate it with completed content; Day 5 — review, polish, and submit. Then take Day 1's goal and break it into 30-minute blocks: 9:00–9:30 pull system A data, 9:30–10:00 pull system B data, and so on, written into your calendar as firm appointments.

Identify a supportive colleague or your manager and tell them your daily completion targets. Send them a brief end-of-day message each day: "Finished today's data pull — on track for tomorrow's analysis." This adds social stakes to every daily mini-deadline.

For rewards: after each 30-minute block, stand up, get water or coffee, and take 3 minutes away from the screen before returning. At the end of each completed day's goal, send your accountability message and allow yourself 15 minutes of any leisure activity before starting or wrapping up.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Overwhelming Home Project**
Your apartment has been accumulating clutter for months. You want to declutter and reorganize every room, but the scope feels so enormous that you have tried to start three times in the past month and have not lasted more than 20 minutes before giving up. Your partner has offered to help but you do not know how to use their support constructively.

*What should you consider?*
- How do you apply the principle that the further the horizon, the smaller the chunks must be, given that this project spans multiple rooms over multiple weekends?
- How can your partner serve as an accountability partner rather than just a co-worker in the task?
- What does the end-of-chunk ritual look like in a home context?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Assign each room to a separate weekend session, then break each session into zones within the room (e.g., bedroom session: 30 minutes for the nightstand area, 30 minutes for the closet floor, 30 minutes for the dresser). Write these on a physical list taped to the wall of the room you are working on, and highlight only the current zone to prevent the full scope from re-entering your awareness.

Use your partner as an accountability partner by telling them your zone-by-zone plan at the start of each session, and reporting completion to them after each 30-minute block — not asking for help with the actual work unless you want it. The act of saying "I finished the nightstand zone" out loud activates the social motivator the chapter describes.

After each 30-minute zone: stop completely, congratulate yourself out loud (even briefly), take a 3-minute break (water, a short walk around the apartment), tell your partner what you finished, and then pick a small reward — a favorite snack, five minutes on your phone, a short piece of music you love. Then return and highlight the next zone.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Long-Term Personal Goal**
You have wanted to learn conversational Spanish for two years. You have downloaded two apps, bought a course, and started three different times. Each time you practiced intensely for a few days and then stopped entirely when life got busy. The goal feels meaningful but the timeline — "fluency someday" — seems impossibly abstract.

*What should you consider?*
- How do you convert a years-long aspiration into a structure of daily microtasks that actually falls within ADHD's motivational window?
- What accountability structure could sustain this over months when there is no external deadline?
- How do you design rewards that are proportionate to 15-minute study sessions?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Strip the goal of all timeline abstraction and reduce it to a daily quota that is almost insultingly small: 15 minutes per day, five days a week, working through one specific lesson or unit. Write this into your calendar as a recurring appointment at a fixed time — ideally immediately after an existing daily habit (like morning coffee) so it borrows that habit's momentum. Mark off each day on a visible paper calendar on your desk or kitchen wall; the unbroken chain of marks becomes its own motivator.

For accountability, find a study partner (a friend also learning a language, even a different one) and exchange a brief daily check-in text: "Did your 15 today?" The social check-in transforms a private goal into a shared commitment without requiring the other person to know Spanish.

For rewards: after each 15-minute session, allow yourself something brief you genuinely enjoy — three minutes of a podcast, a piece of chocolate, scrolling social media for exactly two minutes. Because the session is only 15 minutes, the reward must be proportionate — a few minutes, not a half-hour. The ratio of work to reward must keep the work cycle dominant.

</details>

## ✍️ Reflection Prompts

1. Think of a time when you were given a project with a distant deadline and you either panicked, went blank, or avoided it entirely until the last moment — what was it about the scale or distance of that project that triggered avoidance, and how would you structure the first 90 minutes of that project differently now that you understand chunking?

2. Think of a goal you have repeatedly started and abandoned over the past year — what does that pattern tell you about the mismatch between how you were approaching the goal and what your brain actually needs in terms of near-term feedback and external accountability, and who in your life could realistically serve as your accountability partner for that goal starting this week?

3. Think of a time when you helped someone else accomplish a large task by breaking it into steps and cheering them on at each milestone — you likely did this naturally for them. What would it look like to extend the same patience, structure, and encouragement to yourself that you gave to them, and what internal resistance comes up when you imagine rewarding yourself as systematically as you would reward a child or a friend?

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the concept of "chunking with accountability and self-reward" — the core three-part system of Rule 6 — in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

When a task or goal feels too big to start, the most effective thing you can do is physically break it into the smallest possible pieces — 15 or 30 minutes of work at a time — so that each piece is close enough to feel real and reachable. Then you tell another person your plan and report back to them each time you complete a piece, because knowing someone else is watching makes you far more likely to follow through than any amount of private self-discipline. After each small piece is done, you give yourself a tiny, immediate reward — a snack, a few minutes of something you enjoy — which trains your brain to associate effort with payoff and keeps you willing to start the next piece.

</details>

## 🧩 Synthesis Challenge

Rule 6 teaches that external structure — chunking, accountability, and rewards — compensates for ADHD's impaired internal motivation. Earlier chapters established that ADHD is fundamentally a disorder of self-regulation and time blindness, not intelligence or laziness (Rules 1–3), and that making things visible and physical in the external world is essential because the ADHD brain cannot reliably hold information in working memory (Rule 4, externalizing information).

**Exercise:** Design a complete "launch system" for a specific two-week project of your choosing. Your system must explicitly integrate three elements from different chapters: (1) an external visual display (from Rule 4 / externalizing information) that makes the full chunk structure visible at all times; (2) a time-boundary tool (from Rule 5 / making time physical) that signals the start and end of each work chunk without relying on your sense of elapsed time; and (3) a daily accountability ritual (from Rule 6) with a specific person, a specific reporting format, and a specific small reward for each completed chunk. Write out all three components in enough detail that you could hand this plan to someone else and they could run it for you.

**Chapters involved:** Chapter 34 (Rule 6: Break It Down) + Chapter 33 (Rule 5: Make Time Physical) + Chapter 32 (Rule 4: Externalize Information)

## 📋 Action Items

1. On Monday morning, before opening email, take the single most avoided task currently on your plate, open your calendar, and block it into 30-minute chunks for the rest of the week — assign each block a specific microtask by name (not just "work on project X") and highlight the first chunk to work on that day.

2. By Tuesday, identify one person — a colleague, friend, partner, or family member — and send them a message explaining one specific goal you are working toward this week, the chunk schedule you have set, and ask them if you can text or email them a brief update each day when you complete that day's chunk.

3. Before the week begins, write a list of five small rewards (each taking 5 minutes or less) that you can use as end-of-chunk payoffs — post this list at your workspace so it is visible when you complete each chunk, and actually use one from the list every single time you finish a scheduled block, without skipping the reward step even when the work felt easy.
