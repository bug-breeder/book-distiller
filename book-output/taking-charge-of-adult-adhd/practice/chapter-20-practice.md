# Practice Exercises: Chapter 9 — Executive Functions: The Seven Abilities That Make Up Self-Control … and More

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why does Barkley argue that self-awareness and inhibition must develop together, and what would happen to the other five executive functions without them?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Self-awareness (the mind's mirror) and inhibition (the mind's brakes) are codependent because neither is useful in isolation: if you cannot monitor your own behavior you have no reason to stop it, and if you cannot stop your behavior there is no point in observing it. Together they create the foundation that makes the other five executive functions meaningful. Without self-awareness and inhibition, nonverbal working memory has no "pause" in which to operate, verbal working memory cannot evaluate before speaking, emotional self-regulation cannot soften an impulse before it fires, self-motivation cannot sustain effort toward a goal, and planning cannot generate options that will actually be acted on. All higher self-control collapses.

</details>

---

**Q2:** Barkley describes the "internalization" of executive functions as a developmental process. What exactly gets internalized, and why do adults with ADHD appear to be arrested partway through this process?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

What gets internalized is overt, publicly visible behavior — talking aloud, physical self-reminders, manual manipulation — that gradually moves inside the brain and becomes private thought. A "switch" deep in the brain prevents the neural signal from reaching the spinal cord and producing an observable action, so the behavior becomes a mental simulation rather than a physical act. Adults with ADHD have a lag in this internalization process: their executive functions develop more slowly and incompletely, so behavior that typical adults have fully privatized may still be partly external or urgently impulsive for them. They effectively act out their thoughts rather than holding thoughts long enough to evaluate them, which is why external cues and supports can substitute for the internal controls that have not yet fully developed.

</details>

---

**Q3:** Nonverbal working memory is described as producing eight distinct capacities. How do "hindsight" and "foresight" emerge from this single mechanism, and why does their failure explain ADHD's characteristic time-blindness?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Nonverbal working memory allows us to hold and replay sensory images of past events — what Barkley calls "resensing." Hindsight is the ability to bring those stored images of past events forward into the present moment to inform a decision. Foresight (forethought) extrapolates patterns detected in those past images to anticipate future events. Because both depend on the same image-holding mechanism, a deficit in nonverbal working memory simultaneously cripples both. For someone with ADHD, the image archive is shallow and hard to access quickly, so the future feels vague or nonexistent. The result is that only the present feels real — a state Barkley calls living "in the now" — which produces chronic lateness, missed deadlines, poor time estimation, and impulsive preference for immediate rewards over larger future ones.

</details>

**Q4:** Barkley distinguishes verbal working memory from simple "talking to yourself" and attributes six specific capacities to it. Which of these capacities most directly explains why some adults with ADHD have difficulty following through on self-made rules and diets, even when they genuinely want to?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The most directly relevant capacity is the ability to formulate and adjust rules and meta-rules — the third function of verbal working memory. Making a rule (e.g., "I will limit sugar") requires verbal working memory to connect past behavior to future consequences through self-interrogation and reasoning. Sticking to the rule when circumstances change requires the further ability to generate meta-rules: higher-order principles about when and how to revise a strategy. Without this, an adult with ADHD might follow a rule rigidly until it fails and then simply abandon it, rather than troubleshooting through self-talk. The Nina example illustrates this: she could make a rule but lacked the verbal working memory to create a meta-rule that would keep the plan adaptive (e.g., remove temptations from the home, consult a physician about a sustainable approach).

</details>

**Q5:** How does Barkley use the concept of "selfish altruism" to connect nonverbal working memory to social cooperation, and what does this imply about the social difficulties experienced by adults with ADHD?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Nonverbal working memory gives us a sense of time extending into the future. With that temporal horizon, we can understand that sharing resources now with a friend or community member is an investment — a form of insurance — because those people may reciprocate when we are in need. This voluntary, reciprocal sharing is "selfish altruism": it benefits others but ultimately serves our own long-term survival. Without a robust sense of the future, the calculation changes: all that is perceptible is the immediate loss of one's own resources, so sharing seems irrational. Adults with ADHD who have impaired nonverbal working memory may genuinely fail to see the delayed social return on cooperation, leading others to perceive them as selfish or unreliable — as illustrated by the LaTonya example. The social difficulties are therefore not character flaws but a downstream consequence of a neurologically impaired time sense.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Workplace Email That Blew Up**
Darius, who has ADHD, reads a curt message from his manager questioning his work on a project. He immediately fires off a defensive, emotionally charged reply that his colleagues describe as "way over the top." His manager is now considering a formal warning.

*What should you consider?*
- Which executive function primarily failed here, and which supporting functions were also insufficient?
- What would effective emotional self-regulation have looked like in the moment between reading the email and replying?
- How could Darius use both verbal and nonverbal working memory together to de-escalate?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The primary failure is in emotional self-regulation (the mind's heart), but inhibition also failed — Darius did not pause long enough to allow any other executive function to operate. Effective emotional self-regulation would have involved a deliberate delay: using self-talk to name the emotion ("I feel attacked and ashamed") rather than simply acting on it, and using visual imagery to recall past situations where a cooler response produced a better outcome. Nonverbal working memory could supply a remembered image of a time his manager praised him, countering the catastrophic emotional interpretation of one curt message. Verbal working memory would allow him to interrogate his assumption ("Is this manager usually hostile? Did I misread the tone? What is the least charitable and most charitable reading of this message?"). The optimal response: close the email, take a break, run through the self-talk and imagery sequence, draft a factual reply the next day, and have a trusted colleague read it before sending.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Unfinished Home Renovation**
Priya has ADHD and decided to renovate her kitchen. Six months in, supplies are scattered across three rooms, two walls are half-painted in different colors, and she has not touched the project in two months. Every time she thinks about it she feels paralyzed.

*What should you consider?*
- Which executive functions are most implicated in the stall?
- How does deficient planning/problem-solving interact with deficient self-motivation here?
- What external scaffolding could substitute for the missing internal executive functions?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Planning and problem-solving (the mind's playground), nonverbal working memory, and self-motivation (the mind's fuel tank) are all implicated. Priya cannot hold a clear sequence of steps in mind, so the project has no internal logical order — each work session started fresh without building on the last. Without nonverbal working memory, she cannot mentally simulate the finished kitchen to use as a motivating image. Without self-motivation, the absence of external rewards (the kitchen is not done, so there is no praise or satisfaction yet) causes the activation deficit that ADHD creates. External scaffolding that compensates: break the project into a written, numbered checklist of micro-steps (externalizing verbal working memory); put a visual before-and-after inspiration image on the fridge (externalizing nonverbal working memory); schedule one 30-minute work session per week with a friend present (externalizing self-motivation through social accountability); and use a simple calendar deadline visible every morning (externalizing the time sense provided by nonverbal working memory).

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Meeting Where No One Gets a Word In**
Kwame is in a team brainstorming session. He has strong ideas but keeps interrupting colleagues mid-sentence, jumps between unconnected topics, and talks over the facilitator. Afterward, a well-liked colleague quietly tells him people are avoiding working with him.

*What should you consider?*
- Which executive function deficits explain each specific behavior (interrupting, topic-jumping, talking over)?
- How would self-awareness and verbal working memory, if fully functional, change his behavior in real time?
- What practical strategies could Kwame adopt for the next meeting?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Interrupting and talking over others reflect inhibition failure — the urge to speak is not delayed long enough for social awareness to intervene. Topic-jumping reflects a deficit in verbal working memory: Kwame cannot hold a thread of conversation in mind while someone else is speaking, so new associations fire and displace the current topic before he can self-edit. The self-awareness deficit means he is not reading the facial expressions and body language (nonverbal cues archived through nonverbal working memory) that signal annoyance. With intact self-awareness and verbal working memory, he would notice discomfort in others, silently label it, and use self-talk ("They look frustrated — I have been dominating — let the current speaker finish, then add one point"). Practical strategies: write his key ideas on a notepad before speaking so he does not fear losing them (reduces urgency to interrupt), designate a private signal with a trusted colleague to tap his arm when he is over-talking (external self-awareness substitute), and commit to a rule of letting two others speak before he contributes again.

</details>

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the concept of executive functions — specifically why ADHD is fundamentally a problem of self-control, not intelligence or effort — in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

Your brain has a set of seven mental tools — things like a built-in mirror for watching yourself, an internal voice for thinking before speaking, and an emotional thermostat for calming yourself down — that allow you to pause, think about the future, and choose the wisest action instead of just reacting. In ADHD, these tools develop more slowly and work less reliably, so the problem is not that the person lacks knowledge or willpower, but that the mental machinery for applying what they know in the moment is impaired. This is why someone with ADHD can fully understand a rule, genuinely intend to follow it, and still fail — because following through requires those executive tools to fire automatically under real-world conditions, and in ADHD they often do not.

</details>

## 📋 Action Items

1. On Monday morning before checking email, write down the names of the seven executive functions on a sticky note and place it on your monitor or desk. For each one, write a single word that describes a real situation from the past week where that function felt weakest. Use this as a personal diagnostic — not to criticize yourself, but to identify which "mind tool" to target with compensatory strategies this week.

2. Choose one task you have been avoiding or repeatedly failing to start (a report, a phone call, a household job). Before Wednesday, spend exactly ten minutes breaking it into a written numbered sequence of the smallest possible steps — externalizing the planning/problem-solving function — and schedule one 20-minute slot to complete only step 1. Put the step list somewhere visible.

3. This week, before responding to any message or situation that produces a clear emotional reaction (frustration, irritation, excitement), insert a minimum 10-minute gap between feeling the emotion and sending or saying your response. During that gap, use verbal working memory deliberately: write two sentences — one describing the emotion, one asking what outcome you actually want — before composing your reply.
