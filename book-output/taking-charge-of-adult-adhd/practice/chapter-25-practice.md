# Practice Exercises: Chapter 13 — The Stimulants

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why do stimulant medications reduce hyperactivity and impulsivity in people with ADHD, even though "stimulant" implies they should increase activity?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The term stimulant is misleading in the context of ADHD. These medications stimulate the frontal regions of the brain — areas responsible for inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions — that are underactive in people with ADHD. Because the ADHD brain lacks sufficient dopamine and norepinephrine to send proper signals between neurons, it cannot self-regulate effectively. Stimulants raise these neurochemical levels toward normal, enabling the frontal brain regions to do their job. The result is reduced restlessness and impulsivity, not increased agitation. In people without ADHD, the same medications produce a different effect because those brain regions are already functioning at adequate levels.

</details>

---

**Q2:** Methylphenidate and amphetamine both increase dopamine availability in the brain, but they do so through different mechanisms. What are those mechanisms and why does the distinction matter clinically?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Amphetamines work by increasing the production and release of dopamine (and to a lesser extent norepinephrine) from nerve cells, flooding the synapse — like opening a gate so water rushes into a canal. Methylphenidate works by blocking the reuptake of dopamine back into the nerve cell after it has been released, keeping the dopamine level high in the synapse — like closing a lock to keep the water level elevated. The distinction matters clinically because the two drug classes can have different side effect profiles and different subjective sensations of onset. For example, amphetamines are more likely to cause feelings of inner tension and are somewhat more likely to worsen tics. Patients who experience anxiety or tension on an amphetamine may do better on methylphenidate, and vice versa.

</details>

---

**Q3:** Why is the route of administration so critical in determining whether a stimulant becomes addictive, and how does this explain why prescribed oral use carries low addiction risk?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The addictive properties of stimulants are driven by how rapidly and intensely the drugs flood the brain's reward centers (nucleus accumbens), which then produce euphoria. When stimulants are snorted or injected intravenously, they reach the brain in a sudden, powerful surge, rapidly elevating dopamine well above normal levels and producing the euphoric "rush" that drives addiction. When taken orally in low therapeutic doses, the drug enters and leaves the brain slowly, and it only raises dopamine levels to approximately normal — not to the supraphysiological levels that trigger euphoria. This slow, modest increase does not produce the reward sensation that conditions addictive behavior, which is why more than 16 studies of children treated with stimulants found no increase in later drug abuse risk.

</details>

**Q4:** The chapter describes several delivery systems for stimulants (pills, pump, pellets, patch, pro-drug). What is the core trade-off that each system is trying to solve, and what practical factors should guide a person's choice?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The core trade-off is duration versus flexibility. Immediate-release pills act fast (15–20 minutes) and peak in 60–90 minutes, but last only 3–5 hours, requiring multiple daily doses — a problem for people who already struggle with remembering to take medication. Extended-release systems (the osmotic pump Concerta, time-release pellets like Adderall XR, the skin patch Daytrana, and the pro-drug Vyvanse) solve this by delivering medication across 8–14 hours from a single dose. Practical choice factors include: length of the active day needed, whether peak performance is more important in the morning (pellets perform better then) or afternoon (pump performs better then), whether the person can swallow capsules (pellets can be sprinkled on food), abuse potential concerns (Vyvanse's pro-drug design cannot be activated outside the digestive tract), and whether skin sensitivity rules out the patch.

</details>

---

**Q5:** The chapter states that people on stimulants actually have a somewhat lower rate of sudden cardiac death than the general population. How can this be true if stimulants increase heart rate and blood pressure?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The seemingly paradoxical finding reflects selection bias in prescribing practices: before a stimulant is prescribed, physicians routinely screen patients for cardiac risk factors, including structural heart defects, major arrhythmias, family history of sudden death, and high blood pressure. Anyone with significant cardiac risk is typically not given a stimulant prescription. The general population is not subjected to this systematic screening. Therefore, the group that ends up taking stimulants has, by design, a lower baseline cardiac risk profile than the unscreened general population. The stimulants do modestly raise heart rate and blood pressure — roughly equivalent to climbing half a flight of stairs — but this effect in a pre-screened, low-risk population produces a better overall cardiac outcome than you see in the unsorted general public.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: Choosing a Delivery System**
Marcus, a 34-year-old lawyer, was recently diagnosed with ADHD and has been prescribed methylphenidate. His workday runs from 7 AM to 7 PM with critical client meetings often scheduled in the late afternoon. He tried an immediate-release pill but keeps forgetting his midday dose and feels unfocused by 3 PM.

*What should you consider?*
- Which delivery systems provide coverage across a 12-hour day, and which ones tend to perform better in the afternoon vs. the morning?
- What is the practical trade-off between the pump (Concerta) and the pellet system (Ritalin LA) for someone who needs afternoon peak performance?
- Is there a supplemental strategy his doctor might recommend if no single extended-release form covers his full day?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Marcus's problem — forgetting the midday dose and losing symptom control in the afternoon — is a textbook case for switching to an extended-release delivery system. The osmotic pump (Concerta) is particularly well suited here because research shows it provides relatively better symptom control in the afternoon compared to pellet systems, which tend to peak in the morning. Given that his critical meetings occur late afternoon, Concerta aligns well with his needs. If Concerta still does not extend coverage through 7 PM, his doctor could supplement it with a small immediate-release methylphenidate pill in the mid-afternoon — a common clinical strategy described in the chapter. This approach reduces the multiple-dose problem (one morning capsule plus one afternoon pill) while still matching coverage to the full adult workday. Marcus should also discuss with his doctor whether an amphetamine-based extended release (such as Vyvanse, which offers 10–14 hours) might be worth trying if methylphenidate options fall short.

</details>

**Scenario 2: Managing the Anxiety Concern**
Priya, 28, was prescribed Adderall XR (an amphetamine) for ADHD. After two weeks she tells her doctor that while her focus has improved significantly, she feels jittery and anxious, especially in the mornings after taking her dose. She has a history of mild anxiety that predates her ADHD diagnosis.

*What should you consider?*
- Which stimulant class is more associated with increased anxiety and inner tension, and what switching option exists within the stimulant category?
- What non-stimulant alternative exists for someone with comorbid anxiety (referenced in the chapter)?
- How does dose level factor into these side effects, and what immediate adjustment might help?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Priya's situation illustrates a known risk: research shows amphetamines are somewhat more likely than methylphenidate to increase anxiety and inner tension, and pre-existing anxiety amplifies this effect. Her physician should first consider reducing her dose, since tension and jitteriness are dose-dependent and a lower dose may preserve ADHD benefit while reducing the side effect. If that is insufficient, switching within amphetamines to Vyvanse — which has a smoother, more gradual onset than Adderall XR — sometimes reduces the subjective sensation of a sudden "hit" that triggers anxiety. Alternatively, switching her to a methylphenidate-based extended-release formulation (Concerta or Ritalin LA) may resolve the anxiety entirely, since methylphenidate carries a lower anxiety risk. If stimulants as a class continue to worsen her anxiety, Barkley points toward the nonstimulants (covered in Chapter 14) as a better primary option for adults where comorbid anxiety is a significant concern. A co-prescribed anti-anxiety medication (as mentioned in the chapter's anecdote) is another clinically used approach.

</details>

**Scenario 3: Pregnancy and Medication Decision**
Diane, 32, has been successfully managing her ADHD with amphetamine medication for three years and is now planning to become pregnant. She is worried about stopping her medication because she recalls how impaired she was before treatment: two car accidents, job loss, and severe disorganization. Her OB-GYN told her to stop all ADHD medication immediately upon conceiving.

*What should you consider?*
- What does the available evidence actually say about stimulant use during pregnancy, and what is the basis of the standard recommendation to discontinue?
- What are the concrete risks of stopping medication that must be weighed against hypothetical fetal risk?
- What is the most responsible course of action for Diane to take?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Diane's OB-GYN is following a standard recommendation, but Barkley's chapter urges a more nuanced reading of the evidence. The current data show no evidence of major fetal abnormalities in babies born to mothers who remained on ADHD medication; some amphetamine may enter breast milk but not at levels shown to harm infants, and methylphenidate does not appear in breast milk at all. The recommendation to discontinue is partly precautionary and partly driven by pharmaceutical companies limiting liability — not solely by compelling evidence of harm. Against this, the risks of unmanaged ADHD during pregnancy are concrete and serious: increased risk of accidental injury, car crashes, substance use, poor emotional regulation, and adverse parenting. Given Diane's documented history of accidents and job loss before treatment, these are not abstract risks. The responsible approach is for Diane to have an informed conversation with both her psychiatrist and OB-GYN together, explicitly weighing the known harms of discontinuation against the evidence base for fetal risk, and making a shared decision rather than automatically defaulting to discontinuation.

</details>

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain why stimulant medications help people with ADHD without making them more hyperactive, in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

In the ADHD brain, the parts responsible for focus, impulse control, and sitting still do not have enough of the chemical messengers they need to work properly, so the brain struggles to regulate itself. Stimulant medications fix this by raising those chemical messengers — mainly dopamine — to normal levels in exactly the brain regions that need them, allowing those control systems to finally function as they should. Rather than revving up an already-overactive engine, it is more like adding fuel to an engine that has been running on empty.

</details>

Before your next appointment, write down every stimulant you have tried (or are currently taking), the delivery system it uses, the dose, and the exact times of day when you feel it working well versus when you feel it wearing off — bring this log to your doctor so the two of you can assess whether your current delivery system actually matches your active-day coverage needs.

2. If you have been avoiding or delaying starting a stimulant because of something you heard about heart risk or addiction, spend 20 minutes on Tuesday evening reading the cardiac and addiction sections of this chapter again, then write down the specific concern you had and the specific evidence that addresses it — this turns a vague fear into a named, examined belief you can discuss with your prescriber.

3. This week, take your stimulant at the same time each morning for five consecutive days and keep a brief log (even just three words per entry) noting your focus level, appetite, sleep onset time, and any side effects at three fixed points in the day — morning, midday, and evening — so you have concrete data rather than impressions when evaluating whether your current dose and timing are working.
