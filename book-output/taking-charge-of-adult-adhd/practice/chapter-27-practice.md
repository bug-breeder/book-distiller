# Practice Exercises: Chapter 15 — What to Expect from Treatment

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why does Barkley argue that finding the right ADHD medication is "as much art as science," and what does this mean practically for someone starting treatment?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Barkley uses this phrase to convey that medication selection cannot be reduced to a simple algorithm — it depends on an individual's comorbid conditions, prior medication history, urgency of treatment, lifestyle factors, and personal preferences. Practically, it means a patient should expect a trial-and-error process: starting at a low dose, titrating upward weekly, possibly switching delivery systems or drug classes, and taking several weeks to months before landing on an optimal regimen. It also means the prescribing physician's clinical judgment, shaped by experience with similar patients, plays a large role alongside evidence-based guidelines.

</details>

---

**Q2:** The chapter presents a checklist for deciding between a stimulant and a nonstimulant like atomoxetine. What underlying logic drives that checklist — what categories of concern make a nonstimulant preferable?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The checklist clusters around four categories of concern: (1) medical/physiological factors that make stimulants riskier, such as tic disorders, significant insomnia, or past adverse reactions; (2) psychiatric comorbidities like anxiety or depression that nonstimulants address more broadly; (3) abuse and diversion risk, including personal substance abuse history, a household member with such a history, or a college environment where stimulant theft is common; and (4) practical and logistical barriers, such as the burdensome monthly-prescription requirement for Schedule II stimulants. More "yes" answers across these categories tilts the decision toward atomoxetine, sometimes in combination with a lower-dose stimulant to achieve broader coverage with fewer side effects from either agent alone.

</details>

---

**Q3:** Barkley recommends that adults take ADHD medication seven days a week, year-round rather than only on "school days" or workdays. What is the reasoning behind this recommendation, and when might departing from it make sense?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The reasoning is that ADHD impairments do not stop when the workday ends — they affect driving safety, parenting quality, financial management, household responsibilities, and intimate relationships every day of the week. Limiting medication to weekdays would leave those domains chronically impaired. Departure can make sense, however, when the medication noticeably blunts emotional range or creativity in ways that matter to the person's professional identity or quality of life. Barkley illustrates this with a poet and a symphony musician who found their expressive range was restricted on medication and therefore chose medication-free days specifically for creative work, while continuing medication for all other responsibilities.

</details>

---

**Q4:** After medication begins working, Barkley warns that many ongoing life problems will not automatically resolve. Why not, and how should a person with ADHD think about distinguishing ADHD-caused problems from non-ADHD problems?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Medication improves the brain's executive function deficits, but it cannot undo the accumulated consequences of years of untreated ADHD — damaged relationships, earned reputations for unreliability, underdeveloped skills, financial debts, and internalized defensive or self-defeating attitudes. Non-ADHD problems (incompatibility with a partner, lack of job skills required for promotion, genuine financial overextension) have roots that medication does not touch. A useful heuristic is to ask whether a problem could be directly traced to inattention, impulsivity, or poor self-regulation: if yes, medication should help; if no, other solutions are needed. Barkley notes that the clearer thinking medication provides often makes it easier to finally perform this triage accurately — but some people still need a therapist or coach to work through the layers of resentment and self-protective distortion that years of untreated ADHD can create.

</details>

---

**Q5:** What role does the ADHD Symptom Tracking Scale play in treatment, and why is structured tracking better than relying on memory alone?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The tracking scale allows a patient to rate each DSM-aligned ADHD symptom on a frequency continuum and then separately rate how much those symptoms impair functioning across domains such as home life, driving, finances, and relationships. This serves two clinical purposes: it gives the physician precise, quantified information about whether a given dose is improving specific symptoms rather than relying on vague impressions, and it surfaces functional impairments that a patient might not spontaneously mention during a brief office visit. Memory is unreliable for this purpose because ADHD itself compromises prospective memory and working memory, making patients likely to underreport symptom burden at baseline or overestimate improvement when they feel generally better, even if specific impairments persist. Regular structured tracking makes dosage adjustments more accurate and helps justify changes to the treatment plan with concrete evidence.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The "It Should Have Worked by Now" Trap**
Marcus, 34, was diagnosed with ADHD three weeks ago and started on Ritalin LA at a low dose. He tells his doctor he feels "basically the same" and wants to stop medication entirely, convinced it simply does not work for him. His doctor has not yet increased the dose or tried other delivery systems.

*What should you consider?*
- Is the current dose actually within the therapeutic range, or has the titration process barely begun?
- Has Marcus waited long enough, and has anyone in his life — a partner, a coworker — been asked whether they notice any difference he might be missing?
- Before abandoning stimulants altogether, what is the evidence-based sequence of next steps (delivery system switch, amphetamine class, nonstimulant)?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Marcus is likely abandoning the process prematurely. The chapter specifies that dose titration involves starting low and increasing weekly, and that it can take 2–3 weeks or even 1–2 months to find the optimal dose. Three weeks at a single low dose is not a completed trial of that medication. The appropriate next step is for the doctor to increase the dose, not abandon the drug. If the methylphenidate delivery system (Ritalin LA) still fails after a proper dose range has been explored, the next step is to try a different delivery system — such as Concerta (osmotic pump) or Daytrana (patch) — because the delivery mechanism, not the active compound, might be the issue. Only after exhausting methylphenidate options should the physician pivot to an amphetamine system (Adderall XR, Vyvanse) or a nonstimulant. Barkley also notes that Marcus should solicit feedback from someone close to him, because third-party observers often detect improvements the person on medication misses. Overall, Marcus has an 80–90% chance of responding to one of the available medications — but only if the full sequence is followed with patience.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Comorbidity Conflict**
Priya, 28, has ADHD and moderate generalized anxiety. Her new psychiatrist wants to start her immediately on a stimulant because her workplace situation is urgent — her manager has warned her that her performance must improve within 30 days. Priya is worried about stimulants worsening her anxiety.

*What should you consider?*
- Does Priya's anxiety profile appear on the checklist that would favor a nonstimulant?
- Is the urgency of her work situation a legitimate clinical factor, and if so, how should it be weighed?
- What combined or alternative treatment approaches does the chapter point toward?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Priya's situation reflects a genuine clinical tension the chapter explicitly addresses. Anxiety is listed on the stimulant-vs-nonstimulant checklist as a factor favoring atomoxetine, because nonstimulants have a broader effect on noradrenergic pathways and may address both ADHD and anxiety more safely. At the same time, the chapter acknowledges that urgency — being on the verge of losing a job — is a legitimate reason for choosing a stimulant, because stimulants typically work within days while atomoxetine takes several weeks to build up. One resolution the chapter offers is a low-dose combination: using lower doses of both a stimulant and atomoxetine together to gain broader symptom coverage while limiting the side effects that higher doses of either alone would produce. The physician should also be alert to the principle that comorbid conditions that are more impairing or life-threatening should be treated first; here, the anxiety is described as moderate, so ADHD treatment can proceed with careful monitoring of her anxiety levels. Priya should communicate any anxiety escalation to her doctor immediately and use the ADHD Symptom Tracking Scale to give precise data at follow-up visits.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The "Fixed Now" Misconception**
Devon, 41, has been on an effective ADHD medication for six months and is doing much better at work. But his marriage is still rocky — his wife says he is dismissive, often cancels plans, and never takes responsibility for past mistakes. Devon believes medication should have fixed all of this by now and is confused and frustrated that his relationship has not improved.

*What should you consider?*
- Which of Devon's relationship problems might still be ADHD-related versus problems medication cannot resolve?
- What does the chapter say about the long-term consequences of untreated ADHD on relationships and self-perception?
- What additional resources or interventions does the chapter recommend for exactly this kind of gap?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Medication has addressed Devon's executive function deficits at a neurological level, but it cannot retroactively repair a relationship that accumulated years of damage under untreated ADHD. His wife's responses — expecting unreliability, reacting to a history of broken promises — are not simply ADHD symptoms in Devon; they are conditioned reactions in another person, rooted in accumulated hurt and eroded trust. Some of Devon's behaviors that still frustrate her (dismissiveness, deflecting blame) may now be habitual defensive patterns rather than pure ADHD symptoms — the kind of "shoot-yourself-in-the-foot habits" that Barkley says are hard to shake and are not automatically dissolved by medication. The chapter recommends that adults in this situation recruit therapists, counselors, or life coaches to address relationship patterns, communication skills, and the deep-seated resentment or self-protective attitudes that years of criticism can produce. Couples counseling, not a medication adjustment, is the appropriate next step for Devon. He and his wife may also benefit from the behavioral strategies in Steps Four and Five of the book, which address specific daily-life domains where ADHD has historically created problems.

</details>

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the process of finding the right ADHD medication in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

Finding the right ADHD medication is a step-by-step process, not a single decision: a doctor starts with the drug most likely to help you based on your health history and circumstances, then slowly increases the dose week by week until your symptoms improve or side effects become a problem. If the first medication does not work well enough after a full trial, the doctor methodically tries other options — a different delivery system for the same drug, a different drug class, or a non-stimulant — because roughly 80–90% of people with ADHD will eventually respond well to one of the available options. The key insight is that patience is not passivity: you actively track your symptoms, report changes to your doctor, and understand that the process may take several months, which is entirely normal and expected.

</details>

## 📋 Action Items

1. Before your next doctor's appointment — or tonight if your appointment is soon — download or photocopy the ADHD Symptom Tracking Scale from the back of the book, complete Section I (rating each symptom) and Section II (rating functional impairment across life domains), and bring the completed form to the appointment so your doctor has precise data rather than your in-the-moment verbal summary.

2. This week, write a list of three to five ongoing problems in your life (relationship friction, work difficulties, financial stress) and, for each one, honestly answer the question: "Could this be directly caused by inattention, impulsivity, or poor self-regulation — or does it have a root cause that medication cannot touch?" Bring this list to a therapist, coach, or trusted person who knows you well and ask for their perspective on your attributions.

3. If you are currently taking or considering ADHD medication, set a recurring weekly calendar reminder for a specific day and time — for example, Sunday evening at 8 pm — to spend five minutes completing that week's symptom tracking entries and writing one sentence about any side effects or functional changes you noticed. Keep these weekly notes in a single document or notebook so that at your next follow-up appointment (typically every 3–6 months) you can hand the doctor a coherent record rather than trying to reconstruct three months of experience from memory.
