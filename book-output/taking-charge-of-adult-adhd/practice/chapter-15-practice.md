# Practice Exercises: Chapter 15 — 5. What Will the Evaluation Tell You?

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** The DSM-5 requires at least five symptoms from a symptom list for an ADHD diagnosis, but Barkley argues this threshold is flawed for adults. What are the two reasons he gives, and what does the research actually show about the minimum symptom count?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Barkley identifies two problems with the five-symptom DSM threshold. First, research — including his own — shows that adults need only four symptoms from either list to be experiencing impairment at a clinically significant level; requiring five excludes real sufferers. Second, the DSM criteria were originally designed to diagnose children, not adults, making their direct application to the adult population inherently mismatched. To address this, Barkley and colleagues developed a nine-item list grounded entirely in adult research, which has proven effective at accurately diagnosing hundreds of adults in the twelve years since its creation.

</details>

---

**Q2:** Barkley defines impairment not simply as "having problems" but in a precise, comparative way. What is that precise definition, and why does it matter for determining whether someone actually has ADHD?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Impairment is defined as functioning significantly below the norm — the average or typical person in the population — in any life domain. It explicitly does not mean falling short of an elite or highly educated peer group, nor does it mean failing to meet one's own internal standards. This definition matters because it is the only scientifically defensible anchor for the word "disorder." If someone functions as well as or better than the typical person, they are, by definition, not disordered, regardless of how frustrated they feel with their performance. Without this anchor, the concept of ADHD becomes meaningless and unfair to those who genuinely cannot function at the level of the typical adult.

</details>

---

**Q3:** Critics claim that ADHD symptoms are universal — that everyone experiences them at some level. What specific empirical evidence does Barkley present to refute this claim, and what numbers make the contrast most striking?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Barkley cites a 2008 study comparing 146 adults diagnosed with ADHD to 109 community adults. The contrast is stark: fewer than 10% of typical adults endorsed all but two of the 18 DSM symptoms as occurring "often," and no more than 12% endorsed those remaining two. Meanwhile, the average community adult reported experiencing fewer than one of the 18 symptoms often, while the average adult with ADHD reported more than twelve (seven inattention symptoms and five hyperactive-impulsive symptoms). Impairment data were equally dramatic: in educational activities, 89% of adults with ADHD reported being often impaired versus 1% of community adults; in daily responsibilities, 86% versus 2%.

</details>

---

**Q4:** What is "sluggish cognitive tempo" (SCT), and why does its existence complicate the diagnosis of the predominantly inattentive presentation of ADHD?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Sluggish cognitive tempo is a condition characterized by daydreaming, spaciness, frequent staring, slow movement, lethargy, mental fogginess, slow information processing, social reticence, and passivity — traits that superficially resemble ADHD inattention but represent a deficiency in a different type of attention altogether. Its existence complicates the inattentive ADHD presentation because 30–50% of people who appear to have that presentation may actually have SCT rather than ADHD. This means a clinician evaluating someone with inattentive symptoms cannot simply default to an ADHD diagnosis; the symptom profile must be examined carefully for the sluggish, hypoactive qualities that distinguish SCT from true ADHD, which matters because the interventions may differ.

</details>

---

**Q5:** Barkley describes the three ADHD presentations and their relationship to one another over time. Why does he caution against treating these presentations as stable, qualitatively distinct types?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The three presentations — predominantly hyperactive, predominantly inattentive, and combined — overlap considerably and appear to represent differences in severity and developmental timing rather than truly separate disorders. The predominantly hyperactive presentation, most common in preschoolers, is now believed to be an early developmental stage of the combined presentation in most cases; up to 90% of these children develop sufficient attention problems to qualify for the combined presentation within three to five years. The combined presentation is the most common (roughly 65% or more of clinical cases) and most severe. Because people shift across presentations over time, a label given at one point in life may not hold years later, which means clinicians and patients should not over-invest in the specific subtype as if it defines a permanent, qualitatively different condition.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Frustrated High Achiever**
Marcus is a 34-year-old software engineer who has always considered himself intellectually gifted. His team consistently outperforms other teams, and his manager rates him as "meets expectations." Yet Marcus feels chronically disorganized, frequently loses track of tasks, and becomes easily bored with routine code reviews. He read about ADHD online and is convinced he has it. A psychologist evaluates him and concludes he does not meet criteria for ADHD.

*What should you consider?*
- Does Marcus's functioning fall below the norm, or does it only fall below his own self-image as a high performer?
- Could the symptoms he notices be explained by other factors, such as the mismatch between his role and his interests?
- What is the risk of seeking repeated second opinions when the core disagreement is between his self-assessment and an objective norm-based standard?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The central issue is Barkley's definition of impairment: functioning significantly below the average person in the population, not below one's own aspirations. Marcus's manager rates him as meeting expectations, and his team is outperforming peers — by every objective norm-based measure, he is performing at or above the typical level. The symptoms he notices (boredom, disorganization relative to his own standards) are real experiences, but they do not constitute clinical impairment in Barkley's framework. Pursuing repeated evaluations — as with the Joe case in the chapter — is unlikely to yield a different result because the evaluators are applying the same norm-based standard. The more productive path is to examine whether his current role matches his strengths, and whether non-ADHD factors (e.g., interest mismatch, work environment) are driving the dissatisfaction. Accepting a negative diagnosis is not the same as dismissing his struggles; it redirects him to the right interventions.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Newly Diagnosed Adult Who Wants to Reject the Label**
Priya is 28 and has just been diagnosed with combined presentation ADHD after a thorough evaluation that included rating scales, clinical interview, developmental history, and collateral information from her sister. She is upset by the diagnosis because she associates ADHD with children who can't sit still, and she holds a demanding job as a paralegal. She is considering not following up on the recommendations.

*What should you consider?*
- What criteria did the evaluator need to satisfy to render this diagnosis, and does meeting all five of them indicate a sound conclusion?
- What is the statistical reality about ADHD prevalence, and how does it bear on stigma?
- What does Barkley say is the practical consequence of not "owning" a diagnosis?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The evaluator had to establish five things: high levels of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, at a frequency far above age peers, present for at least six months, with childhood onset before age 12–16, and causing adverse consequences across multiple life domains. Meeting all five is a substantial evidentiary bar, not a casual label. The "ADHD is just for fidgety children" image is outdated; the chapter's data show that 4–5% of adults — roughly 13.6 million Americans as of 2020 — are diagnosed, it occurs across all countries and ethnic groups, and it is even more prevalent in certain professional domains like law, sales, and the performing arts. Most importantly, Barkley is explicit: failing to own the diagnosis dramatically reduces the likelihood that a person will commit to treatment or follow through on professional advice. Priya's job as a paralegal — demanding attention to detail, deadlines, and organization — is precisely the kind of environment where untreated ADHD creates the most friction. Accepting the diagnosis is the prerequisite to getting effective help.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Inattentive Profile That Might Not Be ADHD**
Devon is 22 and has just been told by a counselor that he likely has the predominantly inattentive presentation of ADHD. Devon describes himself as constantly daydreaming, mentally foggy, slow to process what teachers say in lectures, socially withdrawn, and prone to staring off into space. He is not impulsive, not hyperactive, and rarely acts without thinking. He is relieved to have a potential explanation.

*What should you consider?*
- Does Devon's symptom profile fit classic inattentive ADHD, or does it more closely resemble the SCT profile described by Barkley?
- What distinguishes SCT from ADHD in terms of behavioral style and associated characteristics?
- What should Devon do before accepting the inattentive ADHD diagnosis and beginning treatment?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Devon's profile — daydreaming, fogginess, slow processing, staring, social withdrawal, passivity rather than impulsivity — maps almost exactly onto the sluggish cognitive tempo (SCT) profile Barkley describes, rather than onto ADHD inattentive presentation. True ADHD inattention tends to involve active distraction (mind races off on tangents, chases irrelevant stimuli) rather than the hypoactive, foggy quality Devon describes. SCT also differs in its comorbidity pattern: less antisocial behavior, less oppositional defiance, more depression and anxiety, and learning difficulties stemming from errors rather than disruptive behavior — all consistent with Devon's presentation. Barkley explicitly notes that 30–50% of those initially categorized as predominantly inattentive ADHD may actually have SCT. Devon should ask his evaluator specifically whether SCT was considered, request a more detailed differential, and be cautious about starting stimulant medication (which is calibrated to ADHD) before the distinction is clarified, since so little is scientifically known about SCT treatment.

</details>

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the concept of impairment as Barkley defines it — and why it is the most important element in an ADHD evaluation — in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

Having ADHD symptoms is not enough to be diagnosed with ADHD — those symptoms must actually be causing you to function noticeably worse than the average person in real areas of your life, like work, relationships, or managing money. This standard, called impairment, is measured against a norm — what the typical adult in the population can do — not against what you wish you could do or what your most accomplished peers can do. The reason this matters so much is that "disorder" only means something if there is a meaningful, measurable gap between how you function and how most people function; without that gap, what you are experiencing may be frustration or dissatisfaction, but it is not a disorder.

</details>
