# Practice Exercises: Chapter 50 — Discover Related Guilford Books

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

This chapter contains no substantive educational content — it is a publisher advertisement directing readers to the Guilford Press catalog and email alert signup. There are no concepts, arguments, or frameworks to test.

**Q1:** What is the purpose of the "Discover Related Guilford Books" section at the end of this book?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

This section is a standard publisher back-matter advertisement. It directs readers to the Guilford Press catalog so they can find additional titles in areas of interest related to the book they just finished. It also promotes an email alert service (e-Alerts) that notifies subscribers of new releases and special offers. It contains no educational content.

</details>

**Q2:** Why do publishers include catalog sections like this at the end of books?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Publishers include back-matter catalog pages to convert engaged readers — people who have finished a book they found valuable — into repeat customers. A reader who completed "Taking Charge of Adult ADHD" is likely interested in related titles on psychology, self-help, or neurodevelopmental conditions, making them a high-value target for additional Guilford Press books on those topics.

</details>

**Q3:** How does a publisher email alert service function as a marketing tool for readers with ADHD?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

For readers with ADHD, an opt-in email alert service can serve a genuinely useful function beyond marketing: it delivers curated, interest-filtered book recommendations directly to the inbox without requiring the reader to proactively search. Given that ADHD often impairs prospective memory and self-directed information seeking, a push notification system can help readers discover relevant resources they might otherwise miss.

</details>

**Q4:** In the context of finishing "Taking Charge of Adult ADHD," what types of related books might Guilford Press realistically offer?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Guilford Press specializes in mental health, psychology, and education. Related titles would likely include books on executive function deficits, cognitive-behavioral therapy for ADHD, parenting children with ADHD, ADHD in the workplace, co-occurring conditions such as anxiety or depression, and evidence-based behavior management. These would align with the clinical and self-management focus of Barkley's work.

</details>

**Q5:** What does the presence of this back-matter section tell you about how to continue your learning after finishing the main text of the book?
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It signals that the book's educational content is complete and that deliberate next steps are now the reader's responsibility. Continuing to learn requires transitioning from passive reading to active resource-seeking — whether through related books, professional consultations, support groups, or structured skill practice. The publisher advertisement is a nudge to keep going, but the reader must supply the direction and discipline.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: Identifying Your Next Resource**
You have just finished "Taking Charge of Adult ADHD" and feel motivated to keep building skills. You know you struggle most with emotional regulation and time management, but you are unsure which book to read next or whether a book is even the right format.

*What should you consider?*
- Reflect on which chapters from Barkley's book addressed your most pressing challenges and search for titles that go deeper on those specific topics.
- Consider whether a workbook format, therapist-guided program, or support group might serve you better than another self-help text before adding to your reading list.
- Check whether any recommended resource is evidence-based and authored by clinicians with ADHD expertise, using the same critical lens Barkley modeled throughout the book.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The right next step is not automatically another book. After completing "Taking Charge of Adult ADHD," you should audit which of Barkley's strategies you have actually implemented versus merely read. If you implemented fewer than half, a second book is likely premature — you would benefit more from returning to the chapters on your weakest areas and practicing those skills for four to six more weeks. If you have genuinely applied the material and want to go deeper on a specific domain (e.g., emotional dysregulation), then a targeted search of Guilford's catalog or a query to a mental health professional for a specific recommendation is appropriate. Subscribing to Guilford's e-Alerts costs nothing and can surface relevant new titles passively, reducing the planning burden.

</details>

**Scenario 2: Recommending Resources to a Colleague**
A coworker has just been diagnosed with ADHD at age 38 and asks you for book recommendations. You want to point them to something trustworthy without overwhelming them.

*What should you consider?*
- Start with the book you just finished as a primary recommendation, since you can speak to its content and relevance from personal experience.
- Avoid handing someone a long reading list — one or two high-quality, evidence-based titles is more actionable than a dozen options, especially for someone newly diagnosed.
- Ask about their specific concerns (workplace performance, relationships, medication questions) so your recommendation is targeted rather than generic.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Recommend Barkley's "Taking Charge of Adult ADHD" as the starting point, explaining that it is written by a leading clinical researcher and balances scientific grounding with practical strategies. If your colleague expresses specific concerns — for example, how to manage ADHD in professional settings — you can mention that Guilford and similar publishers offer titles focused on occupational functioning or CBT-based ADHD treatment. Keep the recommendation to one book and offer to discuss it with them after they have read the first few chapters, which reduces the isolation of a new diagnosis and increases follow-through.

</details>

**Scenario 3: Using Publisher Resources Strategically**
You sign up for Guilford's e-Alerts and begin receiving emails about new books. After two months you notice you have opened twelve emails but purchased nothing and read nothing new. You are wondering whether the subscription is helping or just adding noise.

*What should you consider?*
- Distinguish between information gathering (reading about books) and actual learning (reading and applying books) — the former is not a substitute for the latter.
- Apply the same prioritization principles Barkley describes for managing ADHD: limit inputs, choose based on current need, and act immediately rather than deferring decisions.
- Set a personal rule: you will only subscribe to one new book per quarter, chosen intentionally, rather than reactively accumulating titles.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Browsing publisher newsletters can become a form of productive-feeling avoidance — the ADHD brain is rewarded by novelty and the idea of learning without the sustained effort actual reading requires. If the e-Alert subscription is generating interest but no action, treat it as a symptom of the same executive function challenges Barkley described throughout the book. The fix is a simple implementation intention: "When I receive a Guilford email, I will spend three minutes deciding yes or no on each title and immediately delete or calendar it." Unsubscribe if the emails consistently produce no action within 30 days, and return to applying the strategies already in your possession.

</details>

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain the importance of deliberately choosing your next learning resource after finishing a self-help or clinical book — rather than immediately starting another one — to someone who has never thought about this before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

When you finish a book full of strategies, your brain feels like it has done the work — but reading and applying are completely different activities, and the real value only comes from applying. Before picking up another book, you should spend several weeks actually using the strategies you just learned, noticing which ones work and which ones need adjustment. Only after that trial period does adding new information make sense, because now you know exactly what gap you are trying to fill rather than just accumulating ideas you will never use.

</details>
