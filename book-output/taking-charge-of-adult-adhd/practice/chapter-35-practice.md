# Practice Exercises: Chapter 22 — Rule 7: Make Problems External, Physical, and Manual

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why does ADHD specifically impair the ability to solve problems mentally, and why is this not a matter of intelligence?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

ADHD weakens verbal and nonverbal working memory — the brain systems responsible for holding information in mind, mentally manipulating it, and recombining it to generate options and plans. This impairment is neurological, not intellectual: a person with ADHD can be highly intelligent yet still be unable to hold multiple facts, possibilities, and constraints in mind simultaneously while weighing them against each other. The brain simply cannot sustain the load of mental juggling that problem solving demands without external support. Making the process tangible offloads that burden from a strained working memory onto the environment, where the senses can assist thinking instead.

</details>

---

**Q2:** What is the developmental sequence of working memory that ADHD disrupts, and how does that disruption show up differently at different life stages?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Typically developing children build verbal working memory (putting words to experience) and nonverbal working memory (retaining and calling up mental images) throughout childhood. They then learn to mentally disassemble those representations, manipulate them, and recombine them — a capacity required for planning, problem solving, and creative thinking. ADHD interrupts this developmental trajectory. In young children the deficit appears as inability to do mental arithmetic or repeat digit sequences backward. In older children and adolescents it surfaces as difficulty holding together all the elements of a story when writing an analysis. In adults it persists as the inability to hold all the constraints and options of a real-world problem — financial, relational, logistical — in mind at once while under the additional pressure of fatigue or competing demands.

</details>

---

**Q3:** How does Rule 7 extend the principle established in Rule 2, and what is the meaningful difference between the two rules?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Rule 2 addresses memory: it tells you to externalize things you need to remember — write them down, post a sign, create a cue — so you do not lose them. Rule 7 addresses active problem solving: it tells you to externalize everything you need to consider while working through a problem, not just a single reminder. Rule 2 deals with retrieval; Rule 7 deals with reasoning under load. The shared insight is that written, visual, or physical representations substitute for internal mental representations that ADHD makes unreliable. Rule 7 is the "advanced version" because it requires externalizing an entire dynamic process — generating options, weighing tradeoffs, rearranging possibilities — rather than a static piece of information.

</details>

---

**Q4:** The chapter describes professionals in many fields — architects, decorators, carpenters, landscapers — who routinely externalize problem solving. What does this observation imply about the relationship between ADHD strategies and normal human cognition?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The observation implies that externalizing problem solving is not a crutch or a workaround unique to people with disabilities — it is a standard technique that skilled professionals in demanding fields use precisely because even fully functional working memory has limits. Blueprints, physical mockups, computer simulations, and miniature props exist because no human mind, ADHD or not, can reliably hold and manipulate the full complexity of a kitchen layout or a bridge design internally. What Rule 7 does for adults with ADHD is normalize and deliberately deploy a strategy that everyone uses implicitly. The difference is that for people with ADHD the strategy is not optional enhancement but necessary compensation for a structural deficit in working memory.

</details>

---

**Q5:** Why does Barkley emphasize identifying where in your life problems are hardest to solve before selecting external tools? What is the diagnostic purpose of that self-assessment?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Different domains of life demand different types of problem solving — abstract analytical thinking at work, emotionally charged negotiation at home, practical spatial reasoning in repairs and shopping. Tools that help in one domain may not translate to another: a spreadsheet helps with financial tradeoffs but does nothing for a heated conversation with a spouse. Similarly, people with ADHD differ in their perceptual strengths — some are visual learners, others auditory or kinesthetic — so the most effective external aids vary by person. By mapping where and how problems paralyze you most, you can prioritize which situations most urgently need external scaffolding and match the specific format of that scaffolding to both the domain and your personal sensory strengths.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Budget Crisis**
Marcus has ADHD and is facing a real squeeze: his car needs $1,200 in repairs, his daughter needs braces estimated at $3,000, and his annual lease renewal is coming up at a higher rate. He has tried twice to figure out what to do — once while driving, once while watching TV with his family — and both times he ended up overwhelmed and abandoned the problem. His partner is pressuring him for a decision by the weekend.

*What should you consider?*
- Why did both previous attempts fail, and what environmental and cognitive factors compounded the difficulty?
- What specific external tools could Marcus use to lay out the financial data so he can actually see the full picture at once?
- How should he structure the physical problem-solving session itself — time, place, format — to reduce the chance of another abandonment?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Both previous attempts failed because Marcus was trying to hold four or five competing financial figures plus their emotional weight in working memory while also managing sensory input from driving or the TV — all conditions that make ADHD-related working memory deficits catastrophic. The first step is to isolate the session: a quiet table, no background noise, no competing demands.

Marcus should write each financial element on a separate index card: current monthly income, monthly fixed expenses, the repair cost, an estimated monthly braces payment, the rent increase amount. He can physically arrange the cards into categories — fixed obligations, one-time costs, discretionary — and move them around to explore scenarios. What if he delays the car repair one month and makes a partial dental payment? What if he asks for a payment plan on braces? Each scenario gets physically rearranged rather than held in his head.

He could also draw a simple timeline on paper: the next six months, with each financial event placed on it. Seeing the sequence visually often reveals whether problems cluster dangerously in one month or whether there is actually more breathing room than it felt like internally. The goal is to drain the problem out of his head and onto the table so that his anxiety about the problem stops blocking his ability to analyze it.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Difficult Conversation**
Priya has ADHD and needs to negotiate a raise with her manager. She knows she deserves one — she has taken on more responsibilities and outperformed her targets — but every time she has tried to prepare mentally, she loses the thread, forgets key points she wanted to make, or gets anxious and abandons the preparation. The meeting is in three days.

*What should you consider?*
- How does the chapter's framework apply to a verbal/interpersonal problem, not just a concrete physical or financial one?
- What external tools are appropriate for preparing for a conversation rather than for building something or managing money?
- How can Priya use physical preparation to manage the emotional component of the problem, not just the informational one?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The chapter explicitly notes that Rule 7 applies to verbal problems too: use paper, index cards, or a laptop to externalize the data. Priya can write each of her accomplishments — specific projects, measurable outcomes, new responsibilities — on separate cards. She can then arrange them into a rough argument structure: strongest evidence first, supporting points next, anticipated objections last. Seeing the structure physically prevents her from forgetting a key point mid-conversation because the card is still there even if her attention drifts.

She can also write down her manager's likely objections on separate cards and draft her responses. This converts the abstract anxiety of "what if they say no?" into a concrete, solvable sub-problem she can work through in advance. The physical act of writing and rearranging forces her to commit to a specific argument rather than floating in a cloud of half-formed possibilities.

On the emotional side, the process of physically building her case — handling the cards, reading her own accomplishments written out — activates a different channel of self-awareness than anxious internal rumination. Many people find that seeing their own evidence laid out tangibly reduces the emotional charge of the problem because it transforms "I hope I deserve a raise" into "here is the evidence that I do." She might also practice saying her opening statement aloud while looking at the card layout, engaging her auditory and kinesthetic channels simultaneously.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Home Renovation Decision**
Derek has ADHD and is planning to remodel his kitchen. He has quotes from three contractors, five or six design ideas from online, a rough budget, and opinions from his partner and his mother — none of which agree. He has been "thinking about it" for two months and no decision has been made. His partner is frustrated. Derek says he just needs to "figure it out in his head."

*What should you consider?*
- What is wrong with Derek's stated approach of figuring it out in his head, given what the chapter explains about ADHD and working memory?
- Which specific externalization technique from the chapter is most directly applicable here, and why?
- How can Derek reframe the social embarrassment of using "childish" tools to solve a grown-up problem?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Derek's approach is precisely the failure mode the chapter predicts: asking a working memory compromised by ADHD to hold contractor quotes, design preferences, budget constraints, relational preferences, and competing opinions simultaneously and synthesize them into a decision. Two months of stalled thinking is the predictable result. The complexity has not reduced; it has just been cycling unproductively in his head, generating frustration rather than resolution.

The chapter's most directly applicable technique is the mockup strategy, illustrated by the homeowner who built a kitchen model from moving boxes. Derek does not need to go that far, but he could print or sketch his top two or three floor plans at the same scale, cut them out, and lay them on a table. He can write each contractor's key terms on a card. He can create a simple grid — contractor versus criteria — that he and his partner fill in together. Physical manipulation of real materials converts a diffuse internal struggle into a joint, solvable problem-solving session.

On the social embarrassment: the chapter makes the point directly that architects, engineers, decorators, and landscapers all use physical models and simulations as professional practice precisely because the problems are too complex for pure mental manipulation. Derek is not playing with toys — he is using the same class of strategy that professionals use when the stakes are high. The chapter gives adults with ADHD explicit permission to use whatever tools help, with no apology required.

</details>

## ✍️ Reflection Prompts

1. Think of a time when you spent a long time "thinking through" a problem — a financial decision, a difficult conversation, a planning challenge — and kept getting stuck, overwhelmed, or losing the thread. Looking back, which parts of the problem were you trying to hold in your head simultaneously? What would have changed if you had written each element on a separate piece of paper and spread them in front of you?

2. Think of a time when someone dismissed a tool you were using — a checklist, a physical layout, a drawing, an audiotape — as unnecessary, childish, or excessive for someone your age. How did that dismissal affect your behavior and your outcome? Now that you understand the neurological basis for why external tools compensate for working memory deficits in ADHD, how would you respond to that dismissal differently?

3. Think about the domain of your life where problem solving feels most impossible — work negotiations, family conflict, financial planning, or something else. What specific type of working memory demand (holding facts, generating options, weighing tradeoffs, managing emotions alongside logic) makes that domain hardest? What single external tool — a card system, a whiteboard, an audio recording, a physical mockup — would most directly target that specific demand?

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain why adults with ADHD need to make problems physical and external in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

ADHD weakens working memory — the brain's ability to hold several pieces of information in mind at once and mentally move them around — which makes solving problems purely in your head exhausting and unreliable, the way trying to do long division in your head is harder than doing it on paper. Writing information down, arranging it on cards, drawing it out, or even building a physical model takes that mental load off the brain and puts it somewhere you can see, touch, and rearrange with your hands instead. This is not a workaround or a crutch — it is the same strategy that architects use blueprints for and that decorators use scale models for, because the problems are genuinely too complex for any human mind to solve well without help from the physical world.

</details>

## 🧩 Synthesis Challenge

**Exercise:** Rule 7 teaches you to externalize the components of a problem so you can manipulate them physically rather than mentally. Rule 2 (from an earlier chapter) teaches you to externalize things you need to remember by creating physical cues and prompts in your environment. Design a combined system for preparing for and following through on a high-stakes recurring situation — such as a monthly budget review, a weekly team meeting, or a regular difficult conversation with a family member. Your system must use Rule 2's cue-and-prompt strategy to ensure you actually begin the preparation, and Rule 7's externalization strategy to carry out the problem-solving itself. Describe the specific tools, their physical location, and the sequence of steps. Then explain how each element targets either memory (Rule 2) or active reasoning under load (Rule 7).

**Chapters involved:** Chapter 22 (Rule 7: Make Problems External, Physical, and Manual) + Chapter 13 (Rule 2: Make It Visible and External — the chapter covering externalized cues and reminders)

## 📋 Action Items

1. On Monday morning, before checking email or messages, identify one recurring problem or decision you have been avoiding or cycling through mentally without resolution. Write every relevant fact, constraint, and option on separate sticky notes or index cards. Spend fifteen minutes physically arranging and rearranging the cards on a flat surface. Do not try to solve the problem in your head — only move the physical pieces.

2. By Wednesday, complete the self-assessment the chapter recommends: write down one specific problem domain from each of the four areas (work, home, social life, practical tasks) where you most often get stuck or overwhelmed. For each one, note whether you are a visual, auditory, tactile, or kinesthetic learner and write down one concrete external tool — a drawing, a spreadsheet, a voice memo, a physical prop — that matches both the domain and your learning style.

3. Before the end of the week, the next time you need to prepare for a difficult conversation — with a boss, a partner, a family member, or a service provider — write each point you want to make on a separate index card. Arrange the cards in the order you want to raise them, write your anticipated objection on the back of each card, and practice saying your opening sentence aloud while looking at the layout. Notice whether the physical preparation reduces your anxiety compared to trying to rehearse entirely in your head.
