# Practice Exercises: Chapter 10 — Step One: To Get Started, Get Evaluated

## Comprehension Check

Write 5 conceptual questions that test deep understanding — not trivia or recall. Each question should require the reader to explain, connect, or apply a concept.

**Q1:** Barkley opens Step One not with clinical definitions or instructions but with five first-person voices from adults with ADHD. What is the psychological and rhetorical purpose of this choice, and why would beginning immediately with diagnostic criteria have been less effective?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Barkley is exploiting a well-established principle: recognition precedes motivation. Before a person will take action — in this case, seeking a professional evaluation — they first need to feel certain that the action is warranted. If the chapter opened with clinical criteria (e.g., "you must have six or more symptoms of inattention"), a reader who has spent their life doubting themselves might read the list and dismiss it as not quite fitting them. By leading with first-person accounts instead, Barkley allows readers to experience identification — the jolt of "that is exactly what happens to me" — before any clinical framing has been applied. This emotional priming removes skepticism and self-doubt at the moment they are most likely to derail action. The five voices do the diagnostic work informally, through self-recognition, creating the motivation that makes the formal chapters that follow (11, 12, 13) actionable rather than abstract.

</details>

---

**Q2:** The speaker in the lawn-mowing story ends up at a bar with police waiting at his car — and explains it by saying "I just go with the flow of what's happening around me." Barkley frames this not as irresponsibility but as a neurological phenomenon. What is that phenomenon, and how does it differ from ordinary forgetfulness or carelessness?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The phenomenon is stimulus-driven behavior — a hallmark of ADHD executive dysfunction in which real-time environmental stimuli become more compelling than internally held intentions. An adult without ADHD maintains goal-directed behavior by keeping an intended action active in working memory and resisting competing stimuli; when a friend appears at the gas station, they acknowledge the friend, perhaps make plans for another day, and continue with the original task. The man in the story cannot do this: his executive system fails to hold the original goal (mow the lawn) in place when a more salient and immediately rewarding alternative appears. Each subsequent stimulus — fishing, then the bar — pulls him further from the original intention. This is distinct from ordinary forgetfulness, where the goal is temporarily dropped but re-engaged later. In this case, the goal is completely displaced and never reactivated. It is also distinct from carelessness, which implies deliberate disregard. The man did not choose to leave his wife waiting; his brain could not maintain the priority hierarchy against environmental competition.

</details>

**Q3:** One speaker says: "I have to do things as soon as I think of them, because if I don't, I'll forget about them, and then they never get done." Barkley treats this as evidence of something psychologically sophisticated. What has this person actually figured out about their own brain, and why does this matter for how we should interpret impulsive behavior in adults with ADHD?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

This speaker has developed a folk theory of their own working memory deficit. They have observed — without clinical vocabulary — that their ability to hold intentions in mind and act on them later is severely impaired. Their response is a compensatory behavioral strategy: act on every impulse immediately, because deferred action will almost certainly mean no action. What looks from the outside like impulsivity — interrupting meetings, abruptly switching tasks, doing things "out of nowhere" — is from the inside a rational adaptation to a brain that cannot reliably return to stored intentions. This reframes the moral judgment that is typically attached to impulsive behavior. When we call someone impulsive, we imply a failure of self-control driven by desire or immaturity. In this case, the behavior is a coping mechanism born from accurate self-knowledge. Understanding this distinction is critical for partners, employers, and clinicians who might otherwise interpret the behavior as selfishness or lack of discipline.

</details>

**Q4:** Chapter 10 contains no clinical instruction, no diagnostic criteria, and no strategies — yet it is titled "Step One: To Get Started, Get Evaluated." What work does this chapter actually do in the context of the book's five-step program, and what would be lost if this chapter were simply omitted?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Chapter 10 functions as an emotional threshold — it is the anteroom the reader must pass through before they can meaningfully engage with Step One's substantive content. The chapter's work is psychological, not informational: it bridges the gap between intellectual understanding (the science chapters that precede it) and willingness to act (the evaluation process the next three chapters describe). If the chapter were omitted, readers would arrive at Chapter 11's diagnostic criteria having just finished the book's science section, still positioned as information-receivers rather than people with a personal stake. The five voices activate identification, reduce the self-doubt that often prevents adults with ADHD from seeking evaluation, and create emotional readiness for the "Get Evaluated" imperative. What would be lost is the motivational bridge — the moment of "this is me" that turns a reader from someone learning about ADHD into someone who believes they may have it and need to do something about it.

</details>

**Q5:** The fourth vignette describes a woman's childhood experience of ADHD — social exclusion, shame over daily medication, and feeling like an outcast — before describing the adult identity she has built around those same traits. What does this arc reveal about the relationship between ADHD impairment and personal identity, and why does this matter for the evaluation process Step One recommends?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The arc reveals that ADHD impairment and personal identity are distinct but intertwined. The woman's ADHD traits — being outgoing, loud, intense, energizing — caused genuine harm in childhood (exclusion, stigma, shame) but also shaped an adult identity she has come to value. She is not denying that she has ADHD; she is showing that she has built meaning around the same features that once marginalized her. This matters for evaluation in two ways. First, it illustrates a common barrier to adult diagnosis: if a person has reframed their ADHD traits as core personality features, they may resist evaluation as a threat to their identity rather than welcoming it as an explanation. Second, it highlights that adult evaluation is not purely about present symptoms — it also involves reconstructing a developmental history, including the social and emotional wounds of childhood. A thorough evaluation must ask not just "what symptoms do you have now?" but "what was your experience growing up?" The childhood social pain embedded in this vignette is also clinical evidence: ADHD's diagnostic requirement that symptoms originate in childhood is precisely what this woman's memory supports.

</details>

## Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Reluctant Partner**
Your spouse has been struggling for years — losing track of time, failing to follow through on household responsibilities, forgetting conversations you had the day before, and making impulsive decisions that have affected your finances. They have always explained this as "just how I am" and resisted any suggestion that there might be a clinical explanation. You have just read Chapter 10 and recognize their experience in the five vignettes.

*What should you consider?*
- The vignettes illustrate that adults with ADHD often develop folk explanations ("that's just how I am") because they have never had the clinical vocabulary to name what they experience — meaning your spouse's resistance may be rooted in genuine self-uncertainty rather than defensiveness.
- Sharing the five voices from this chapter, rather than diagnostic criteria or clinical arguments, is more likely to create recognition than persuasion — recognition is what precedes willingness to seek evaluation.
- The lawn-mowing story in particular is useful: it frames the behavior as neurological rather than moral, which may reduce the shame your spouse associates with acknowledging the problem.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Rather than presenting the case for ADHD diagnostically — which can feel like an accusation — share Chapter 10's vignettes as stories and ask your spouse which ones, if any, sound familiar. The goal is not to convince them they have ADHD but to create the moment of identification Barkley is engineering. If your spouse recognizes themselves in even one story, they have moved from "there is nothing wrong with me" to "other people experience what I experience, and they got help." From there, the question becomes not "do you have ADHD?" but "would it be worth finding out?" Frame the evaluation as information-gathering, not labeling. You might say: "I am not saying you have ADHD — I am saying some of what I read sounds like our life, and I would like us to get more information." Avoid using the lawn-mowing story as an accusation ("that is what you did last month"); instead, reference it as evidence that the pattern has a name and is treatable. Remember that many adults with ADHD carry years of shame and self-blame — your tone needs to communicate that evaluation is an act of self-compassion, not diagnosis as punishment.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Self-Doubting Reader**
You are reading this book because someone suggested you might have ADHD. As you read the five vignettes in Chapter 10, you recognize parts of your experience in all five — but you keep second-guessing yourself: "Everyone loses track of time sometimes. Everyone gets distracted. Maybe I am just looking for an excuse." You are on the verge of closing the book and deciding you are fine.

*What should you consider?*
- The pattern Barkley is describing is not about having these experiences occasionally; it is about having them consistently, across multiple life domains, at a severity that causes meaningful impairment — which is exactly why he recommends professional evaluation rather than self-diagnosis from a book.
- The inner voice saying "maybe I am just looking for an excuse" is itself a symptom of how long-undiagnosed adults with ADHD learn to dismiss their own experience, having been told for years to try harder.
- Barkley's entire argument in Step One is that you do not need to be certain — you need only to recognize enough of yourself to warrant finding out. The evaluation is not a verdict; it is information.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The self-doubt you are experiencing is normal and extremely common in adults who reach potential ADHD diagnosis after decades of being told (or telling themselves) that their struggles are character flaws. The key insight from Chapter 10 is that the book is not asking you to self-diagnose — it is asking you to recognize enough of yourself in these voices to take the step of getting evaluated. You do not need to be 100% certain you have ADHD to make an appointment. You need only enough recognition to think "this might be worth exploring." The professional evaluator will determine whether the symptoms are present, whether they have been present since childhood, whether they reach clinical threshold, and whether they are better explained by another condition. Your job right now is not to decide — your job is to take the first step. If after evaluation you learn you do not have ADHD, you will have lost a few hours and gained clarity. If you learn you do, you will have access to treatments that Barkley describes as "leveling the playing field" for the first time in your life. The cost of not finding out is years more of the same struggle.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Workplace Manager**
One of your best employees is highly creative and enthusiastic but chronically unreliable in ways that have started affecting the team. They miss deadlines, abandon projects mid-stream, forget commitments made in meetings, and occasionally disappear into a tangential task for an entire afternoon. They are not lazy — their work, when they complete it, is excellent. But the pattern is affecting team morale. After reading Chapter 10, you wonder whether ADHD might explain what you are observing.

*What should you consider?*
- The behaviors you are observing — task abandonment, forgotten commitments, stimulus-driven distraction — map closely to the symptom domains illustrated in the chapter's vignettes, particularly the stimulus-driven behavior of the lawn-mowing story and the task-switching of the third speaker.
- As a manager, it is not your role to diagnose or suggest a diagnosis; your role is to create conditions where the employee can succeed and, if relevant, to point toward appropriate resources.
- The chapter's key insight — that impulsive task-switching and forgotten commitments are often compensatory behaviors or neurological failures, not moral failures — should inform how you discuss the pattern with the employee and what accommodations you consider.

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Approach the conversation from a performance and support framework rather than a diagnostic one. Focus on the specific, observable patterns — missed deadlines, unfinished projects, forgotten commitments — and frame the conversation around finding solutions together, not assigning blame. You might say: "I have noticed this pattern, and I want to understand what is getting in the way and what we can do differently." Do not suggest ADHD; instead, ask open questions about what the employee finds most difficult and what conditions help them do their best work. If the employee themselves raises the possibility of ADHD or a similar condition, you can mention that the company's employee assistance program or HR benefits may include resources for evaluation. In terms of immediate adjustments, consider strategies that externalize structure: written summaries of meeting commitments, broken-down deliverables with shorter interim deadlines, and brief end-of-day check-ins. These accommodate the pattern regardless of whether ADHD is ever formally identified. The chapter's insight that the person is not choosing to be unreliable — they are failing to hold goals against competing stimuli — should shift your interpretation from "this person does not care" to "this person needs a different system."

</details>

## Reflection Prompts

1. Think of a time when you abandoned a task — not because you lost interest, but because something else appeared and pulled your attention away before you could stop it. Looking back through the lens of the lawn-mowing story, was your behavior driven by choice, or by something happening in your attention and working memory that you did not have language for at the time? What would you do differently now that you understand how ADHD's stimulus-driven behavior actually works?

2. Think of a time when someone close to you — a partner, parent, boss, or friend — interpreted something you did as careless, selfish, or irresponsible, when from the inside it felt more like you simply could not help it. Now that you understand the difference between behavioral choice and executive-system failure, how would you explain what actually happened? What would that conversation look like if you could have it again?

3. Think of a personal trait — something about your personality, your way of working, or your social style — that you have always explained as "just how I am." After reading the five vignettes in Chapter 10, particularly the woman who reframed her childhood ADHD traits into an adult identity she values, is there a version of your "that's just how I am" that might be covering for an unexamined struggle? What would it mean to get curious about it rather than simply accepting it?

## Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain what the lawn-mowing story reveals about how ADHD executive dysfunction actually works — in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

When a person without ADHD sets out to do a task, their brain holds that task as a priority and filters out competing distractions — even appealing ones — until the task is done or they consciously decide to stop. In adults with ADHD, this filtering mechanism is impaired: when a more immediately stimulating option appears, the brain treats it as more important than the original intention, and the original goal simply fades away without the person consciously choosing to abandon it. The man who left to buy gas and ended up fishing and drinking was not being reckless or irresponsible — his brain could not hold "mow the lawn" in place against the real-time pull of his environment, which is the core of what executive dysfunction in ADHD actually means.

</details>

## Synthesis Challenge

**Exercise:** Chapter 10 uses five first-person voices to create identification before clinical instruction begins. Chapter 9 (the introduction to Part II) establishes six symptom domains of adult ADHD — time management, attention, organization, planning and problem solving, emotional control, and concentration. Your task: take each of the five vignettes in Chapter 10 and map it precisely to one or more of Chapter 9's six symptom domains, then explain in a paragraph why a reader who has already absorbed Chapter 9's framework will read Chapter 10's vignettes differently than a reader coming in cold. What does the prior conceptual scaffolding add to the moment of recognition?

**Chapters involved:** Chapter 10 + Chapter 9

A reader coming in cold to Chapter 10 will experience the vignettes as relatable stories — they may recognize their own life in the narratives, but they will not know what to call what they are seeing or why it matters clinically. A reader who has absorbed Chapter 9's six-domain framework has a conceptual map already in place. When they read "time escapes me," they immediately file it under time management and recall that this is one of ADHD's core impairment domains — not a personality quirk but a named, researched deficit. When they read the third vignette about flitting between tasks, they recognize the planning and problem solving domain and understand that acting immediately on impulses is the person's compensatory response to that deficit. When they read the lawn-mowing story, they see stimulus-driven behavior as a failure of attention regulation and impulse control — domains they already understand. The prior scaffolding transforms vague recognition ("this sounds like me") into structured recognition ("this is specifically the time management and attention domains from the framework I just learned, and I can see exactly how they manifest in daily life"). The Chapter 9 framework does not reduce the emotional impact of the stories — it amplifies it, because the reader now knows that what sounds like a personal failing is actually a documented, neurologically grounded pattern shared by millions of adults.
