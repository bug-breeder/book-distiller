# Practice Exercises: Chapter 26 — Money

## 🧪 Comprehension Check

**Q1:** Why does weak nonverbal working memory specifically make impulse spending worse for adults with ADHD — and what does the "mind's eye" have to do with it?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Nonverbal working memory is the brain's ability to hold mental images of the past and project them into the future — the "mind's eye." When it is weak, a person cannot vividly recall what happened the last time they made an unaffordable impulse purchase, nor can they picture the future consequence (an overdrawn account, a maxed-out card). Without those mental movies playing on demand, the abstract knowledge that spending is harmful cannot compete with the immediate pull of the item in front of them. The result is that good intentions and financial knowledge are present but functionally inaccessible at the moment they are most needed.

</details>

---

**Q2:** The chapter describes a cascade effect in financial problems. Explain how one financial failure can systematically generate several others, using the examples Barkley provides.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Financial problems compound because each failure creates conditions that make the next one more likely. Exceeding a credit limit or accumulating student debt while making late payments drives down a credit score, which then triggers loan and mortgage denials. Being denied a mortgage forces continued renting; being denied a car loan traps someone in an unreliable vehicle with mounting repair costs. Both outcomes drain cash that could have become savings. With no savings buffer, a single unexpected event — a car accident, a medical bill — produces a crisis with no safety net, often leading to high-interest borrowing that deepens the hole further.

</details>

---

**Q3:** The chapter presents four "faculties" — the mind's eye, mind's voice, mind's heart, and mind's playground — as separate sources of financial trouble. Why does Barkley treat them as distinct rather than lumping all ADHD money problems together?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Each faculty represents a different underlying cognitive or emotional mechanism, and the failure of each produces a qualitatively different type of financial mistake. The mind's eye (nonverbal working memory) fails to conjure consequences, producing impulse purchases. The mind's voice (verbal self-regulation) fails to produce internal rules and self-talk, causing missed bill-payment deadlines even when reminders are set. The mind's heart (emotional regulation) causes spending driven by mood states — celebratory, depressive, or resentful — rather than need. The mind's playground (planning and problem-solving) fails to produce budgets and financial projections. Treating them as distinct means the interventions can be targeted: a visual cue on a credit card addresses the mind's eye; talking aloud before a purchase addresses the mind's voice; lifestyle health habits address the mind's heart.

</details>

---

**Q4:** Barkley recommends a specific account structure involving pretax retirement contributions, direct deposit, and automatic savings transfers. What is the underlying logic of this system, and why is it particularly suited to adults with ADHD?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The system is designed around a core ADHD principle: reduce the number of moments when willpower must be exercised. Every manual transfer or deliberate savings decision is a moment where impulse can override intention. By routing money automatically — 10% pretax into a retirement account before it is ever seen, then direct deposit of the remainder, then automatic transfer of another 10% to savings — the person never has access to the full paycheck. There is nothing to resist. Visibility of cash is the enemy; the system minimizes visibility. An added benefit is that pretax retirement contributions lower taxable income and, when employer matching is available, provide free additional money that is forfeited by non-participation.

</details>

---

**Q5:** The chapter advises against payday loans and implies a clear distinction between borrowing for depreciating versus appreciating (or necessary) purchases. What principle underlies this distinction, and why is it especially critical for adults with ADHD?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The principle is that debt should only be taken on when the purchase either retains/grows value or is an unavoidable necessity — not for consumption, entertainment, or items that depreciate instantly. Payday loans violate this at the extreme: astronomically high interest rates mean the cost of borrowing far exceeds any benefit, and the short repayment window creates a debt trap that compounds quickly. For adults with ADHD, the risk is amplified because impulsive borrowing decisions are made in the moment without adequate projection of repayment reality. A person who would never rationally agree to a 400% annualized interest rate will sign a payday loan in a moment of urgency. The firmness of the rule — never, ever, ever — is deliberate: bright-line rules are easier for the ADHD brain to follow than situational judgment calls.

</details>

## 🔄 Apply It

**Scenario 1: The Antique Store Problem**
Marcus has ADHD and manages his spending reasonably well during regular weeks. But his Saturday routine involves meeting friends at a café that sits inside a large antique and vintage market. He has been overspending on decorative items he does not need, and his credit card balance has climbed $800 over two months from these outings. He knows intellectually it is a problem but feels fine each time he is there.

*What should you consider?*
- Why does Marcus's "knowing it is a problem" fail to protect him in the moment, and which cognitive faculty is breaking down?
- What environmental change would have the highest impact, and why is changing the environment more reliable than building stronger willpower?
- What physical cue or externalizing strategy could serve as a circuit-breaker at the point of purchase if he cannot change the Saturday routine entirely?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Marcus's knowledge is not translating into behavior because nonverbal working memory — the mind's eye — is not supplying a vivid enough image of the growing credit card balance at the moment he sees an appealing item. Knowledge stored abstractly cannot compete with immediate sensory appeal when working memory is impaired. The most powerful intervention is environmental: stop going to the antique market. Barkley explicitly states that for adults with ADHD, walking into a shopping environment when there is nothing to buy is itself the error — the temptation is too great when impulse control is limited. If Marcus cannot abandon the café, he should go via a route that does not enter the market and agree with his friends to meet outside rather than inside it. As a fallback, he should carry no credit card (replaced with a "For Emergency Use Only" sticker card left at home), carry only enough cash for the café, and put a photo of his credit card statement — with the balance circled in red — in his wallet so that reaching for it forces him to see the consequence first.

</details>

**Scenario 2: The Partner Conflict**
Dani and her partner Sam have joint finances. Dani has ADHD and consistently misses the due date on their electricity and internet bills despite having calendar reminders set. Sam is frustrated, and the arguments are escalating. Dani feels guilty and defensive; Sam feels like Dani does not care. The bills are affordable — the issue is not money shortage, it is execution failure.

*What should you consider?*
- Which ADHD executive function deficit best explains why reminders alone are not working?
- What structural change would remove the need for Dani to execute the bill payment at all?
- How should the couple frame this arrangement — as punishment or as a practical accommodation — and why does framing matter for the relationship?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

The mind's voice is failing: Dani can set reminders but cannot follow through on the self-directed internal motivation to act on them at the scheduled moment. For ADHD, the gap between intention and action is not closed by awareness alone — it requires externalized systems. The structural solution is automatic bill payment: set up direct debit for fixed recurring bills (electricity, internet, rent) so that payment happens with zero executive function required. For variable bills, Sam can be designated as the bill manager for a defined trial period, with Dani handing over the relevant account access and receiving a cash allowance for discretionary spending. This arrangement should be framed explicitly as a practical accommodation for a neurological condition — the same logic as wearing glasses for poor eyesight — not as punishment or evidence of incompetence. The framing matters because defensiveness and shame from Dani will undermine cooperation, and resentment from Sam will undermine the relationship. A time-limited trial with a shared review date reduces the power imbalance and gives both partners a sense of agency.

</details>

**Scenario 3: The Emotional Spender**
Priya has ADHD and has identified a clear pattern: she makes large online purchases when she feels lonely or socially excluded. After a difficult weekend where friends did not invite her to an outing, she spent $340 on clothes she did not need. She wants to stop but says that in the moment, shopping "just feels like the only thing that helps."

*What should you consider?*
- Which faculty is driving Priya's spending, and what does the chapter say about managing it?
- What lifestyle factors might be increasing her emotional vulnerability and making the pattern harder to interrupt?
- What is one concrete barrier she could put between the emotional state and the purchase, and one alternative she could substitute for the emotional function shopping is serving?

<details>
<summary>Model Response</summary>

Priya's spending is driven by the mind's heart — emotional regulation failure in which negative feelings override financial judgment and spending becomes a mood-repair mechanism. The chapter explicitly names this pattern (buying a new outfit when feeling down) and advises using mental imagery and self-talk to recognize when emotions are carrying the person away. It also emphasizes that sleep deprivation, poor diet, caffeine overconsumption, lack of exercise, and alcohol use all increase emotional vulnerability to this kind of trigger. Priya should audit these lifestyle factors first, since they set the floor on her emotional resilience. The concrete barrier with the highest leverage is removing saved payment details from shopping apps and websites, so that completing a purchase requires getting up, finding a card, and manually entering details — multiple friction points that create a pause window. During that pause she can deploy self-talk: "Am I buying this because I need it, or because I feel left out right now?" The emotional function shopping is serving — soothing rejection and loneliness — needs a substitute that addresses the actual feeling: texting a friend, taking a walk, or writing in a journal. The substitute does not need to be as immediately pleasurable as shopping; it only needs to be available and practiced enough to become habitual.

</details>

## ✍️ Reflection Prompts

1. Think of a time when you made a purchase you immediately regretted — one you knew even at the moment was probably a bad idea. What was happening emotionally just before you made it, and now that you understand the mind's heart and emotional spending patterns, what would you do differently at that moment to interrupt the cycle before the purchase?

2. Think of a financial obligation — a bill, a tax filing, a savings transfer — that you have repeatedly delayed even though you knew the due date and could afford it. Now that you understand the difference between knowing what to do and the executive function required to actually do it, what single structural change (automatic payment, delegating to someone, a different reminder format) would have prevented the failure, and what has stopped you from making that change?

3. Think of a time when a money problem you had spilled over into a relationship — with a partner, a friend, a family member. Looking back with the understanding that ADHD creates systematic financial vulnerabilities rather than moral failures, how would you have handled the conversation differently, and what would you want the other person to understand about the mechanism behind the behavior?

## 🗣️ Teach It Back (Feynman Technique)

**Prompt:** Explain why adults with ADHD struggle with money specifically — not just "they're bad with money" but the actual brain-level reason — in exactly 3 sentences to someone who has never encountered this idea before.

<details>
<summary>Model Explanation</summary>

ADHD weakens the brain's ability to mentally project into the future and replay the past, so when someone with ADHD is standing in a store holding something they want, they genuinely cannot summon a vivid picture of the credit card bill they'll face next month or the financial mess they were in last time. This means their knowledge that overspending is harmful exists in the abstract but cannot compete with the concrete, immediate pull of the purchase right in front of them. Add to that difficulty regulating emotions — so stress, boredom, or excitement can trigger spending as mood management — and difficulty executing routine tasks like bill payment even when the money and the intention are both present, and you have a complete picture of why financial trouble is not a character flaw but a predictable outcome of specific executive function deficits.

</details>

## 📋 Action Items

1. On Monday morning before opening any other app or email, open your bank's mobile app and review your current checking and savings balances. Then write down — by hand on paper — every recurring monthly expense you can name from memory. Compare that list to your actual recent transactions and identify one expense that appeared in your transactions but not in your memory. Set up an automatic payment or cancellation for it before closing the app.

2. This week, remove all stored credit card details from the three online retailers or apps where you most frequently make impulse purchases. Replace the saved cards with a note in the app's saved address field that reads: "Do I need this or do I want it?" The friction of re-entering card details manually will create a pause — use that pause to wait 24 hours before completing any non-essential purchase over $30.

3. By Friday, contact your employer's HR or payroll department and ask two questions: (a) does the company offer a retirement plan with employer matching, and if so, are you currently enrolled and contributing enough to receive the full match? (b) can you set up a split direct deposit so that a fixed percentage goes automatically to a savings account each pay period? If either answer reveals money you are leaving on the table or an automatic savings system you have not yet activated, complete the enrollment or setup form before the end of the week.
